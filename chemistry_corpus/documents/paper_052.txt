ABSTRACT
The creation of glycoconjugates, particularly well-defined glycopeptides, is crucial for advancing biological research and therapeutic development. However, the direct and selective coupling of unprotected thioglycosides to native biomolecules remains a formidable challenge due to the incompatibility of conventional synthetic methods with the delicate and complex functional groups present in these molecules. Here, we report a heterogeneous metallaphotocatalytic strategy that addresses this limitation through an energy-transfer mechanism. By embedding nickel catalytic centers within a photoactive covalent organic framework, we create a system that directly excites transient thiosugar-nickel complexes, thereby bypassing the high activation barriers inherent to conventional redox pathways. This approach enables site-selective and stereocontrolled thioglycosylation under mild, operationally simple conditions, featuring a high turnover frequency (114 per hour) and exceptional catalyst recyclability. The broad scope of the method is demonstrated through the glycosylation of diverse substrates, including amino acids and bioactive peptides, without the need for protecting groups. Notably, this method enables the first heterogeneous photocatalytic glycosylation of an antimicrobial peptide mastoparan, enhancing its antitumor activity. This work provides a versatile and sustainable tool for precision bioconjugation, offering immediate potential in drug discovery and chemical biology.

INTRODUCTION
The central goal of modern synthetic chemistry is to seek catalytic paradigms that can directly and selectively synthesize challenging molecular structures. Among these, 1-thioglycosides, particularly in their unprotected forms, occupy a pivotal role in chemical biology and drug discovery ( 1 - 6 ). Their superior metabolic stability relative to O -glycosides, combined with inherent aqueous solubility and biocompatibility, renders them invaluable as glycosyl donors, bioconjugates, and key components of therapeutic agents exhibiting antitumor and antiviral activities ( 7 - 10 ). Despite their utility, the direct and selective installation of unprotected thioglycosides onto complex molecules remains a formidable challenge, although it is a transformative strategy for probing and modulating biological function ( 9 , 10 ). The principal obstacle lies in the high activation barrier required to forge the C─S bond while preserving the delicate, polyfunctional structures of both the thiosugar and the target substrate.

Existing catalytic paradigms, primarily relying on electron-transfer (ET) pathways, have fallen short in addressing this challenge ( Fig. 1, A and B ). Pioneering work by Messaoudi and others demonstrated the feasibility of Pd- and Ni-catalyzed cross-couplings with unprotected thiosugars ( 11 - 13 ). However, these systems necessitate highly reactive metal(0) intermediates to overcome the demanding oxidative addition step, inevitably requiring elevated temperatures, stoichiometric reductants, and suffering from limited functional group tolerance. Electrocatalytic Ni systems offer an attractive alternative but demand stringent anhydrous conditions to suppress competing water reduction, precluding their application to water-soluble biomolecules such as peptides ( 14 ). Metallaphotocatalysis, which merges the power of photoredox and transition metal catalysis, provides a mild and tunable approach ( 15 , 16 ). However, the direct photoinduced single-electron oxidation of thiols is energetically prohibitive, and hydrogen atom transfer strategies lack the requisite chemoselectivity between hydroxyl and thiol groups in unprotected systems ( 17 ). The fundamental limitation of these conventional strategies lies in the high activation barriers inherent to thermally driven, ET-based catalytic cycles. This suggests that overcoming such bottlenecks requires the development of an energy-transfer (EnT)-driven catalytic platform that enables low-energy reaction pathways inaccessible to traditional catalytic systems.

EnT photocatalysis offers such a mechanistic alternative ( 18 - 20 ). The Dexter EnT mechanism facilitates direct electron exchange, enabling the population of triplet-state metal complexes and a consequent significant reduction in the activation barrier for pivotal steps such as oxidative addition. Crucially, substrate coordination to a metal center [e.g., Ni(II)] narrows the singlet-triplet energy gap, rendering the complex more responsive to selective photoexcitation ( 21 - 24 ). We hypothesized that embedding molecular Ni catalysts within a heterogeneous, light-harvesting covalent organic framework (COF) could orchestrate a highly efficient EnT process. This heterogeneous system would not only enhance stability and recyclability but also enforce preassembly and proximity between the photosensitizer and the metal center, maximizing EnT efficiency for challenging transformations, which is difficult to achieve in homogeneous systems.

Here, we report a heterogeneous EnT-based metallaphotocatalytic platform for the direct, site-selective thioglycosylation of unprotected thiosugars and peptides through a low-energy catalytic pathway ( Fig. 1C ). We demonstrate that this system overcomes the intrinsic energy barriers of ET catalysis, achieving a marked rate enhancement and exceptional functional group tolerance. Its applicability is further showcased by the glycosylation of a mastoparan peptide, which enhances its biological activity. A combination of spectroscopic probes and computational studies confirms that the reaction proceeds via EnT excitation of a key nickel-thiosugar complex, which subsequently undergoes facile oxidative addition. This work establishes a general and efficient route for the precision synthesis of complex biomolecules, effectively marrying the principles of heterogeneous catalysis, photochemistry, and biomolecular engineering to advance research in synthetic biology and medicine.

RESULTS
Because of the extended π-conjugated system of the pyrene moiety, which confers prolonged excited-state lifetime and efficient electron-hole pair dissociation capabilities ( 25 , 26 ), a 2,2′-bipyridine-functionalized pyrene-based COF (denoted as Pybpy-COF) was prepared by reacting 4,4′,4″,4″′-(pyrene1,3,6,8-tetrayl)-tetraaniline and [2,2′-bipyridine]-5,5′-dicarbaldehyde (Bpy-CHO) in the presence of 1-butanol, 1,2-dichlorobenzene, and 3 M AcOH at 120°C for 72 hours. Then, Ni ions were incorporated into Pybpy-COF via a simple postsynthetic wet impregnation with NiBr 2 ·glyme, and the product was denoted as Ni(II)-Pybpy-COF. Ni(II) loading could be easily controlled by adjusting the amount of NiBr 2 ·glyme added ( Fig. 2A ). The experimental powder x-ray diffraction (PXRD) patterns of Pybpy-COF is well matched with that of the simulated eclipsed AA-stacking model. Pawley refinement afforded the optimized cell parameters as a = 35.4142 Å, b = 434.9031 Å, c = 4.1898 Å, and α = β = γ = 90°, which belongs to the P 222 space group, and the residual R wp and R p values converged to 4.15 and 3.23%, respectively ( Fig. 2B and fig. S5). This indicates the material’s structure and its successful preparation. The PXRD patterns of Ni(II)-Pybpy-COF closely resemble those of the pristine COFs; the peak intensities progressively decrease with increasing Ni loading. This reduction can be attributed to pore filling that reduces scattering contrast ( 27 ) and, more importantly, to local structural distortions arising from Ni coordination to the bipyridine units, which lead to partial amorphization while the COF retains its basic structural integrity. In addition, in the high-resolution Ni 2p x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectrum ( Fig. 2C and fig. S15), the binding energy peaks at 856.2 and 873.8 eV correspond to divalent Ni ions in the Ni-bipyridine unit of Ni(II)-Pybpy-COF. In addition, the valence state and coordination environment of nickel are further verified by extended x-ray absorption fine structure spectroscopy measurement, which shows that the structure drawn in Fig. 2A is reasonable (fig. S21 and table S1). An in-depth structural characterization was performed using Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra, field-emission scanning electron microscopy, and transmission electron microscope (TEM), while the surface areas were evaluated from nitrogen adsorption isotherms (figs. S6 to S13). The optical properties of Pybpy-COF and Ni(II)-Pybpy-COF were assessed by solid-state ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) diffuse reflectance spectra to verify the feasibility of using Ni(II)-Pybpy-COF to catalyze reactions under visible light irradiation solely ( Fig. 2D ).

Next, visible-light-driven C-S cross-coupling reaction between 4-iodobenzonitrile 1a and tetra- O -acetylated 1-thio-β- d -glucopyranose 2a was first used to compare their catalytic performances. Pleasingly, after extensive optimization, 99% yield of the desired thioglycoside 3a could be obtained with 2 mg of Ni(II)-Pybpy-COF (4.96 wt % Ni) with N , N -diisopropylethylamine (DIPEA) in CH 3 CN:CH 3 OH = 3:1 (0.1 M) at 25°C under Ar atmosphere and blue LED irradiation (table S2, entry 1). Only single β-anomer product was observed in this reaction system, which shows high stereoselectivity. The effect of other crucial parameters of reaction was further studied. Notably, the reaction yield decreased significantly in the absence of a nickel catalyst (table S2, entry 2). Similarly, no desired product was observed without Ni(II)-Pybpy-COF or light (table S2, entries 3 and 4). Further screening revealed that DIPEA plays an essential role in this transformation (table S2, entry 5). In comparison, homogeneous pyrene/Ni catalytic system achieved only a 6% yield in an hour (table S2, entry 6). This thus highlights that the Ni(II)-Pybpy-COF catalyst roughly surpasses the corresponding homogeneous catalysts by an order of magnitude. Moreover, other nickel catalysts (table S2, entry 7) could work for this strategy, although with lower efficiency (67 and 31% yield, respectively). In addition, aryl bromide also had a good result (table S2, entry 8). Coupling of unprotected 1-thio-β- d -glucopyranose with 4-iodobenzonitrile under our optimized conditions furnished exclusively β-anomer product in a good yield (86%; table S2, entry 9).

With the optimal conditions in hand, we turned our attention to investigating the generality of this protocol. All the couplings proceeded cleanly and selectively in good yields. As shown in Fig. 3 , cross-coupling of 2a with aryl halides bearing various functional groups [-COOMe, -C(O)Me, -CN, and halide atom] yielded corresponding thioglycosylated products ( 3a to 3e ) in up to 96%. The position of the substituent has no significant influence on the resulting yield ( 3a , 3f , and 3h ). In addition, an excellent yield was observed for substrates with electron-donating or electron-withdrawing substituents ( 3a to 3e and 3j to 3p ). Coupling reactions using heteroaryl iodides prepared from pyridine, thiophene, and indole have similarly produced 3q , 3u , and 3t in yields of 82, 86, and 79%, respectively. Next, we moved on to investigate the reactivity of alkene and alkyne bromides with 2a . The use of E-styryl bromide 1v resulted in the stereoselective formation of the desired alkenyl thioglycoside derivative 3v , achieved with a yield of 96%. Furthermore, the reaction of 1-(bromoethynyl)-4-methylbenzene 1w with 2a produced the desired alkynyl-thioether 3w , yielding 73%.

Subsequently, we investigated the cross-coupling reaction involving various glycosyl thiols and 4-iodobenzonitrile 1a . As illustrated in Fig. 3 , this coupling reaction demonstrates a tolerance for different sugars, including O -acetylated 1-thio-β- d -galactopyranose, O -acetylated 1-thio-β- l -fucopyranose, and O -benzoylated 1-thio-β- d -glucopyranose, yielding thioglycosides 3x to 3z with yields reaching up to 98%. In addition, the size of reactants exerts an influence on the catalytic reactivity (fig. S36). Furthermore, this coupling methodology was successfully extended to more complex and biologically relevant saccharide derivatives, specifically O -acetylated 1-thio-β- d -cellobiose and 1-thio-β- d -maltotriose, resulting in the formation of corresponding products 3aa and 3ab with yields of 83 and 86%, respectively. The coupling procedure is not restricted solely to β-thiosugars; it also functions effectively with thiols having the α configuration. Notably, there were minimal differences in reactivity between the α- and β-anomers, as evidenced by a 96% yield of 3a from the coupling of 4-iodobenzonitrile with 1-thio-β- d -glucopyranose, in contrast to a 60% yield of 3ac from the coupling of 1-thio-α- d -glucopyranose. Furthermore, when 2,3,4-tris- O -(phenylmethyl)-1-thio-α- d -glucopyranose was used as the substrate, the corresponding product 3ad achieved an excellent yield of 93%. In the previous researches, the preparation of α-configuration products was not realized by photocatalysis and electrocatalysis, but our work successfully achieved this breakthrough.

To further investigate the efficiency and potential of our work at the synthetic application level, we conducted a systematic evaluation of the feasibility of the S-arylation reaction of unprotected thiosaccharides. Unprotected thio-β- d -galactopyranose 1ae , thio-β- d -cellobiose 1af , thio-β- d -glucose 1ag , and thio-β- l -fucopyranose 1ah reacted effectively, yielding the corresponding products 3ae to 3ah with significant yields. Even when the reaction is subjected to long-term irradiation, the product does not undergo any side reactions (fig. S41). Unprotected thiosugars require a relatively longer reaction time, which is attributed to the dual effects of strong adsorption with COFs and a high energy barrier for oxidative addition step (fig. S37). This outcome highlights the potential of the photocatalytic method for the S arylation of unprotected thiosugars, emphasizing its significant advantages and robust performance in this application.

Encouraged by the aforementioned results, a variety of protected and unprotected amino acids and peptides were investigated. Protected cysteine 1ai , the dipeptide cysteine-glycine 1aj , the dipeptide cysteine-histidine 1ak , and glutathione 1al successfully reacted with aryl iodides, yielding the desired products 3ai to 3al up to 98% yield. Notably, unprotected cysteine 1am and glutathione 1an also produced target products 3am and 3an in 90 and 86% yield, respectively. In addition, both protected and deprotected thiol sugars demonstrated the ability to react with iodobenzene-containing dipeptides 3ao and 3ap . This preliminary result indicates that the combination of free carboxyl groups and free amino groups is well tolerated in this heterogeneous photochemical cross-coupling.

Last, we explored the efficacy of Ni(II)-Pybpy-COF in facilitating heterogeneous thioglycosylation between unprotected thiosugars and MP peptides (antimicrobial peptide mastoparan). As illustrated in Fig. 4A , MP-X (where X denotes an amino acid derived from mastoparan MP with the sequence INLKALAALAKKIL) underwent modified heterogeneous reaction conditions with unprotected thiosugars in the presence of Ni(II)-Pybpy-COF, that is, 1-thio-β- d -glucopyranose in a MeCN/H 2 O mixture under an argon atmosphere at room temperature irradiated with 455-nm blue light. To our delight, we successfully achieved thioglycosylation of peptides MP1, MP2, and MP3. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported instance of peptide backbone functionalization via heterogeneous photocatalysis. The success of this method establishes a novel pathway for innovative applications in bioconjugation chemistry. Furthermore, these glycopeptides were evaluated for cytotoxicity in the colorectal cancer (CRC; the third most common malignant tumor with high mortality) cell line CT26 in vitro, with median inhibitory concentration values ranging from 6.95 to 19.01 μM ( Fig. 4B ). The sugar-modified MP3 exhibited significantly enhanced anti-CRC activity compared to the parent MP, making it a promising candidate for the development of innovative cancer therapeutics.

To evaluate the scalability of this photochemical protocol, we conducted selected reactions on an expanded scale (fig. S22). Reaction of 4-iodobenzonitrile 1a with tetra- O -acetylated 1-thio-β- d -glucopyranose 2a or unprotected 1-thio-β- d -glucopyranose 2ag afforded 3a in 97% yield (1.35 g) and 3ag in 92% yield (1.09 g), respectively. Moreover, the reusability of Ni(II)-Pybpy-COF, as an intrinsic advantage of heterogeneous catalysts, was verified through reusable experiments. As illustrated in fig. S23, with 4-iodobenzonitrile 1a and tetra- O -acetylated 1-thio-β- d -glucopyranose 2a serving as the reactants, the catalytic capability of Ni(II)-Pybpy-COF remains almost unchanged for at least 5 cycles. Subsequently, we investigated the heterogeneous nature of the Ni(II)-Pybpy-COF catalyst through a hot filtration experiment (fig. S24). The catalyst was isolated from the reaction solution, after which the reaction continued in the filtrate without the presence of Ni(II)-Pybpy-COF. No increase in the conversion of 4-iodobenzonitrile or the yield of the desired thioglycoside 3a was observed, indicating that the catalytic reaction was inhibited. Comparisons of UV-Vis spectra, PXRD patterns, and Ni 2p XPS spectra between fresh and used Ni(II)-Pybpy-COF demonstrate that both the composition and structure of Ni(II)-Pybpy-COF were preserved despite partial loss of crystallinity (fig. S23, B to D). X-ray absorption fine structure analysis reveals that the recovered Ni(II)-Pybpy-COF maintains stable Ni-N coordinated active sites (Ni 2+ , no NiO or Ni 0 aggregates), accounting for its excellent recyclability and stable photocatalysis (fig. S25 and table S15). Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) analysis revealed a Ni concentration of only 0.042 parts per million in the supernatant after the first catalytic run and a Ni retention of 4.35 wt % after 5 cycles (fresh: 4.96 wt %), confirming that the Ni species are firmly anchored to the COF framework and that the catalysis is primarily heterogeneous in nature. The performance evaluation shows that the turnover frequency (TOF) of the system is as high as 114 hour -1 , which is 43 times higher than that of the previously reported nickel-catalyzed ET systems. At the same time, it also shows generally better compatibility in substrate applicability ( Fig. 4C ) ( 12 , 14 , 28 ).

DISCUSSION
To evaluate whether the EnT pathway was operative, as outlined in our mechanistic scheme ( Fig. 1C ), we conducted the coupling of 1a and 2a under our catalytic conditions using only Ni(bpy)Br 2 . This reaction yielded the desired product 3a at an 8% yield after 1.0 hour ( Fig. 4D ). The efficiency of this process is enhanced in the presence of benzophenone, an organic triplet sensitizer. This observation demonstrates that the formation of a Ni(II) excited-state species, which supports the conclusion that an EnT mechanism is operative in the presence of the photocatalyst. To further investigate the EnT mechanism, we used photosensitizers with varying triplet energies alongside Ni(bpy)Br 2 complexes to catalyze the reaction ( Fig. 4E ).

The yield of 3a was observed to be strongly correlated with the energy of the excited state associated with the photosensitizer and, in fact, exhibited a cutoff of ∼40 kcal mol -1 , below which no conversion occurred. Furthermore, the catalytic performance did not correlate with the redox potential of the photocatalyst, which is consistent with the triplet-triplet EnT (TTEnT) mechanism. Next, the electrochemical properties of tetra- O -acetylated 1-thio-β- d -glucopyranose 2a was carried out (fig. S27). The irreversible oxidation potential of 2a was observed at 0.79 V versus saturated calomel electrode (SCE), while the oxidation potential of 2a in the presence of DIPEA was recorded at 1.28 V versus SCE. Both values lie outside the redox range of the excited state of Ni(II)-Pybpy-COF, indicating that a photoinduced single-electron transfer (SET) pathway was not thermodynamically feasible.

To obtain a comprehensive understanding of role of nickel species in catalytic cycle, we examined the UV-Vis spectra of the reaction components and their mixtures. As shown in Fig. 5A , a significant color change and a bathochromic shift in the absorption spectrum within the visible region were observed upon mixing Ni(bpy)Br 2 with 1a in the presence of DIPEA in a mixed solvent of MeCN and MeOH ( Fig. 5B ). In addition, a new absorption peak at 425 nm was detected, which is attributed to the coordination between nickel and thiol. This finding aligns with the anticipated characteristics of parity- and spin-allowed metal-ligand charge transfer absorption bands ( 29 ). To verify this phenomenon, Ni(II)-Pybpy-COF and thiosugar were stirred in MeCN with DIPEA to produce Ni-SR-coordinated COF, referred to as Ni(II)-Pybpy-COF-SR. FTIR analysis reveals that Ni(II)-Pybpy-COF-SR retains the characteristic peaks of thiosugar ( Fig. 5C ), while a peak in the Raman spectrum at 273 cm -1 indicates the formation of Ni-SR ( Fig. 5F ) ( 30 ). The XPS analysis shows that the Ni 2p peak shifts to a higher binding energy, suggesting alterations in the coordination environment of nickel, while the S 2p peak further confirms the formation of Ni─S bonds ( Fig. 5, D and E ) ( 31 , 32 ). This evidence indicates that the coordination of Ni-S is completed before illumination, and this bpy-Ni(II)-SR intermediate acts as an energy acceptor.

To verify our hypothesis, Stern-Volmer quenching experiments demonstrated the quenching of the photoexcited Ni(II)-Pybpy-COF by bpy-Ni(II)-SR (prepared in situ; figs. S28 to S35). To identify the specific moiety interacting with the photocatalyst, substrate 1a and 2a and base (DIPEA) were subjected to the quenching experiments. In contrast to bpy-Ni(II)-SR ( K SV = 927.1 M -1 ), which effectively engages in fluorescence quenching with Ni(II)-Pybpy-COF, the quenching effects were weak for substrate 1a ( K SV = 6.0 M -1 ) and DIPEA ( K SV = 1.3 M -1 ), while no quenching was observed for substrate 2a ( Fig. 5G ). These results indicated that the excited photocatalyst is quenched by EnT with bpy-Ni(II)-SR. In addition, a partial overlap was observed between the emission spectrum of Pybpy-COF and the UV-Vis absorption spectrum of bpy-Ni(II)-SR, indicating the presence of EnT ( Fig. 5H ) ( 18 ). The relatively small degree of overlap suggests that Dexter transfer, which can be very efficient over very short distances, may play a significant role in the reductive elimination step leading to the formation of the C─S bond. To further verify the spatial constraint, we designed and synthesized a new COF (Pydbbpy-COF) with extended bipyridine linkers (figs. S4, S20, and S43 to S48), which increases the distance between the photosensitizer and Ni centers. Catalytic tests revealed that the reaction yield was significantly decreased with this longer linker (table S16). These results strongly support that close spatial proximity is essential for efficient EnT in this system.

To further prove the effect of EnT, NQ-Pybpy-COF with lower triplet energy was synthesized. PXRD, FTIR spectra, nitrogen-sorption isotherms measurements, TEM, and XPS spectrum confirmed the successful preparation of the materials (see Supplementary Text and figs. S6, S7, and S15 to S17). Then, the steady-state phosphorescence spectrum was measured for Pybpy-COF and NQ-Pybpy-COF at 77 K ( Fig. 5I ). According to the computing formula, the triplet energies of these materials were determined to be 43.5 and 41.4 kcal/mol, respectively. It has been reported in literatures that COFs modified by imine bond to quinoline exhibit excellent ET ability ( 33 , 34 ). However, because of its low triplet energy, it hinders EnT and shows limited activity in our system. This is consistent with our mechanism above.

To investigate the mechanism of the photocatalytic EnT-driven C-S cross-coupling reaction, 1,1-diphenylethylene and butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT) were used as radical scavengers. When 2.0 equiv. of 1,1-diphenylethylene and BHT were added, the yields of the desired product 3a were 92 and 86%, respectively. This observation eliminates the possibility of free radicals being present in the reaction. In addition, the yield of 3a hugely diminished in the presence of O 2 (Δ E T1→S0 = 22.5 kcal mol -1 ), which acts as an effective triplet energy quencher of the excited state ( Fig. 5J ) ( 35 , 36 ). The formation of the Ni(I) species was further evidenced by electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy, which identified a Ni(I) species with g II = 2.29 and g ⊥ = 2.10 from the comproportionation reaction ( Fig. 5K ) ( 37 , 38 ). Thus, we proposed that the reaction process was governed by the Ni(I)/Ni(III) cycle.

The mechanisms of the cross-coupling reaction of thioglycosides with aryl halides have been investigated using density functional theory (DFT) calculations ( Fig. 6 ). According to computational and experimental results, a plausible stepwise reaction mechanism was proposed via TTEnT from photocatalyst to bpy-Ni(II)-SR. The equilibrium isotropy of the Ni complex does not enhance the degeneracy of the resonance states of the excited state at the light-initiated reaction site. DFT calculation shows that the Gibbs free-energy change (Δ G ) associated with the formation of the bpy-Ni(II)-SR ( B ) complex is -29.7 kcal/mol, which suggests that this transformation is thermodynamically spontaneous and highly favorable. These energy data not only reflect the substitution trend of the thiosugar ligand over the original ligand but also provide a stable precursor structure for the subsequent photodriven reactions. When visible light irradiates the photocatalyst, the photocatalyst is excited, which may cause EnT and ET with the complex B . The DFT results indicate that the SET step must overcome an activation energy barrier (Δ G ‡) of 47.94 kcal/mol. However, the formation of the valence inversion triplet species ( 3 B; C ) through TTEnT has an energy barrier of only 37.11 kcal/mol ( 39 ). This suggests that the EnT pathway has a significant kinetic advantage (ΔΔ G = 10.83 kcal/mol). The coordination configuration of excited species C has an empty coordination site in its metal center, which significantly reduces the steric hindrance for subsequent oxidative addition. According to theoretical simulations, the oxidative addition of iodobenzene at this site requires an energy state of 5.17 kcal/mol ( TS C-D ). Consequently, it exhibits superior reactivity compared to the 15.94 kcal/mol activation energy barrier associated with traditional Ni(I) active intermediates E . In the end, the newly formed Ni(III) intermediate F can produce the target product through coordinated reductive elimination steps, thereby regenerating the Ni(I) species. This result was confirmed by in situ FTIR spectroscopy conducted under optimal reaction conditions, where the rapid formation of C-S products was observed (fig. S26). The Ni(I) species combines with the bromine radical to form the initial Ni(II) species A , completing the catalytic cycle.

In summary, a heterogeneous metal photocatalyst was prepared by embedding Ni(II) species into photoactive Pybpy-COF. This platform drives the functionalization of 1-thiosugars via EnT from the COF to a thiosugar-Ni complex, which effectively lowers the activation barrier for excitation and enhances EnT efficiency. The method provides high chemoselectivity, broad substrate scope, mild conditions, and excellent functional group tolerance. Notably, the direct functionalization of unprotected 1-thioglycosides, amino acids, and bioactive MP-peptides was achieved with a TOF up to 114 hour -1 , a 43-fold increase over previously reported. The practical utility of this strategy was further demonstrated by gram-scale synthesis of thioglycoside products, while the catalyst retained its crystallinity and catalytic activity after more than five recycles. This work reveals an EnT mechanism for heterogeneous metallaphotocatalysis in C-S cross-coupling for 1-thioglycoside synthesis, offering a versatile platform with broad potential applications in synthetic chemistry and related fields.

MATERIALS AND METHODS
To a 15-ml glass tube were added 4,4′,4″,4″′-(pyrene-1,3,6,8-tetrayl)tetraaniline (0.04 mmol), 2,2′-bipyridyl-5,5′-dialdehyde (0.08 mmol), 1,2-dichlorobenzene (1.0 ml), and 1-butanol (1.0 ml). The mixture was sonicated for 2 min, and then 0.4 ml of 3 M acetic acid was added to it as the catalyst and sonicated for another 5 min. Subsequently, the tube was placed in a bath of liquid nitrogen and degassed under freeze-pump-thaw for 3 cycles. The tube was flame sealed under vacuum, allowed to return to room temperature, and placed in an oven at 120°C for 3 days. The solid product was collected by centrifugation and washed alternately and repeatedly with tetrahydrofuran and CH 3 OH to remove impurities. After initial drying by filtration, the product was placed in a vacuum drying oven at 80°C for 12 hours to give the orange Pybpy-COF.

To a 25-ml three-necked flask were added a stirring bar, 50 mg of Pybpy-COF, and a certain mass of NiBr 2 ·glyme in 10 ml of CH 3 OH and stirred for 12 hours at room temperature. The solid product was collected by centrifugation and washed alternately and repeatedly with H 2 O and CH 3 OH to remove impurities. After initial drying by filtration, the product was placed in a vacuum drying oven at 120°C for 12 hours to give the brown Ni(II)-Pybpy-COF. The content of nickel was determined by ICP-OES test.

An oven-dried 10-ml tube equipped with a stir bar was charged with 4.96% wt % Ni(II)-Pybpy-COF (2 mg, 0.86 mol % Ni) and solid reagents. Subsequently, the tube was sealed with a rubber plug and inflated deflated three times using a vacuum gas manifold to ensure that the tube was filled with inert gas (argon). The prepared liquid reagents were then injected into the tube using a syringe and sealed again with a parafilm. The tube was lastly placed the 455-nm Blue LEDs and irradiated at room temperature for corresponding time under a fan cooling. At the end of the reaction, silica gel was added to the mixture, and the crude product was obtained by rotary evaporation. Afterward, the crude product was purified by flash column chromatography to afford the desired product and characterized by 1 H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), 19 F NMR, and mass spectrometry (EI).
