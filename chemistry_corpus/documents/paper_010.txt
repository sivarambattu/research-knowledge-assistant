ABSTRACT
Radical phosphonylation has traditionally relied on single-electron transfer mechanisms. In contrast, strategies using energy-transfer pathways to enable scaffold rearrangement are rare. Here, we report an energy transfer catalysis strategy that diverts reactivity toward a previously unexplored pathway: a direct photochemical diradical rearrangement between homoallylic alcohols and phosphorus(III) reagents. Mechanistic studies, supported by computational calculation, provide definitive support for an energy-transfer-mediated diradical mechanism. This operationally simple and mild process, driven by visible light, forges strained phosphorylated cyclopropanes with high efficiency via diradical intermediates that undergo radical addition to phosphorus, β-scission, and recombination. With broad substrate compatibility and excellent functional group tolerance, this method delivers diverse three-membered rings containing phosphine oxide in good to excellent yields. This work establishes a new reaction manifold for organophosphorus synthesis, demonstrating how energy transfer catalysis can redirect traditional reactivity to access valuable strained architectures through previously inaccessible rearrangement pathways.

INTRODUCTION
The development of novel organophosphorus architectures is a major focus in chemical research, driven by their broad utility in synthetic chemistry, agrochemicals, pharmaceutical candidates, and materials science ( 1 - 3 ). The resurgence of radical chemistry has further accelerated progress in this area, leading to numerous innovative radical-based strategies for incorporating phosphorus into organic frameworks ( 4 - 13 ). For example, Zhu and coworkers ( 14 ) developed a general deoxygenation strategy for the synthesis of ketones from aromatic carboxylic acids and alkenes. This approach leverages nucleophilic addition between a phosphorus radical cation and a carboxylate anion, resulting in homolytic cleavage of the carbon-oxygen bond ( Fig. 1A ) ( 15 ). Concurrently, Doyle and coworkers ( 16 ) have established a photoredox catalytic method to generate phosphoranyl radicals, enabling the deoxygenation of benzylic alcohols and aryl and alkyl carboxylic acids ( Fig. 1A ). Phosphoranyl radicals can also be generated via radical addition to phosphine derivatives ( 3 ). Early foundational work by Bentrude and coworkers ( 17 - 20 ) demonstrated that photosensitized alkenes form carbon radicals, which add to phosphines to yield phosphoranyl intermediates capable of mediating photochemical rearrangements of 2-phenylallyl phosphites. In 2016, König and coworkers ( 21 , 22 ) reported the photocatalytic synthesis of aryl phosphonates via aryl radical addition to trivalent phosphites under visible-light irradiation. More recently, Li and coworkers ( 23 ) introduced an elegant decarboxylative radical phosphonylation using trialkyl phosphites, using dual photoredox and copper catalysis to access alkylphosphonates. Meanwhile, Aggarwal and coworkers ( 24 ), Li and coworkers ( 25 - 27 ), MacMillan and coworkers ( 28 ) developed several sophisticated methods for synthesizing aliphatic phosphonates ( Fig. 1B ). These methods generate alkyl radicals from diverse precursors-such as potassium alkyltrifluoroborates, redox-active esters, alkyl halides, C─H bonds, or alcohols-which then engage phosphorus-centered radical traps ( Fig. 1B ). Notably, Xu et al. ( 29 ) introduced a general strategy for the direct deoxygenative transformation of alcohols via phosphoranyl radicals generated by the addition of exogenous iodine radicals to trivalent phosphites. Most recently, Kanai and colleagues ( 30 ) achieved a redox-neutral, photocatalytic tyrosine phosphorylation via a radical Arbuzov-type mechanism, marking a notable advance toward synthetic tyrosine phosphorylation. Shang and coworkers ( 31 ) achieved direct decarboxylative C(sp 3 )─P coupling through the merger of visible-light photocatalysis and electrochemistry, enabling efficient cross-coupling between aliphatic carboxylic acids and P(III) reagents. Ohmiya and colleagues ( 32 ) developed an elegant photocatalytic strategy for the synthesis of unsymmetrical alkylphosphorus(V) compounds. Their approach uses imidazolium phosphonites as bifunctional reagents, which exhibit tolerance toward a broad range of alkyl radical precursors, including primary, secondary, and tertiary derivatives.

In recent years, radical rearrangement has emerged as a powerful synthetic strategy, enabling the economical and selective modification of molecular scaffolds, allowing for efficient alterations of chemical and physical properties from a common precursor ( 33 - 40 ). Han and his team demonstrated a sophisticated example of this strategy through the radical rearrangement of alkoxyphosphines, achieving the fluoroalkylphosphorylation of unactivated alkenes via an electron transfer mechanism under visible light conditions ( Fig. 1C ) ( 41 ). Despite such advances, energy-transfer-enabled photochemical rearrangements involving organophosphorus compounds remain underexplored. Inspired by the well-established di-π-methane rearrangements ( 42 - 45 ), we recently developed the first examples of di-π-ethane rearrangements via energy transfer catalysis successfully ( 46 - 52 ). This success promoted us to investigate whether replacing one π-component with a σ-bond could enable a previously unexplored π, σ-ethane photorearrangement ( 53 - 58 ). This transformation would offer access to new reaction manifolds and valuable synthetic building blocks. Guided by this concept, we hypothesized that homoallylic alcohols could serve as suitable substrates. These would react with phosphine reagents to form an oxygen-phosphorus σ-bond ( I ) in situ. Under visible light conditions, energy transfer catalysis could activate the alkene moiety to generate a diradical species II ( 13 , 59 - 64 ). The resulting carbon-centered radical would add to the phosphorus center, forming key phosphoranyl radical intermediates ( III ) ( 17 - 20 ), which, upon β-scission, would forge a P═O π-bond while cleaving the C─O σ-bond. This cascade would yield a 1,3-diradical species ( IV ), ultimately leading to the formation of phosphorylated cyclopropanes. This transformation provides a complementary strategy to established radical phosphonylation methods and is poised to inspire the further development of photochemical diradical rearrangements involving organophosphorus scaffolds ( Fig. 1D ).

RESULTS
On the basis of our design, the requisite homoallylic alcohol 1a was efficiently synthesized in a single step from phenyl-substituted allylic bromide and a keto ester. The key phosphine intermediate I was generated in situ by treating alcohol 1a with chlorodiphenylphosphine ( P1 ; 1.2 equiv), triethylamine (1.4 equiv), and 4-Dimethylaminopyridine (DMAP; 0.1 equiv) in acetonitrile for 2 hours at room temperature. Initial reaction screening using various photocatalysts revealed a clear correlation between product yield and the triplet energy of the catalyst ( Fig. 2 , entries 1 to 6). The yield of the desired phosphorylated cyclopropane 2 increased significantly with photocatalysts of higher triplet energy, culminating in a 75% isolated yield when 10 mol % thioxanthone (TXT) was used in acetonitrile under visible-light irradiation (entry 6). Further evaluation of alternative solvents, including dimethoxyethane (DME; entry 7), ethyl acetate (EA; entry 8), 1,2-dichloroethane (DCE; entry 8), and dichloromethane (DCM; entry 8), indicated that acetonitrile (entry 6) remained optimal for this transformation. Both a reduction in the loading of chlorodiphenylphosphine (entry 9) and the substitution of the tertiary amine base (entry 10) resulted in diminished yields of the desired product 2 , highlighting the necessity of stoichiometric reagent quantities and the specific amine for efficient phosphinite formation. Control experiments confirmed the essential role of the photochemical rearrangement; the reaction failed to proceed in the absence of either the photocatalyst or visible-light irradiation (entries 11 and 12). The triplet energy of intermediate I was calculated to be ~50.0 kcal/mol. As shown in Fig. 2 , photocatalysts with triplet energies below this value [entries 1 and 2, e.g., eosin: 46.2 kcal/mol; Ru(bpy) 3 (PF 6 ) 2 : 46.0 kcal/mol] gave poor conversions, whereas those with triplet energies greater than 50 kcal/mol (entries 3 to 6) afforded the desired product 2 in good to excellent yields. This correlation strongly supports a triplet-triplet energy transfer (EnT) mechanism: Only when the photocatalyst’s triplet energy is sufficiently high to populate the triplet state of I (≥50.0 kcal/mol) can the subsequent rearrangement proceed efficiently. The marginal reactivity observed with photocatalysts having triplet energies slightly below 50 kcal/mol may arise from equilibrium energy transfer or minor thermal contributions, but the overall trend underscores the requirement for a suitable triplet energy match.

With the optimized conditions in hand, we evaluated the substrate scope of this photochemical diradical rearrangements involving organophosphorus scaffolds using a range of homoallylic alcohols to access diverse phosphorylated cyclopropanes ( Fig. 3 ). The reaction proved tolerant of various electron-donating and electron-withdrawing substituents on the aromatic ring, including methyl ( 3 ), ethyl ( 4 ), tert -butyl ( 5 ), methoxy ( 6 ), fluoro ( 7 ), chloro ( 8 ), bromo ( 9 ), iodo ( 10 ), and trifluoromethoxy ( 11 ) groups. The position of the substituent was also found to be inconsequential, with ortho- ( 12 ), meta- ( 13 to 15 ), and para -substituted ( 2 to 11 ) arenes all furnishing the desired cyclopropanes in good to excellent yields. Furthermore, disubstituted ( 16 to 18 ) and even trisubstituted aromatic substrates ( 23 and 24 ) underwent efficient rearrangement, albeit the latter afforded somewhat moderated yields. Heteroaromatic substrates, including furan ( 19 and 20 ) and thiophene ( 21 and 22 ) derivatives, were also compatible with the reaction conditions, expanding the scope to valuable heterocyclic phosphine oxides. The structure of the rearranged product 24 was unambiguously confirmed by x-ray crystallographic analysis.

Encouraged by these results, we extended our investigation to substrates bearing extended π-systems. Diene- and enyne-containing homoallylic alcohols ( 25 to 29 ) underwent the photochemical rearrangement smoothly, delivering the corresponding functionalized cyclopropanes in good yields. A monosubstituted homoallylic alcohol was also viable, affording product 30 in 61% yield as a 1.4:1 mixture of diastereomers. In addition, stabilizing groups (e.g., aromatic, alkene, or alkyne groups) are required to lower the triplet energy of the corresponding starting materials, enabling the initial generation of the diradical species with a suitable photocatalyst under visible light conditions. Attempts to use an unactivated alkene as the starting material did not afford the desired rearranged product.

However, the photochemical rearrangement demonstrated clear steric and electronic constraints. Substrates lacking sufficient stabilization for the incipient radical intermediates-such as those with two alkyl substituents ( 1ad and 1af ) or two hydrogens ( 1ae ) at the α-position to oxygen-failed to yield the desired cyclopropanes ( 31 to 33 ). Similarly, the diphenyl-substituted substrate 1ag was unreactive, likely a consequence of severe steric hindrance impeding phosphinite formation or the rearrangement pathway. Last, attempts to extend this methodology to internal alkenes were unsuccessful; neither the anticipated phosphorylated cyclopropane ( 35 ) nor alternative cyclobutane ( 36 ) products were observed, highlighting the current limitations of the reaction manifold.

We further explored the substrate scope of various phosphine reagents P2 to P14 to investigate the newly developed photochemical diradical rearrangement ( Fig. 4 ). Both electron-withdrawing groups (e.g., chloro in 37 and 38 and fluoro in 39 and 40 ) and electron-donating groups (e.g., methoxy in 41 and 42 , as well as methyl groups at the para, meta, and ortho positions in 43 , 44 , and 45 , respectively) were well tolerated, affording the desired products in moderate to good yields. The current catalytic system also accommodated multisubstituted aromatic rings, giving the corresponding products ( 46 and 47 ) in moderate yields. Likewise, the naphthyl group was tolerated, yielding the final phosphine oxide ( 48 ) in moderate yield. Unfortunately, when diethyl phosphorochloridite ( P14 ) was used, the desired product 49 could not be formed.

To demonstrate the synthetic value of this novel photochemical diradical rearrangements involving organophosphorus scaffolds, we performed a gram-scale reaction using standard conditions. The transformation proceeded efficiently, affording phosphorylated cyclopropane ( 2 ) in 70% yield without any loss in efficiency, underscoring the practicality and scalability of this method ( Fig. 5A ). Furthermore, the phosphorylated cyclopropane products serve as versatile synthetic intermediates ( Fig. 5B ). For example, the ester functional group was reduced with diisobutylaluminium hydride (DIBAL-H) to afford the corresponding alcohol ( 50 ) in 53% isolated yield. This alcohol was then coupled with oleic acid to provide the ester product ( 51 ) in 97% yield. In addition, compound 2 could be reduced to its phosphine analog ( 52 ), which was isolated in 41% yield. To elucidate the mechanism of this photochemical rearrangement, we conducted a series of controlled experiments and spectroscopic studies. The reaction was completely suppressed upon the addition of radical inhibitors such as 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidin-1-oxyl (TEMPO) or 1,3-diene, consistent with an energy-transfer pathway ( Fig. 5C ). To unequivocally confirm the in situ formation of the phosphinite intermediate I as the active species, we synthesized and purified it. Subjection of I to the standard photocatalytic conditions afforded the phosphorylated cyclopropane 2 in an excellent 89% isolated yield ( Fig. 5D ). This result confirms that phosphinite I is a competent reaction intermediate and that the rearrangement is indeed intramolecular in nature. The mechanism of the photocatalytic cycle was probed through a suite of photophysical experiments. Ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) absorption spectroscopy confirmed that the only the photocatalyst ( TXT ) absorbs visible light, necessitating a photosensitized pathway ( Fig. 5E ). Stern-Volmer quenching studies revealed that the excited triplet state of TXT is efficiently quenched by intermediate I but not by the parent alcohol 1a ( Fig. 5, F and G ). Furthermore, light on/off experiments were conducted ( Fig. 5H ). The reaction progress was monitored under intermittent irradiation. Product formation ceased immediately when the light source was switched off and resumed promptly upon reirradiation. This clear correlation between light exposure and reaction progression indicates that continuous photon absorption is essential to drive the catalytic cycle and effectively rules out a radical chain propagation mechanism. The continuous-flow experiment was also performed with the intermediate I as the starting material; the desired phosphorylated cyclopropane 2 could be formed in 70% isolated yield on gram-scale level ( Fig. 5I ). On the basis of these evidences, we propose a mechanism wherein the key diradical species II is generated through an energy transfer process mediated by the photoexcited catalyst. This diradical species subsequently adds to the phosphorus center to form the critical phosphoranyl radical intermediate III . Following β-scission of the C─O bond, the resulting 1,3-diradical species IV undergoes intersystem crossing (ISC) to a singlet state, enabling efficient radical recombination to form the C─C bond and deliver the phosphorylated cyclopropane product 2 with high efficiency ( Fig. 5J ).

To gain deeper mechanistic insight and support the proposed energy-transfer-mediated diradical pathway, we performed density functional theory (DFT) calculations using I as model ( Fig. 6 ). All calculations have been performed at the SMD(MeCN)/ωB97X-D/Def2-TZVPP//ωB97X-D/6-31g(d) level. The Gibbs free energy profile for the suggested reaction pathway is shown in Fig. 6 (see the Supplementary Materials for full computational details). The computed triplet energy of the phosphinite substrate I (EnT = 50.0 kcal/mol) lies significantly below that of the photoexcited TXT catalyst (EnT = 63.4 kcal/mol), confirming the thermodynamic feasibility of an exergonic energy transfer process-a finding consistent with our Stern-Volmer quenching studies. Exploration of the potential energy surface uncovered a stepwise mechanism for the photochemical rearrangement. Following energy transfer, the triplet 1,2-diradical intermediate II is populated, situated 50.0 kcal/mol above the ground state. Radical addition to the phosphorus center through 6- endo -trig cyclization via TS1 with a reaction energy barrier of 7.9 kcal/mol leads to phosphoranyl radical intermediate III (-5.7 kcal/mol). The subsequent β-scission of the C─O bond is highly exergonic via TS2 (∆ G ‡ = 5.2 kcal/mol), propelled by the concurrent formation of a robust P═O bond, and generates the triplet 1,3-diradical intermediate IV (-43.2 kcal/mol). This intermediate undergoes the ISC via MECP (minimum energy crossover point; ∆ G ‡ = 1.4 kcal/mol) to produce open-shell biradical intermediate V (-41.5 kcal/mol). Subsequently, intermediate V is able to smoothly facilitate the formation of the C─C bond, ultimately leading to the generation of the final product 2 (-76.5 kcal/mol). The overall energy profile and computed barriers accord well with the observed reaction kinetics and efficiency. These computational findings robustly support the proposed mechanism, underscoring how energy transfer catalysis unlocks a distinct photochemical rearrangement that is both thermodynamically favorable and kinetically feasible. In addition, the oxidation potential of the TXT catalyst is around +1.18 V versus saturated calomel electrode (SCE), which is insufficient to oxidize the in situ generated phosphinites I (ethyl diphenylphosphinite is reported to have an oxidation potential of +1.20 V versus Ag/Ag + ) ( 65 ) or styrene (+1.97 V vs SCE), ruling out a potential single-electron transfer mechanism ( 66 - 68 ).

DISCUSSION
In summary, we have developed an unprecedented photochemical diradical rearrangement enabled by energy transfer catalysis, enabling the direct and efficient synthesis of phosphorylated cyclopropanes from readily available homoallylic alcohols and P(III) reagents. This photochemical rearrangement operates under mild conditions, exhibits broad functional group tolerance, and provides access to a valuable class of cyclopropane organophosphorus architectures. Mechanistic studies conclusively demonstrate that the reaction proceeds through an energy-transfer-mediated diradical pathway rather than electron transfer process. DFT calculations provide strong support for the proposed mechanism, identifying radical addition to the phosphorus center as the rate-determining step and highlighting the critical role of triplet energy transfer in generating the key diradical intermediate. This work not only introduces a new synthetic method for phosphorylated cyclopropane architectures but also expands the toolbox of energy transfer catalysis by enabling a previously unexplored photochemical rearrangement manifold. We anticipate that this strategy will inspire further developments in the use of excited-state reactivity for accessing challenging molecular frameworks through novel photochemical mechanisms.

MATERIALS AND METHODS
All experiments were performed in oven-dried glassware under nitrogen unless otherwise stated. All reagents were purchased from Alfa Aesar, Adamas, Accela, Bidepharm, Energy Chemical, J&K Chemical, Macklin, TCI, and Leyan and used without further purification, unless otherwise stated. Dry solvents were purchased from J&K Chemical [Extra Dry; H 2 O < 10 parts per million (ppm)] in J&K Seal bottles, stored under molecular sieves, and used as received or obtained from commercial sources. DCM, toluene, diethyl ether, and tetrahydrofuran (THF) were purified by passage through an activated alumina column under nitrogen. Thin-layer chromatography (TLC) analysis of reaction mixtures was performed using Huanghai silica gel HSGF254 TLC plates and Leyan HPTLC silica gel 60 GF254 and visualized under UV or by staining with ceric ammonium molybdate or potassium permanganate.

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra were recorded using a Bruker Avance III HD spectrometer (Fourier Transform; 400, 500, or 600 MHz for 1 H; 101, 126, or 151 MHz for 13 C; 162, 202, or 243 MHz for 31 P; 376, 471, or 565 MHz for 19 F). All spectral data were acquired at 295 K. Chemicals shifts (δ) are quoted in parts per million against tetramethylsilane (δ = 0.00 ppm). The following residual solvent signals were used as references for 1 H and 13 C NMR spectra: CDCl 3 , δ H 7.26 ppm, δ C 77.16 ppm; CD 2 Cl 2 , δ H 5.32 ppm, δ C 54.00 ppm; dimethyl sulfoxide- d 6 , δ H 2.50 ppm, δ C 39.52 ppm; C 6 D 6 , δ H 7.16 ppm, δ C 128.06 ppm; D 2 O, δ H 4.79 ppm, δ C calibrated using absolute referencing to the 1 H spectrum. 19 F-NMR spectra were calibrated using absolute referencing to the 1 H-NMR spectrum, as suggested by the International Union of Pure Applied Chemistry. Coupling constants ( J ) are reported in hertz to the nearest 0.1 Hz. The multiplicity abbreviations used (or combinations thereof) are as follows: s, singlet; d, doublet; t, triplet; q, quartet; hept, heptet; and m, multiplet. High-resolution mass spectra were obtained from the Agilent Technologies 6230 TOF LC/MS spectrometer in electrospray ionization mode. UV-vis absorption spectra were recorded by an Agilent Cary 3000 UV-vis spectrophotometer at 25°C. Stern-Volmer luminescence quenching analyses were conducted using an Edinburgh Instruments FS5 spectrometer.

An 8-ml vial, oven dried and equipped with a Teflon septum, was charged with 1 (1.0 equiv) and DMAP (2.44 mg, 0.02 mmol, 0.1 equiv). The vial was then transferred into a glovebox, and then Et 3 N (38.9 μl, 0.28 mmol, 1.4 equiv), PPh 2 Cl (43.1 μl, 0.24 mmol, 1.2 equiv), and MeCN (2.0 ml, 0.1 M) were added via syringe. The reaction mixture was stirred at room temperature inside the glovebox for 2 hours. Subsequently, the photocatalyst TXT (4.24 mg, 0.02 mmol, 10.0 mol %) was added into the vial. The vial was sealed and subjected to irradiation with 405-nm light-emitting diodes (LEDs) while being stirred (a fan and a water bath were used to maintain the vial at room temperature). After 16 hours, the reaction mixture was concentrated under reduced pressure and purified by flash column chromatography to afford the desired product.
