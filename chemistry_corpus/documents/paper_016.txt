ABSTRACT
Escalating environmental pollution and the increasing complexity of contaminants pose significant challenges to conventional remediation technologies, necessitating the development of innovative, sustainable solutions. This review critically examines the role of next-generation materials in environmental remediation and sustainable development, emphasizing their design principles, mechanisms, and practical relevance. Advanced material classes, including nanomaterials, polymeric and composite systems, and emerging functional materials such as metal-organic frameworks, covalent organic frameworks, and smart hybrid materials are systematically discussed with respect to their structure, property, and function relationships. The review highlights material-enabled remediation strategies for removing organic pollutants, heavy metals, metalloids, and emerging contaminants via adsorption, catalytic degradation, membrane separation, and integrated hybrid approaches. Particular attention is given to mechanistic insights, sustainability considerations, and the incorporation of green synthesis and life cycle assessment. The integration of artificial intelligence, smart sensing, and data-driven optimization for material design and process control is also addressed. Finally, key challenges related to scalability, environmental safety, and real-world implementation are outlined, along with future research directions. Overall, this review underscores the critical importance of material innovation in advancing efficient, scalable, and sustainable environmental remediation technologies aligned with global Sustainable Development Goals. INTRODUCTION
Rapid industrialization, urban expansion, and population growth have intensified environmental pollution, posing severe risks to ecosystems, public health, and economic stability worldwide [ 1 ]. Contamination of water, soil, and air by industrial effluents, heavy metals, synthetic dyes, pharmaceuticals, pesticides, microplastics, and greenhouse gases has become a persistent global challenge [ 2 ]. Conventional environmental remediation strategies such as physicochemical treatment, thermal processes, and bulk adsorption have played an essential role in mitigating pollution; however, they are often energy-intensive, costly, inefficient at low contaminant concentrations, and may generate secondary pollutants or hazardous sludge [ 3 ]. These limitations underscore the urgent need for innovative, efficient, and sustainable remediation approaches that can address complex and emerging environmental contaminants.

Materials science has emerged as a framework in the development of advanced technologies aimed at achieving the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly those related to clean water and sanitation (SDG 6), affordable and clean energy (SDG 7), sustainable cities and communities (SDG 11), responsible consumption and production (SDG 12), climate action (SDG 13), and life below water and on land (SDGs 14 and 15) [ 4 ]. By enabling precise control over material composition, structure, surface chemistry, and functionality at the nano- and micro-scale, materials science offers transformative solutions for environmental remediation that are more efficient, selective, and environmentally benign than traditional methods[ 5 ].

Despite progress, conventional remediation materials such as activated carbon, natural clays, and bulk catalysts often suffer from drawbacks including limited adsorption capacity, poor selectivity, slow kinetics, low reusability, and susceptibility to fouling [ 6 , 7 ]. Furthermore, many traditional systems are not designed with sustainability principles in mind, as they rely on non-renewable resources, involve energy-intensive synthesis routes, or lack scalability and life-cycle compatibility. These challenges have accelerated research into next-generation materials that integrate high performance with sustainability, circularity, and eco-friendly design [ 8 , 9 ].

Next-generation materials for environmental remediation encompass a broad and rapidly expanding class of advanced systems, [ 10 ]including nanomaterials, engineered bio-based materials, metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), covalent organic frameworks (COFs), advanced membranes, hybrid nano-bio composites, photocatalysts, electrocatalysts, and smart or stimuli-responsive materials. These materials exhibit unique physicochemical properties such as high surface area, tunable porosity, enhanced reactivity, selective binding sites, and multifunctionality, enabling efficient removal, transformation, or mineralization of a wide range of pollutants [ 11 ]. Importantly, many of these materials are being developed with sustainability considerations, including green synthesis, use of renewable feedstocks, low energy input, recyclability, and reduced environmental footprint [ 12 ].

In addition to pollution control, next-generation materials play a critical role in sustainable development by enabling resource recovery, energy-efficient processes, and integration with biological systems [ 13 ]. For example, advanced materials can facilitate nutrient recovery from wastewater, conversion of pollutants into value-added products, coupling of remediation with renewable energy generation, and enhancement of nature-based solutions. Such multifunctional capabilities align environmental remediation with broader sustainability and circular economy goals [ 14 , 15 ].

Unlike many existing reviews that mainly summarize individual material classes or remediation performances, the present review provides a critical and integrated analysis of next-generation materials by correlating structure-property-function relationships with remediation mechanisms, sustainability, scalability, and real-world applicability. In addition, this review uniquely incorporates discussions on techno-economic feasibility, life-cycle assessment, environmental safety, AI-assisted material design, smart sensing technologies, and alignment with Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). The manuscript also critically compares different material classes in terms of efficiency, stability, regeneration, cost, and environmental impact rather than only describing their applications. These aspects distinguish the present review from previous literature and provide a broader multidisciplinary perspective for the future development of sustainable environmental remediation technologies.

ENVIRONMENTAL POLLUTION AND THE NEED FOR ADVANCED REMEDIATION TECHNOLOGIES
Environmental pollution remains one of the most critical challenges threatening ecological integrity, human health, and sustainable economic development [ 16 ]. The continuous release of contaminants into water, soil, and air because of industrialization, agricultural intensification, energy production, and urban activities has led to widespread environmental degradation. The complexity, persistence, and increasing diversity of pollutants demand remediation technologies that go beyond conventional treatment approaches [ 17 ].

Water pollution arises primarily from industrial effluents, agricultural runoff, domestic sewage, mining activities, and improper waste disposal [ 18 ]. Contaminants such as heavy metals, synthetic dyes, pharmaceuticals, personal care products, nutrients, pathogens, and microplastics compromise water quality and pose serious risks to aquatic ecosystems and human health [ 19 ]. Persistent organic pollutants and endocrine-disrupting compounds can bioaccumulate through the food chain, leading to long-term ecological and toxicological consequences [ 20 ]. Soil pollution is closely linked to industrial waste dumping, excessive use of agrochemicals, mining operations, and accidental oil and chemical spills. Heavy metals, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), pesticides, and industrial solvents alter soil physicochemical properties, reduce fertility, and inhibit microbial activity [ 21 ]. Contaminated soils not only threaten food security but also act as secondary pollution sources through leaching into groundwater and uptake by crops [ 22 ]. Air pollution, driven by fossil fuel combustion, industrial emissions, vehicular exhaust, and open burning of waste, introduces harmful gases (SO₂, NOₓ, CO, and volatile organic compounds) and particulate matter into the atmosphere [ 23 ]. These pollutants contribute to climate change, acid rain, smog formation, and respiratory and cardiovascular diseases [ 24 ]. The interconnected nature of air, water, and soil systems further amplifies pollution impacts, emphasizing the need for integrated remediation strategies.

Industrial wastewater represents one of the most challenging pollution sources due to its high contaminant load, complex composition, and variable discharge patterns [ 25 ]. Effluents from textile, pharmaceutical, petrochemical, food processing, paper, and metal-finishing industries often contain toxic dyes, recalcitrant organic compounds, surfactants, salts, and heavy metals [ 26 ]. Many of these substances exhibit high chemical stability and resistance to biodegradation, making their removal particularly difficult [ 27 ]. In recent years, emerging contaminants such as pharmaceuticals, antibiotics, hormones, nanoparticles, microplastics, and per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) have gained increasing attention [ 28 ]. These pollutants are typically present at low concentrations but exhibit high persistence and biological activity. Conventional treatment systems are not explicitly designed to target such compounds, leading to their continuous release into the environment and raising concerns over antimicrobial resistance, endocrine disruption, and long-term ecological effects [ 29 ].

Traditional remediation technologies, including coagulation-flocculation, chemical oxidation, biological treatment, activated carbon adsorption, and thermal processes, have been widely implemented in pollution control [ 30 , 31 ]. While effective under certain conditions, these methods suffer from several inherent limitations. Many processes are energy-intensive, require large chemical inputs, or produce secondary waste such as toxic sludge that requires further treatment or disposal. Biological treatments can be sensitive to fluctuations in pollutant concentration, temperature, and pH, and often fail to degrade recalcitrant or toxic compounds [ 32 ]. Moreover, conventional materials used in remediation typically exhibit limited selectivity, low adsorption capacity for emerging contaminants, slow reaction kinetics, and poor regeneration performance [ 33 ]. These constraints reduce overall treatment efficiency and increase operational costs, hindering large-scale implementation, particularly in resource-limited settings [ 34 ].

The growing complexity of environmental pollution necessitates a paradigm shift toward advanced remediation technologies that are not only effective but also sustainable and scalable [ 35 ]. Future solutions must combine high removal efficiency with low energy consumption, minimal chemical use, and reduced environmental footprint. They should be adaptable to diverse pollutant profiles, capable of treating low-concentration and emerging contaminants, and suitable for integration into existing treatment infrastructures. Next-generation materials offer significant potential to address these requirements by enabling enhanced adsorption, catalytic degradation, membrane separation, and hybrid treatment processes [ 36 ]. When designed with sustainability principles such as green synthesis, recyclability, and life-cycle compatibility these materials can bridge the gap between pollution control and sustainable development. The development and deployment of advanced remediation technologies based on innovative materials are essential for mitigating environmental pollution and ensuring long-term ecological and human well-being [ 37 ].

CLASSIFICATION OF NEXT-GENERATION MATERIALS FOR ENVIRONMENTAL APPLICATIONS
Next-generation materials for environmental remediation are characterized by their tunable structure, multifunctionality, and ability to interact with contaminants at molecular and interfacial levels [ 38 ]. Unlike conventional materials, which rely primarily on bulk properties, these advanced materials are engineered through precise control of composition, morphology, surface chemistry, and electronic structure [ 39 ]. This section classifies such materials into nanomaterials, polymeric and composite materials, and advanced functional materials, with an emphasis on their underlying concepts, structure-property relationships, and relevance to environmental applications. Nanomaterials are defined by at least one dimension in the nanoscale range (1-100 nm), where quantum confinement effects, high surface-to-volume ratios, and enhanced surface reactivity dominate their behavior [ 40 ]. These attributes make nanomaterials particularly effective for environmental remediation, as pollutant interactions occur predominantly at surfaces and interfaces [ 41 ]. Metal and metal oxide nanoparticles represent one of the most extensively studied classes of nanomaterials. Metals such as silver, iron, and copper, and metal oxides such as titanium dioxide, zinc oxide, and iron oxides, exhibit unique redox, catalytic, and adsorption properties [ 42 , 43 ]. Conceptually, their high density of surface-active sites enables strong interactions with pollutants through electrostatic attraction, surface complexation, and redox reactions [ 44 ]. Iron-based nanoparticles can simultaneously adsorb and chemically transform contaminants through reduction or oxidation mechanisms [ 45 ]. The ability to tailor particle size, crystal phase, and surface functionality allows precise control over reactivity, selectivity, and stability [ 46 ]. Carbon-based nanomaterials, including graphene, carbon nanotubes (CNTs), and biochar, are distinguished by their extended π-electron systems, mechanical robustness, and chemical tenability [ 47 ]. Graphene and CNTs provide large accessible surface areas and strong π-π interactions with aromatic pollutants, making them particularly effective for organic contaminant removal. Biochar, although more heterogeneous, offers a sustainable and low-cost alternative with abundant surface functional groups derived from biomass precursors [ 48 , 49 ]. Conceptually, carbon nanomaterials function as electron-rich platforms that facilitate adsorption, charge transfer, and, in some cases, catalytic degradation, especially when doped or hybridized with metals [ 50 ]. Magnetic nanomaterials combine remediation efficiency with ease of recovery, addressing one of the significant limitations of nanoscale systemspost-treatment separation.Typically based on iron oxides such as magnetite or maghemite, these materials can be retrieved using external magnetic fields without filtration or centrifugation [ 51 ]. Magnetic nanomaterials enable a closed-loop remediation strategy, where adsorption or catalytic activity is followed by rapid separation and regeneration, enhancing operational sustainability and reusability [ 52 ]. Nanomaterials often suffer from aggregation during operation, which reduces their accessible active surface area and overall remediation efficiency over repeated cycles [ 53 ]. Additional concerns include difficulties in regeneration and recovery, nanoparticle leaching, possible ecotoxicological effects, and the relatively high synthesis costs associated with engineered nanostructures such as graphene and carbon nanotubes (CNTs) [ 54 ]. Carbon-based materials, particularly biochar, may exhibit substantial variability in physicochemical properties depending on biomass feedstock and pyrolysis conditions, leading to reproducibility challenges and fouling during long-term operation. Magnetic nanomaterials, while advantageous for easy separation and reuse, may gradually lose magnetic responsiveness and catalytic efficiency after multiple regeneration cycles [ 55 ].

Polymeric and composite materials introduce structural flexibility, mechanical stability, and processability, making them suitable for large-scale and continuous remediation systems [ 56 ]. Their performance is largely dictated by macromolecular architecture, functional group chemistry, and interfacial interactions. Biopolymers and biodegradable materials are derived from renewable resources such as cellulose, chitosan, alginate, and starch [ 57 ]. Conceptually, these materials align with sustainability principles by offering low toxicity, biodegradability, and environmental compatibility [ 58 ]. Their abundant functional groups enable effective binding of metal ions and organic pollutants through chelation, hydrogen bonding, and electrostatic interactions [ 59 ]. Biopolymers are particularly attractive for low-cost, decentralized treatment systems and for applications where environmental safety is a primary concern [ 60 ]. Polymer-nanocomposites combine the functional advantages of nanomaterials with the structural integrity of polymer matrices [ 61 ]. In these systems, nanofillers such as metal oxides, carbon nanomaterials, or clay nanoparticles are dispersed within polymers to create synergistic effects [ 62 ]. Polymer-nanocomposites exploit interfacial interactions between phases to enhance adsorption capacity, catalytic activity, mechanical strength, and resistance to fouling. This hybrid approach enables the translation of nanoscale reactivity into macroscale, application-ready materials [ 63 ]. Functionalized membranes represent a critical class of polymeric materials for separation-based remediation [ 64 ]. By incorporating functional groups, nanoparticles, or responsive moieties into membrane matrices, these materials achieve selective permeability and resistance to fouling. Functionalized membranes operate through a combination of size exclusion, electrostatic interactions, and surface chemistry, enabling precise control over solute transport while maintaining high water flux and operational stability [ 65 ]. Polymeric and composite materials also face challenges including membrane fouling, mechanical instability, limited long-term durability, and difficulties associated with homogeneous nanofiller dispersion and production cost [ 66 ].

Advanced functional materials are distinguished by their highly ordered structures, tunable porosity, and programmable chemical environments, enabling unprecedented control over pollutant interactions [ 67 ]. Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) are crystalline materials composed of metal nodes coordinated to organic linkers, forming highly porous networks with exceptionally high surface areas [ 68 ]. MOFs function as molecular scaffolds where pore size, shape, and chemical functionality can be tailored for specific contaminants [ 69 ]. Their modular design allows selective adsorption, catalytic degradation, and even sensing of pollutants [ 70 ]. However, challenges related to water stability and scalability continue to guide research toward robust and environmentally resilient MOF architectures. The Covalent organic frameworks (COFs) are entirely organic, crystalline porous materials linked by strong covalent bonds [ 71 ]. Compared to MOFs, COFs offer enhanced chemical stability and lower metal-related toxicity. Their ordered π-conjugated frameworks enable selective adsorption and photocatalytic activity, particularly for organic pollutants [ 72 ].The ability to design COFs with precise pore environments and functional groups makes them promising candidates for next-generation, metal-free remediation technologies [ 73 ]. Hybrid and smart materials represent an emerging class that integrates multiple functionalities and responsive behaviors into a single system [ 74 ]. These materials can adapt to environmental stimuli such as pH, light, temperature, or redox conditions, enabling controlled adsorption, on-demand regeneration, or self-cleaning behavior [ 75 ]. Metal organic frameworks (MOFs) and covalent organic frameworks (COFs) are constrained by hydrolytic instability, solvent-intensive synthesis procedures, scalability limitations, and potential structural degradation under realistic wastewater treatment conditions, which collectively affect their economic feasibility for industrial applications. Smart and hybrid materials, despite their multifunctionality and adaptive behavior, are often associated with complex design requirements, higher fabrication costs, and operational challenges related to process integration and real-world system control [ 76 ]. Hybrid and smart materials embody a system-level approach, where remediation is dynamically regulated rather than passively applied, paving the way for intelligent and autonomous environmental treatment technologies [ 77 ]. COMPARISON OF DIFFERENT MATERIAL CLASSES
Advanced remediation materials exhibit distinct advantages and limitations depending on their physicochemical properties, operational conditions, and environmental applications [ 78 ]. Nanomaterials, including metal oxides, graphene derivatives, and carbon nanotubes, generally demonstrate exceptionally high adsorption capacity, catalytic activity, and rapid reaction kinetics due to their large surface area and abundant active sites [ 79 ]. However, their practical application is often constrained by nanoparticle aggregation, difficult post-treatment recovery, possible ecotoxicity, and high synthesis costs, particularly for engineered carbon nanostructures [ 80 ]. MOFs possess ultra-high porosity, variable pore architecture, and excellent selectivity toward specific pollutants, making them highly effective for adsorption and catalytic degradation of dyes, pharmaceuticals, and heavy metals [ 81 ]. Nevertheless, many MOFs suffer from hydrolytic instability, solvent-intensive synthesis, limited long-term durability under realistic wastewater conditions, and scalability challenges that hinder industrial implementation. COFs offer advantages over MOFs in terms of improved chemical stability, lower metal-associated toxicity, lightweight structures, and highly ordered porous frameworks suitable for selective adsorption and photocatalysis [ 82 ]. Despite these advantages, COFs still face limitations associated with complex synthesis procedures, lower crystallinity control, and relatively high production cost.

Biochar and biopolymeric materials such as cellulose, chitosan, and alginate are considered environmentally sustainable alternatives because of their biodegradability, low toxicity, renewable origin, and relatively low production cost [ 83 , 84 ]. Biochar also enables carbon sequestration and waste valorization. However, these materials generally exhibit lower selectivity, reduced adsorption capacity for certain emerging contaminants, and variability in physicochemical properties depending on feedstock source and preparation conditions [ 85 ]. Functionalized membranes and polymeric nanocomposites provide excellent separation efficiency, continuous operation capability, and high-water flux, particularly in desalination and wastewater reuse systems [ 86 ]. Their performance can be further enhanced through nanoparticle incorporation and surface modification. However, membrane fouling, limited lifespan, energy consumption, and difficulties associated with membrane regeneration remain major operational challenges [ 87 ]. Hybrid and smart materials combine multiple functionalities such as adsorption, catalysis, sensing, and self-cleaning behavior within a single platform, enabling adaptive and multifunctional remediation systems [ 88 ]. Although these systems exhibit superior performance and operational flexibility, they are often associated with higher fabrication complexity, increased cost, and limited large-scale validation. From a sustainability perspective, biochar and biodegradable polymeric materials are generally more environmentally benign and economically feasible for large-scale deployment, whereas MOFs, COFs, and engineered nanomaterials offer superior selectivity and remediation efficiency but require improvements in green synthesis, recyclability, and life-cycle sustainability [ 89 , 90 ]. Therefore, no single material class can universally satisfy all remediation requirements, and future development should focus on designing multifunctional, scalable, low-cost, and environmentally safe hybrid systems that balance remediation efficiency with sustainability and practical applicability. Recent studies have demonstrated the significant potential of advanced functional materials such as hydrogels, Nano catalysts, aerogels, xerogels, and hybrid nanostructures for sustainable environmental remediation [ 91 , 92 ]. Green hydrogels and hydrogel nanospheres showed efficient adsorption of dyes and heavy metals due to enhanced surface functionality and porosity, while Nano catalysts exhibited improved catalytic activity and recyclability through structural engineering and hybrid architectures [ 93 ]. Similarly, aerogel- and xerogel-based materials attracted attention because of their ultra-high porosity, low density, and high adsorption capacity for wastewater treatment [ 94 ]. These studies collectively highlight that material morphology, surface chemistry, and porous structure strongly influence remediation efficiency, regeneration, and sustainability.

ADVANCED MATERIALS-BASED TECHNOLOGIES FOR WATER AND WASTEWATER TREATMENT
The increasing chemical complexity of wastewater arising from industrial effluents, emerging contaminants, and variable operating conditions has necessitated a shift from conventional treatment methods toward material-driven, mechanism-based technologies. Advanced materials are now designed not only to remove contaminants efficiently but also to operate through well-defined physicochemical and catalytic mechanisms that enable selectivity, stability, and sustainability [ 95 ]. Adsorption is a surface-controlled phenomenon in which contaminants are transferred from the aqueous phase to the solid phase via physical or chemical interactions [ 96 ]. Advanced materials have transformed adsorption from a passive process into a tunable, selective, and regenerable remediation strategy.

MECHANISTIC BASIS OF DYE, HEAVY METAL, AND PHARMACEUTICAL REMOVAL
A combination of electrostatic attraction governs dye adsorption mechanisms, π-π stacking, hydrogen bonding, and pore diffusion [ 97 ]. Many synthetic dyes possess aromatic rings and charged functional groups, making them highly responsive to surface chemistry [ 98 ]. Carbon-based materials such as graphene oxide and biochar enable π-π electron donor-acceptor interactions between conjugated dye molecules and sp 2 -hybridized carbon domains. Electrostatic attraction becomes dominant when oppositely charged dye molecules interact with functionalized adsorbent surfaces, with adsorption strongly influenced by solution pH and ionic strength [ 99 ]. Heavy metal adsorption is fundamentally different from organic pollutant removal, as metals cannot be degraded and must be immobilized or extracted [ 100 ]. Advanced materials remove metal ions primarily through surface complexation, ion exchange, and chelation [ 101 ]. Functional groups such as -NH₂, -COOH, -SH, and -OH act as coordination sites, forming inner-sphere or outer-sphere complexes with metal ions. Chitosan and metal oxide surfaces bind Pb 2 ⁺ and Cd 2 ⁺ through ligand-metal coordination, while iron-based materials can induce redox transformation, such as the reduction of Cr(VI) to Cr(III), followed by precipitation or adsorption [ 102 , 103 ]. Pharmaceutical adsorption mechanisms are more complex due to molecular diversity, polarity, and persistence at trace concentrations [ 104 ]. Advanced porous materials such as MOFs, COFs, and tailored activated carbons rely on size-selective pore confinement, hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and electrostatic forces [ 67 ]. Molecular sieving plays a key role in selectively adsorbing pharmaceuticals based on molecular dimensions and functional groups, while surface polarity tuning enhances selective affinity [ 105 ]. Adsorption kinetics is often described using pseudo-first order and pseudo-second-order models, while equilibrium behavior is interpreted using Langmuir, Freundlich, and Sips isotherms [ 106 ]. However, future work increasingly emphasizes molecular-level understanding using spectroscopic and computational tools to elucidate binding mechanisms. SURFACE MODIFICATION STRATEGIES: MECHANISTIC ENHANCEMENT
Surface modification is a central strategy for controlling adsorption mechanisms and enhancing selectivity [ 116 ]. Chemical functionalization introduces specific binding sites that alter surface charge, polarity, and coordination behavior [ 117 ]. Sulfur-functionalized materials exhibit enhanced affinity toward soft metal ions such as Hg 2 ⁺, while nitrogen-doped carbons improve adsorption of aromatic organic compounds through enhanced electron density [ 118 ]. Hybridization with magnetic nanoparticles introduces a secondary mechanism magnetic separation allowing rapid recovery of spent adsorbents without filtration [ 119 ]. Additionally, surface grafting of polymers can improve hydrophilicity and prevent fouling, maintaining active site accessibility. Surface modification alters the energy landscape of adsorption by lowering activation energy barriers and increasing adsorption site density [ 120 ]. Unlike adsorption, catalytic technologies actively transform pollutants through redox reactions. Photocatalytic and electrocatalytic processes are governed by charge transfer, radical formation, and surface reaction mechanisms that enable complete mineralization of organic contaminants [ 132 ].

PHOTOCATALYTIC DEGRADATION MECHANISMS OF ORGANIC POLLUTANTS
Advanced photocatalytic materials improve performance primarily through band-structure engineering, heterojunction construction, surface functionalization, and defect or dopant introduction [ 133 ]. For instance, heterostructure photocatalysts (e.g., Z-scheme or S-scheme systems) promote efficient separation of photogenerated electron-hole pairs, thereby increasing the lifetime and availability of reactive species such as ·OH, ·O₂⁻, and h⁺ for pollutant oxidation [ 134 ]. Bandgap tuning through metal or non-metal doping extends light absorption into the visible range, enhancing solar utilization and reaction kinetics [ 135 ]. Surface modification with cocatalysts or oxygen-containing functional groups improves pollutant adsorption near active sites, facilitating interfacial charge transfer and accelerating degradation pathways [ 136 ]. Importantly, photocatalytic degradation does not inherently guarantee detoxification or complete mineralization; partial oxidation can generate intermediate compounds that may exhibit equal or greater toxicity than the parent pollutants. Recent studies emphasize the need to track degradation pathways, intermediate formation, and total organic carbon (TOC) removal rather than relying solely on apparent pollutant disappearance. According to [ 137 , 138 ]these works demonstrate that rational photocatalyst design can steer reaction pathways toward deeper oxidation and mineralization while suppressing the accumulation of hazardous intermediates, underscoring the necessity of coupling material innovation with comprehensive toxicity and pathway analysis for realistic environmental applications.

Type-II heterojunctions are described as systems formed between two semiconductors possessing staggered band structures [ 139 ] where photogenerated electrons migrate from the conduction band (CB) of one semiconductor to the lower CB of the second semiconductor, while holes transfer in the opposite direction between valence bands (VBs) [ 140 ]. This spatial separation significantly suppresses electron-hole recombination and enhances charge carrier lifetime. However, the migration of electrons and holes to lower-energy bands often reduce the overall redox potential of the system, thereby limiting the generation of highly reactive oxidative and reductive species required for complete pollutant mineralization. To address this limitation, explains Z-scheme heterojunction systems, which mimic the natural photosynthetic Z-scheme electron transfer pathway. In these systems, electrons from the CB of one semiconductor recombine with holes from the VB of another semiconductor, preserving the highly oxidative holes and strongly reductive electrons on opposite semiconductors [ 141 ]. As a result, Z-scheme photocatalysts exhibit enhanced redox capability, improved generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and superior degradation efficiency toward recalcitrant organic pollutants [ 142 ]. The study also distinguishes between traditional mediator-based Z-schemes and direct Z-scheme systems, emphasizing that direct interfacial charge transfer improves stability and minimizes energy loss [ 143 ]. Furthermore, an explanation of S-scheme heterojunctions, which represent a recently developed charge transfer mechanism designed to combine efficient charge separation with strong redox ability [ 144 ]. In S-scheme systems, internal electric fields and band bending at the heterointerface selectively retain electrons with high reduction potential and holes with high oxidation potential while facilitating recombination of low-energy charge carriers [ 145 ]. Compared to conventional type-II heterojunctions, S-scheme architectures maintain stronger photocatalytic oxidation and reduction capacity while simultaneously suppressing recombination losses [ 146 ]. Their enhanced interfacial charge dynamics and efficient utilization of visible light make them highly promising for environmental remediation applications. Study also discusses Schottky junctions, which are formed at metal-semiconductor interfaces [ 147 ]. Due to differences in Fermi energy levels, electrons migrate from the semiconductor to the metal until equilibrium is reached, creating a Schottky barrier [ 148 ]. Noble metals such as Ag, Au, and Pt function as electron sinks, trapping photogenerated electrons and preventing electron-hole recombination [ 149 ]. This mechanism improves interfacial charge separation and prolongs carrier lifetime, thereby enhancing photocatalytic degradation reactions. Additionally, surface plasmon resonance effects in certain noble metals can broaden visible-light absorption and improve photocatalytic efficiency under solar irradiation [ 150 ]. Finally, the section now includes a discussion of p-n junction heterostructures formed between p-type and n-type semiconductors [ 151 ]. Upon contact, charge redistribution occurs across the interface, generating an internal electric field that drives directional migration of photogenerated electrons and holes [ 152 ]. This built-in electric field facilitates rapid charge separation and suppresses recombination, thereby improving photocatalytic activity and stability. p-n junction systems are particularly effective in enhancing visible-light-driven photocatalysis and have shown strong potential for degradation of dyes, pharmaceuticals, and emerging contaminants.

ELECTROCATALYTIC PROCESSES AND REACTION PATHWAYS
Electrocatalytic remediation relies on externally applied potential to drive oxidation or reduction reactions at electrode surfaces [ 19 ]. Organic pollutants are degraded either directly through electron transfer or indirectly via electrogenerated oxidants such as hydroxyl radicals, ozone, or active chlorine species [ 153 ]. Mechanistically, electrocatalytic degradation is governed by electrode material composition and surface structure, applied potential and current density, mass transfer and diffusion of pollutants to reactive sites. Advanced materials such as doped carbon electrodes, metal oxide coatings, and conductive polymer composites enhance electron transfer efficiency and catalytic stability [ 154 ]. Research trends emphasize selective electrocatalysis, aiming to control reaction pathways and minimize by-product formation.

SOLAR-DRIVEN REMEDIATION SYSTEMS
Solar-driven systems integrate photocatalysis with renewable energy utilization, enabling sustainable and decentralized wastewater treatment. These systems rely on visible-light-active materials capable of efficient photon absorption and charge utilization under natural sunlight [ 155 ]. Hybrid systems that combine adsorption and photocatalysis exhibit synergistic mechanisms. Pollutants are first concentrated at the material surface through adsorption, followed by localized photocatalytic degradation [ 156 ]. This approach improves reaction kinetics and reduces catalyst deactivation, representing a major research direction for scalable solar remediation technologies.

Transport phenomena, interfacial interactions, and material surface chemistry govern membrane separation. Advanced membranes enable precise control over mass transfer while addressing challenges such as fouling and energy consumption [ 157 ].

Nanofiltration and reverse osmosis membranes operate through a combination of size exclusion, electrostatic repulsion and solution-diffusion mechanisms [ 158 ]. Advanced materials enhance these mechanisms by tailoring pore size distribution, surface charge, and hydrophilicity [ 159 ]. Nanomaterial-incorporated membranes create preferential water transport pathways, increasing permeability without sacrificing selectivity [ 160 ]. Interfacial water-membrane interactions and nano-scale transport channels play a critical role in determining flux and rejection behavior [ 161 ].

ANTIFOULING AND SELF-CLEANING MECHANISMS
Membrane fouling arises from adsorption and deposition of organic matter, microorganisms, and inorganic salts [ 162 ]. Advanced antifouling membranes are designed to minimize foulant adhesion through surface hydration layers, electrostatic repulsion, and antimicrobial activity [ 163 ]. Self-cleaning membranes introduce active mechanisms such as photocatalytic degradation of foulants under light irradiation or stimulus-responsive surface restructuring [ 131 ]. These materials continuously regenerate active surfaces, reducing the need for chemical cleaning and extending membrane lifespan.

The remediation performance of advanced materials is strongly governed by pollutant-specific interaction mechanisms and environmental operating conditions. Electrostatic attraction predominantly influences the adsorption of charged contaminants such as dyes, pharmaceuticals, and heavy metal ions, where solution pH and ionic strength significantly alter surface charge and adsorption efficiency. π-π interactions are particularly important for the removal of aromatic organic pollutants, including polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons and antibiotic residues, through interactions between conjugated carbon structures and aromatic contaminant molecules. Hydrogen bonding contributes to the adsorption of polar compounds containing hydroxyl, amino, or carboxyl functional groups, whereas pore diffusion controls the transport and accessibility of contaminants within porous adsorbent structures such as MOFs, COFs, and activated biochar. Surface complexation and chelation mechanisms play dominant roles in heavy metal sequestration through coordination interactions with oxygen-, nitrogen-, or sulfur-containing functional groups. In contrast, redox transformation mechanisms are crucial for degrading persistent organic pollutants and reducing toxic metal ions into less hazardous forms. However, under realistic environmental conditions, factors such as competing ions, natural organic matter, fluctuating pH, salinity, and complex wastewater matrices may significantly influence these interaction pathways, thereby affecting remediation efficiency, selectivity, and long-term material stability.

MATERIALS FOR ENVIRONMENTAL REMEDIATION
The complexity and diversity of environmental contaminants demand remediation strategies that are both mechanism-driven and adaptable to varying chemical structures, concentrations, and environmental matrices. Advanced materials have emerged as powerful tools for environmental remediation by enabling selective adsorption, catalytic transformation, immobilization, and separation of contaminants [ 164 ]. In Table 2 the uses of advanced materials are listed for environmental remediation, these advanced materials are used for the removal of organic pollutants, heavy metals and metalloids and other emerging contaminants.

Organic pollutants constitute one of the most pervasive classes of environmental contaminants, originating from industrial effluents, agricultural runoff, domestic wastewater, and pharmaceutical residues [ 165 ]. Their persistence, toxicity, and resistance to conventional treatment methods necessitate the use of advanced material-based remediation strategies. Dyes, widely used in textile, paper, and leather industries, are characterized by complex aromatic structures and chromophore groups that impart chemical stability and recalcitrance [ 166 , 167 ]. Advanced materials such as carbon-based nanomaterials, functionalized polymers, and porous frameworks remove dyes primarily through adsorption mechanisms involving π-π stacking, electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and pore confinement [ 168 ]. In parallel, photocatalytic materials enable oxidative degradation of dye molecules into smaller, less toxic intermediates and, ultimately, mineralized products [ 169 ]. Hybrid adsorption-photocatalysis systems are particularly effective, as adsorption concentrates dyes on catalyst surfaces, enhancing degradation kinetics [ 170 ] . Pesticides including organochlorines, organophosphates, and carbamates, pose significant risks due to their persistence and bioaccumulation. Remediation materials target pesticides through hydrophobic interactions, surface complexation, and catalytic transformation [ 171 ]. Porous carbon materials and polymer-nanocomposites adsorb pesticides efficiently, while photocatalytic and electrocatalytic materials promote bond cleavage and detoxification [ 172 ]. Pesticide remediation increasingly focuses on selective degradation pathways that minimize the formation of toxic by-products [ 173 ]. Pharmaceuticals and personal care products (PPCPs) represent a rapidly growing class of micropollutants. Their diverse molecular structures and biological activity at trace concentrations challenge conventional treatment processes [ 174 ]. Advanced materials such as MOFs, COFs, and tailored membranes remove pharmaceuticals through size-selective adsorption, hydrogen bonding, and electrostatic interactions. Advanced oxidation processes (AOPs), supported by photocatalysts and electrocatalysts, enable complete mineralization or transformation into less active compounds [ 175 ]. Research increasingly emphasizes understanding transformation pathways and identifying potentially harmful intermediates using high-resolution analytical techniques.

Heavy metals and metalloids, including lead, cadmium, mercury, chromium, arsenic, and selenium, are non-biodegradable and persist indefinitely in the environment. Their remediation relies on removal, immobilization, or transformation rather than destruction [ 176 ]. Adsorption mechanisms dominate metal remediation strategies and are governed by surface complexation, ion exchange, chelation, and electrostatic attraction [ 177 ]. Functionalized materials containing oxygen-, nitrogen-, sulfur-, or phosphorus-based groups form strong coordination bonds with metal ions [ 178 ]. Biopolymers such as chitosan and alginate exhibit high affinity for metal ions through chelation. In contrast, metal oxides and hydroxides provide reactive surfaces for inner-sphere complex formation [ 179 ]. Immobilization strategies aim to reduce metal mobility and bioavailability rather than complete removal. Materials such as biochar, clay minerals, and phosphate-based composites stabilize metals through precipitation, encapsulation, or redox-induced transformation [ 180 ]. For instance, iron-based materials can reduce toxic Cr(VI) to the less mobile Cr(III), followed by adsorption or precipitation[ 181 ]. Immobilization is particularly relevant for soil and sediment remediation, where complete removal may be impractical. Advanced techniques, including magnetic separation, electrokinetic remediation, and membrane-based metal recovery, further expand the toolkit for metal remediation. Magnetic nanocomposites allow rapid separation and regeneration, while electrochemical systems enable selective metal recovery and resource reclamation, aligning remediation with circular economy principles [ 182 ].

Emerging contaminants encompass a broad range of substances that are not fully regulated but pose potential risks to ecosystems and human health [ 183 ]. Their structural diversity and persistence demand innovative, material-driven remediation approaches. Microplastics and nanoplastics are ubiquitous pollutants originating from plastic degradation and synthetic textiles [ 184 ]. Advanced materials address microplastic pollution through filtration, adsorption, and magnetic separation [ 185 ]. Functionalized membranes and fibrous filters physically retain microplastics in contrast, surface-engineered materials promote aggregation and capture. Research is increasingly exploring photocatalytic and enzymatic materials capable of degrading plastic polymers into benign by-products [ 186 ]. Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) are highly persistent due to strong carbon-fluorine bonds. Their remediation represents a major challenge, as conventional treatment methods are largely ineffective [ 187 ]. Advanced materials such as tailored adsorbents, ion-exchange resins, and high-surface-area carbons capture PFAS through hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions [ 188 ]. Emerging approaches integrate adsorption with electrochemical or plasma-based degradation to break C-F bonds, reflecting a shift toward destructive remediation strategies. Endocrine-disrupting compounds (EDCs), including hormones, plasticizers, and certain pharmaceuticals, interfere with biological signaling even at trace concentrations [ 189 ]. Advanced porous materials and membranes remove EDCs through selective adsorption and size exclusion, while photocatalytic materials enable oxidative degradation [ 190 ]. Conceptually, the remediation of EDCs emphasizes sensitivity and selectivity, given their activity at ultra-low concentrations. Additional emerging contaminants include antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs), nanoparticles, disinfection by-products, volatile organic compounds (VOCs), and cyanotoxins [ 191 ]. Advanced materials such as antimicrobial membranes, redox-active catalysts, and multifunctional adsorbents are being developed to address these challenges through combined physical, chemical, and biological mechanisms [ 192 ].

Beyond individual material classes, modern remediation increasingly relies on integrated techniques that combine multiple mechanisms within a single system. Examples include adsorption-photocatalysis hybrids, membrane-bioreactor systems, electrochemical-adsorptive processes, and nature-based material integrations. Conceptually, these approaches aim to maximize treatment efficiency, reduce secondary pollution, and enhance system resilience under variable environmental conditions [ 193 ].

INTEGRATION OF AI AND SMART TECHNOLOGIES
The convergence of advanced materials with artificial intelligence (AI), machine learning (ML), and smart sensing technologies is redefining environmental remediation strategies. These digital tools accelerate material discovery, optimize treatment processes, and enable real-time monitoring, thereby enhancing efficiency, adaptability, and sustainability [ 194 ].

AI-driven approaches enable rapid screening and rational design of advanced materials by predicting structure, property, performance relationships [ 195 ]. Machine learning algorithms analyze large datasets of material compositions, synthesis parameters, and remediation performance to identify optimal material architectures with minimal experimental effort [ 196 ]. In environmental remediation, AI has been increasingly applied to predict adsorption capacity and selectivity of porous materials such as MOFs, COFs, and biochar, optimize photocatalyst bandgaps and charge separation efficiency, guide surface functionalization strategies for enhanced contaminant affinity [ 197 ]. These approaches significantly reduce trial-and-error experimentation, lower development costs, and accelerate translation from laboratory-scale discovery to practical applications.

AI-based process modeling enables real-time optimization of water and wastewater treatment systems [ 198 ]. Data-driven models integrate operational parameters such as pH, temperature, pollutant concentration, hydraulic retention time, and material dosage to optimize treatment efficiency and energy consumption [ 199 ]. Digital twins and predictive models facilitate adaptive control of treatment processes under fluctuating wastewater composition, optimization of regeneration cycles and material lifespan, and reduction of chemical and energy inputs. Such intelligent process control is particularly valuable for decentralized treatment systems and industrial wastewater management [ 200 ].

Machine learning models have emerged as powerful tools for predicting contaminant removal performance across diverse materials and environmental conditions [ 201 ]. Supervised learning algorithms, including random forests, neural networks, and support vector machines, are used to forecast adsorption kinetics, degradation efficiency, and fouling behavior [ 202 ]. ML-based predictive tools enable rapid assessment of treatment feasibility for emerging contaminants, risk-based prioritization of remediation strategies, and design of multi-contaminant treatment systems. The integration of ML with experimental and field data enhances decision-making and supports scalable deployment of advanced remediation technologies [ 203 ].The use of machine learning algorithms such as artificial neural networks (ANN), random forest (RF), support vector machine (SVM), gradient boosting, and deep learning models for predicting adsorption capacity, photocatalytic degradation efficiency, membrane fouling behavior, and contaminant removal performance[ 204 ]. Specific examples have been added showing how AI models have been used to optimize MOF and COF structures for selective adsorption, predict bandgap and charge-transfer properties of photocatalysts, and model structure-property-performance relationships in nanocomposite systems. In addition, the datasets and data-driven approaches are used for remediation process optimization, including adsorption isotherm databases, photocatalytic degradation datasets, sensor-generated water quality data, and real-time operational datasets from wastewater treatment systems. The study incorporated recent case studies demonstrating AI-assisted optimization of wastewater treatment parameters, predictive contaminant removal modeling, smart sensing integration, and digital twin approaches for adaptive remediation process control [ 205 , 206 ].

Smart sensing systems incorporating advanced materials enable real-time detection and monitoring of contaminants [ 207 ]. Nano sensors, electrochemical sensors, and optical sensors functionalized with selective materials provide high sensitivity and specificity toward pollutants such as heavy metals, pesticides, and endocrine disruptors [ 208 ]. When integrated with wireless communication and AI-based analytics, smart monitoring systems enable early detection of contamination events, automated feedback control for treatment systems, long-term environmental surveillance. These technologies are critical for ensuring regulatory compliance, protecting public health, and enabling adaptive water management [ 209 ].

The overall objective of integrating AI and ML into environmental remediation is to develop more efficient, cost-effective, and sustainable remediation technologies. However, despite significant progress, several limitations remain associated with AI-assisted remediation systems. Effective training of AI and ML models requires high-quality, comprehensive, and standardized datasets, as incomplete, inconsistent, or biased data can result in inaccurate predictions and unreliable model performance [ 205 ].

SUSTAINABILITY, TOXICITY, AND ENVIRONMENTAL IMPACT ASSESSMENT
Life cycle assessment provides a holistic evaluation of environmental impacts associated with material production, use, and disposal [ 210 ]. LCA frameworks assess energy consumption, greenhouse gas emissions, water use, and resource depletion across the material life cycle [ 211 ]. For remediation materials, LCA highlights trade-offs between high performance and environmental cost, guiding the development of materials with lower carbon footprint, energy-efficient synthesis, and extended service life [ 212 ].

Nanomaterials and chemically functionalized materials may pose ecotoxicological risks if released into the environment. Potential concerns include bioaccumulation, toxicity to aquatic organisms, and disruption of microbial communities. Systematic ecotoxicity assessments, including acute and chronic toxicity studies, are essential to ensure material safety. Surface modification, immobilization, and encapsulation strategies are increasingly employed to mitigate environmental risks [ 213 ].The potential environmental and biological risks associated with nanomaterials, including nanoparticle leaching, persistence in aquatic and soil environments, bioaccumulation, trophic transfer, oxidative stress generation, and possible toxicity toward microorganisms, aquatic organisms, plants, and human health [ 214 ]. Particular attention has been given to the post-treatment fate of nanomaterials after remediation processes, including aggregation, dissolution, surface transformation, sedimentation, and release of metal ions or reactive intermediates under environmental conditions [ 215 ]. Material recovery and containment remain major challenges for nanoscale remediation systems, especially under realistic wastewater conditions. Strategies such as immobilization of nanoparticles onto supports, magnetic recovery, encapsulation, surface coating, and development of biodegradable or bio-derived nanomaterials approaches to minimize environmental release and improve operational safety [ 216 ].

Material recyclability and regeneration are critical for sustainable remediation. Advanced materials are increasingly designed for multiple use cycles without significant loss of performance [ 217 ]. Magnetic separation, chemical regeneration, and thermal treatment enable efficient recovery and reuse of adsorbents and catalysts. Recyclable materials reduce operational costs, minimize secondary waste, and support circular economy principles [ 218 ].

Green synthesis strategies aim to minimize environmental impact during material fabrication by using renewable feedstocks, non-toxic solvents, low-temperature processes, and waste-derived precursors [ 219 ]. Biochar, biopolymer composites, and biologically synthesized nanoparticles exemplify sustainable material design [ 220 ]. The adoption of green chemistry principles ensures that remediation technologies contribute positively to environmental sustainability throughout their life cycle.

CHALLENGES, KNOWLEDGE GAPS, AND FUTURE PERSPECTIVES
Despite substantial progress in the development of next-generation materials for environmental remediation and sustainable development, several scientific, technological, and socio-economic challenges continue to hinder their large-scale deployment. Addressing these challenges requires a holistic understanding of material behavior under real-world conditions, integration of sustainability principles, and coordinated efforts among researchers, industry stakeholders, and policymakers [ 264 ]. In contrast, knowledge gaps discussion specifically highlights unresolved scientific questions and limitations in current understanding. These include insufficient mechanistic understanding of contaminant-material interactions at molecular and interfacial levels, limited knowledge of long-term environmental fate, transformation pathways, and ecotoxicological impacts of advanced materials, lack of standardized methodologies for evaluating photocatalytic mineralization and toxicity of degradation intermediates, inadequate understanding of material behavior under realistic multi-contaminant and variable environmental conditions, and limited predictive models correlating material structure, surface chemistry, and remediation performance.

One of the most significant barriers to the practical implementation of advanced remediation materials is scalability [ 265 ]. Many high-performance materials such as MOFs, COFs, engineered nanomaterials, and multifunctional composites are typically synthesized using complex, multi-step laboratory procedures that rely on high-purity reagents, organic solvents, and energy-intensive conditions. These factors significantly increase production costs and limit feasibility for large-scale or decentralized applications [ 266 ]. In addition, material performance demonstrated under controlled laboratory conditions often declines when applied to real wastewater matrices containing competing ions, organic matter, and fluctuating physicochemical parameters [ 267 ]. This discrepancy highlights the need for scalable synthesis methods that ensure consistent material quality and performance at industrial scale [ 268 ]. Future efforts must focus on developing low-cost, high-throughput synthesis routes using abundant or waste-derived raw materials, simplifying material architectures without compromising functionality, conducting pilot-scale and field-scale demonstrations to validate economic and operational feasibility.

The long-term stability of advanced materials under operational and environmental conditions remains poorly understood. Many materials exhibit excellent short-term performance but suffer from degradation, fouling, leaching of active components, or loss of structural integrity over extended use [ 269 ]. Environmental factors such as pH fluctuations, temperature variation, salinity, and exposure to natural organic matter can significantly affect material durability [ 270 ]. Equally important is the environmental fate of materials after use. Nanomaterials and chemically functionalized materials may persist in the environment if not properly recovered, raising concerns about accumulation, toxicity, and unintended ecological impacts [ 271 ]. Knowledge gaps remain regarding long-term transformation and degradation pathways of materials in aquatic and soil environments, bioavailability and trophic transfer of released material components, interactions with microbial communities and ecosystem processes, addressing these gaps require systematic long-term studies, standardized aging protocols, and integration of environmental fate modeling with experimental validation.

The absence of clear regulatory frameworks and standardized testing protocols poses a major challenge to commercialization. Many advanced materials fall outside existing regulatory categories, leading to uncertainty in approval processes for environmental deployment. Regulatory agencies often require extensive data on material safety, performance, and environmental impact, which may not be readily available for novel materials [ 272 ]. Furthermore, the transition from laboratory research to market-ready technologies is hindered by limited industry-academia collaboration, high initial investment costs and perceived technological risk, lack of incentives for adopting innovative remediation technologies. Bridging of this gap requires early engagement with regulatory bodies, development of standardized performance metrics, and demonstration of long-term safety and cost-effectiveness. Public-private partnerships and policy-driven innovation incentives can play a crucial role in accelerating commercialization [ 273 ].

Future research aimed at overcoming the current limitations of next-generation materials for environmental remediation must adopt a multidisciplinary, application-driven, and systems-oriented approach to enable large-scale deployment and real-world impact. A central priority is the design of materials with scalability and sustainability in mind, emphasizing green and low-energy synthesis routes, modular and cost-effective fabrication strategies, and life-cycle optimization to minimize environmental footprints while maintaining performance. The development of multifunctional and hybrid materials that seamlessly integrate adsorption, catalytic degradation, sensing, and in situ regeneration within a single platform represents another critical direction, as such systems can significantly enhance treatment efficiency and operational flexibility. In parallel, AI- and data-driven innovation is expected to play a transformative role by accelerating material discovery, enabling structure-property-performance prediction, supporting digital twin development, and optimizing treatment processes under dynamic conditions. To bridge the gap between laboratory success and practical implementation, future studies must prioritize real-world validation through pilot-scale demonstrations, long-term field trials, and performance monitoring in complex environmental matrices. Equally important is the adoption of safety-by-design strategies, wherein ecotoxicity, human health risks, and environmental fate are assessed at early stages of material development to ensure regulatory compliance and public acceptance. Finally, system-level integration of advanced materials with biological processes, electrochemical technologies, and nature-based solutions will be essential for developing holistic, resilient, and sustainable remediation frameworks capable of addressing the multifaceted challenges of environmental pollution.

CONCLUSIONS
Advanced materials have emerged as promising solutions for sustainable environmental remediation because of their tunable surface chemistry, multifunctionality, high adsorption capacity, and catalytic efficiency. This review highlighted the applications of nanomaterials, polymeric composites, metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), covalent organic frameworks (COFs), advanced membranes, photocatalysts, electrocatalysts, and smart hybrid materials for the removal of dyes, heavy metals, pharmaceuticals, PFAS, microplastics, and other emerging contaminants. Mechanistic interactions including electrostatic attraction, π-π interactions, hydrogen bonding, surface complexation, redox transformation, photocatalytic charge transfer, and membrane separation were critically discussed to establish structure-property-function relationships governing remediation performance. The integration of artificial intelligence, machine learning, smart sensing technologies, green synthesis approaches, and life-cycle assessment further demonstrates the transition toward intelligent and sustainable remediation systems.

Several key conclusions emerge from this review. First, no single material can universally address all remediation challenges, highlighting the importance of multifunctional and hybrid systems integrating adsorption, catalysis, sensing, and regeneration. Second, sustainability considerations such as recyclability, low energy demand, renewable feedstocks, and reduced secondary pollution are becoming equally important as treatment efficiency. Third, AI-assisted material design and predictive modeling offer significant potential for accelerating material discovery and process optimization. Despite remarkable progress, important research gaps remain regarding long-term stability, fouling behavior, ecotoxicity, regeneration efficiency, and environmental fate of advanced materials under realistic conditions. Furthermore, scalability, synthesis cost, nanoparticle recovery, and limited pilot-scale validation continue to restrict practical implementation. Future research should therefore prioritize green and scalable synthesis, recyclable and biodegradable materials, techno-economic assessment, life-cycle analysis, and integration with AI-driven and renewable-energy-assisted treatment systems to develop sustainable, cost-effective, and resilient environmental remediation technologies aligned with global Sustainable Development Goals.
