ABSTRACT
Abstract Vitamins play a vital role in organic transformations, particularly in the field of organocatalysis. Vitamins have been found to function as photoredox catalysts in an array of synthetic processes, supporting advancements in the realm of photocatalysis over the past few decades. Several applications of vitamins as photoredox catalysts are covered in this article. Furthermore, the mechanistic aspects that provide insights into the functions of vitamins in significant chemical transformations are thoroughly discussed, offering an understanding of reaction pathways and strengthening the applications of vitamins in organocatalysis methods.

INTRODUCTION
Vitamins play an essential role in biological processes, ensuring the proper function of metabolic pathways. Beyond this well-established function, a fascinating avenue of exploration is the potential of vitamins to act as catalysts in organic synthesis. 1 In the past, vitamins have been reported to function as coenzymes, which are organic compounds that aid in enzyme-catalysed chemical reactions. 2 Vitamins are small, photoactive, and sustainable organic compounds with a number of functional groups, such as the hydroxyl, amino, and carboxyl groups. They are highly advantageous due to their availability, nontoxicity, stability, and cost. There are many catalytic uses of vitamins in organic as well as photochemical reactions. Because of their rich coordination chemistry, facilitating coordination via the side chains and the amino, carboxylate, and hydroxyl groups, they are appealing bioligands for the development of novel hybrid inorganic-organic compounds. Since vitamins have many different ways to bind to metals, they are excellent candidates for the formation of metal complexes. 3 Vitamins are vital to both human and animal life since the body cannot synthesize them or cannot produce sufficient amounts of them. 4 Vitamins are categorized according to their biological action. Vitamin B 1 (VB 1 ), also known as thiamine hydrochloride, is a water-soluble vitamin involved in the transmission of neuronal impulses, the synthesis of nucleic acids (DNA) and the production of energy from food. 5 In recent years, vitamin B 2 , i.e. riboflavin (RF), 6 and its derivatives have garnered significant attention as special redox organocatalysts that facilitate a wide range of catalytic oxidations. 7,8 As the fundamental building blocks of redox-active coenzymes, flavin derivatives (FLs) can withstand changes in redox potentials and can serve as the reaction sites of many heat- and photo-induced activities. 9-12 (-)-Riboflavin (vitamin B 2 ) has intrinsic energy transfer (ET) as well as single electron transfer (SET) patterns, which can be stimulated upon irradiation, 14 making it an extremely flexible organocatalyst for a number of transformations. 13 Vitamin B 12 is extremely soluble in water. Advanced organic synthesis requires the development of environmentally sustainable synthetic methods. In this manner, “green” and sustainable approaches seek to reduce waste production and steer clear of the usage of dangerous or poisonous materials. 15-18 In the chemical industry, a lot of organic conversions are still carried out by employing almost stoichiometric amounts of catalysts, producing huge amounts of chemical waste. Most catalytic processes depend on expensive, toxic heavy metals such as Pd, Pt, Ru, and Ir. 19 More strict environmental regulations and growing environmental concerns require the development of chemical processes that are both economically and ecologically beneficial. According to this viewpoint, the search for more effective and environmentally friendly catalysts has been at the center of organic chemistry research. Over the past two decades, there has been renewed interest in the application of visible-light photochemistry in organic synthesis; organic photochemistry dates back more than a century to Giacomo Ciamician's laboratory. A major step toward Ciamician's goals has been the ongoing advancement of photoredox catalysis methods, which use photosensitizers that absorb visible light to produce high-energy excited states that can produce reactive radicals using low-energy reactants through energy transfer processes and single-electron transfer (SET). 20-23 Many persistent problems in medicinal chemistry, 25-27 natural product synthesis, 28-30 cross-coupling reactions, 31,32 materials chemistry, 33,34 and other fields have been resolved using this simple and effective method for radical generation. 24 In addition to its influence on the field of organic chemistry, photoredox catalysis is commonly regarded as a more environmentally friendly, or “greener,” option for chemical synthesis, satisfying a number of the green chemistry principles. 37 Due to their water and air compatibility and ease of use, vitamin B 1 , vitamin B 2 , vitamin B 12 , vitamin B 7 , and vitamin C have become popular naturally derived photoredox catalysts in combination with a number of organocatalysts ( Fig. 1 ). 35,36

The photocatalyst (PC) moves from the ground state (S 0 ) to a singlet excited state (S 1 ) by absorbing visible light. Moreover, the intersystem crossover (T 1 ) increases the probability that the strongest PCs create a persistent triplet excited state. The T 1 excited state typically mediates intermolecular interactions, resulting in further chemical transformations that are typically dependent on single electron transfer (SET). When exposed to light, a photoexcited PC may donate or receive electrons and become a more potent oxidant and reducer. An oxidative or reductive quenching cycle is commonly used to describe mechanistic activities ( Scheme 1 ). In a reductive quenching cycle, the photoexcited PC* initially acts as an oxidant by absorbing an electron from an electron donor (D), also known as a reductive quencher. This leads to the formation of a PC˙ - radical anion with substantial reducing potential. Tertiary amines are the most often used reductive quenchers. However, in an oxidative quenching cycle, the photoexcited PC* transfers an electron to an electron acceptor (A, oxidative quencher) by acting first as a reductant. As a result, a PC˙ + radical cation is generated, which has a higher oxidative potential than PC*. Common oxidative quenchers include dicyanobenzenes, polyhalomethanes, and aryldiazonium salts. Energy transfer through the excited PC to the substrate is also known to result in chemical modifications. 41 A lot of studies have been conducted recently to develop catalytic systems that utilize natural building blocks. In continuation of our study, 42,43 this review is intended to provide a brief overview of some important aspects of photoredox catalysts, particularly vitamin-derived photocatalytic systems for organic synthesis, and comprises vitamin-catalysed photochemical transformations from 2015 to 2026.

VISIBLE LIGHT-INDUCED, VITAMIN-CATALYSED CHEMICAL TRANSFORMATIONS
Green chemistry is a scientific methodology that revolutionizes the synthesis of active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) by focusing on the development of sustainable, non-toxic chemical methods that reduce or eliminate hazardous compounds. Metal catalysts offer unparalleled reactivity and remarkable control over the complex molecular architecture for medicinal alterations compared to catalytic frameworks. However, they are costly, scarce, and highly regulated in terms of metal residue contamination of pharmaceuticals. Such problems can be completely eliminated using synthetic organophotocatalysts, which entirely prevent metal toxicity by relying on tiny organic molecules to boost light-induced single-electron conversions to circumvent these problems. They often involve multi-step, petroleum-derived syntheses, but they offer an extremely large and extremely adaptable redox scope for difficult, novel reactions. Vitamin-based catalysts are a natural subset of organophotocatalysts, providing the benefits of metal-based catalysts and organic dye-based catalysts together. Although with a smaller range of redox values and lower photostability than synthetic dyes, they exhibit a superior green footprint, inexpensiveness and excellent biocompatibility in the final step of drug functionalization.

Chao et al. 44 described a catalytic system for arene migration via ether to carboxylic acid conversion using water as an environmentally conscious solvent and riboflavin tetraacetate (RFT), a widely available ester of natural vitamin B 2 , as the corresponding photocatalyst; this system does not require any external oxidants, bases, metals, inert gas protection, or extended reaction times ( Scheme 2 ). In addition to the substrates of recognized substituted 2-phenyloxybenzoic acids, a series of equivalents derived from naphthalene and heterocycles were also transformed for the first time into the corresponding aryl salicylates. Mechanistic investigations, particularly a few kinetic isotope effect (KIE) experiments, indicated that the bifunctional flavin photocatalyst facilitated a series of electron transfer-proton transfer events. A proposed reaction mechanism is shown in Scheme 3 . Using an external base, carboxylic acid 1 was first dissolved in water or an organic solvent. Simultaneously, RFT was protonated and converted into RFTH + due to its mild basicity. When exposed to blue light irradiation in an aqueous solution, excited RFTH + acts as a strong oxidant (1.8-2.0 V), promoting the one-electron oxidation of carboxylate A (1.77 V) and converting to RFTH˙. Carboxylate radical B undergoes intramolecular addition to the neighbouring arene ring, resulting in the formation of intermediate C. The C-O bond was then homolysed and rearomatized to form phenoxy radical D. The transfer of electrons between radical D (1.21 V) and semiquinone RFTH˙ (∼-0.8 V) may produce related phenoxide D and protonated RFTH + . In the end, the proton of RFTH + goes to intermediate E to produce product 2, as RFT recovers in the ground state.

An easy approach for the direct photocatalytic formation of benzo-3,4-coumarins via biaryl carboxylic acids utilizing (-)-riboflavin as a photocatalyst in the presence of visible light was described by Gilmour et al. 48 ( Scheme 10 ). This synthetic method is simple to employ, uses a cheap catalyst, and does not require any metal additions. A proposed reaction mechanism is shown in Scheme 11 based on previous studies. When visible light is present, the catalytic process starts by protonating (-)-riboflavin (RF), and at λ max = 402 nm, the protonated flavin is strongly absorbed. The protonated flavin radical (RFH˙) and carboxy radical B are then produced by a single electron transfer via carboxylate anion A to RFH + . These radicals can then cyclize to produce intermediate C. Ultimately, intermediate C is oxidized and rearomatized with the flavin radical RFH˙ in a SET/deprotonation or hydrogen atom transfer reaction to produce the final product. Reduced (-)-riboflavin (RFH 2 ) underwent molecular oxygen reoxidation.

The photocatalytic efficacy of the Cu( ii )-vitamin C complex immobilized on titanium dioxide nanoparticles was investigated by Rezaeifard et al. 49 and utilized in the photo-derived homocoupling process of aryl boronic acids under heterogeneous conditions. Despite the utilization of any supplementary substances, like a base or oxidant, the homocoupling process produces the appropriate symmetrical biaryls in 50-97% yields at room temperature in the air under the age of visible light irradiation. Several substituents on aryl boronic acids, including halogen, carbonyl, and nitro groups, have been supported by this technique. The highest possible perceived quantum efficiency (AQY) at 410 nm was found in the light-induced photocatalytic activity of the aforementioned catalyst assessed by action spectra, indicating a visible-light-mediated photocatalytic reaction ( Scheme 12 ).

Jamshidinajafabadi and collaborators 50 in 2025 presented an effective photocatalytic system using a natural-based nanostructured catalyst consisting of a copper complex using a g-C 3 N 4 -vitamin B 5 hybrid supported on magnetic nanoparticles. Its band gap shifted from 2.8 eV (g-C 3 N 4 -B 5 ) to 2.45 eV (g-C 3 N 4 -B 5 -Cu@γ-Fe 2 O 3 ). Numerous significant reactions, such as the aerobic photooxidation of benzylic alcohols 18 to aldehydes 19 and the simultaneous formation of imines 21 and benzimidazoles via the dehydrogenative coupling of primary benzylic alcohols along with aromatic amines and diamines under visible-light irradiation, were effectively catalysed by the aforementioned magnetic nanohybrid of the g-C 3 N 4 -B 5 -Cu@γ-Fe 2 O 3 photocatalyst ( Scheme 13 ).

For the first time, Osaka et al. 53 showed the Giese reaction utilising easily accessible alkyl tosylates as alkyl radical sources. The reaction continued effectively using vitamin B 12 or Co(TTMP) catalysts, Mn reductant and a proton donor in the presence of visible light irradiation ( Scheme 18 ). The creation of alkyl-cobalt( iii ) intermediates through the SN 2 -type oxidative addition of alkyl tosylates into nucleophilic cobalt( i ) is an essential step in this process. Therefore, the reaction efficacy was impacted by the bulk surrounding the TsO group with alkyl tosylates. However, the regioselective functionalization of polyol molecules was made possible by classical tosylation and the Giese reaction that followed. Based on these findings, a proposed reaction mechanism is shown in Scheme 19 . Alkyl tosylates 29 were added to nucleophilic cobalt( i ) A via the SN 2 -type oxidative process, resulting in alkylcobalt( iii ) B. Co-C bond breakage then occurs when exposed to visible light, producing alkyl radical 30 and planar-cobalt( ii ) C. After the addition of an active olefin to β-position 31, the produced alkyl radical is reduced with Mn and protonated to form adduct 32. Mn reduction of planar-cobalt( ii ) C terminates the catalytic cycle and produces nucleophilic cobalt( i ) A.

Pitre et al. 54 published a protocol employing vitamin B 12 photocatalysis and dichloromethane (CH 2 Cl 2 ) as the methylene supplier to facilitate the cyclopropanation of electron-deficient alkenes ( Scheme 20 ). CH 2 Cl 2 goes through a nucleophilic substitution of the SN 2 type, resulting in Co III -CH 2 Cl intermediate A by utilizing vitamin B 12 's extremely nucleophilic Co I oxidation state. This intermediate can be photolyzed via green (525 nm) LED radiation to produce ˙CH 2 Cl radical C. In electron-deficient alkenes, this radical takes part in a polarity-matched Giese addition. Co II then traps the carbon-centered radical to produce intermediate D. Ultimately, the required cyclopropane product 35 is obtained by the reduction of D accompanied by 3- exo-tet cyclization. The generation of ˙CH 2 Cl radicals was demonstrated by radical trapping experiments, whereas UV-vis measurements supported the suggested SN 2 process. Significantly, the system demonstrated good chemoselectivity and functional group tolerance while exhibiting a wide substrate scope, comprising acrylates, acrylamides, acrylonitrile, and derivatives of dehydroamino acids. Substrates with protic or amine functionalities, which are usually troublesome in traditional photoredox techniques, were effectively cyclopropanated. In addition to CH 2 Cl 2 , the procedure was expanded to include isotopically labeled CD 2 Cl 2 , which provided direct access to deuterated cyclopropanes with therapeutic value, and 1,1-dichloroethane, which provided methyl-substituted cyclopropyl adducts ( Scheme 21 ).

At room temperature and under visible light irradiation, they employed low-cost natural vitamin B 2 (riboflavin, RF) or the simplest ester (riboflavin tetraacetate, RFT) as the photocatalysts, 1,8-diazabicyclo[5.4.0]-7-undecene (DBU) as the organic base and electron source, and ambient air as the terminal oxidant ( Scheme 22 ). Following the creation of the superoxide radical anion (O 2 ˙ - ), molecular oxygen oxidized the resultant RFT˙ - for regeneration. The O 2 ˙ - was then transformed into HOO - using the additional one electron provided by DBU or simply by disproportionation, which targeted the Michael acceptor (α,β-enone) to produce the epoxy ketone. Sun and colleagues 58 developed a biological cascading reaction for the formation of chalcones facilitated by visible light and catalysed by enzymes . This method relies on the persistent decarboxylative coupling reaction between cinnamic acids and aroylcarboxylic acids in the presence of 30 °C water. Chalcone variations were produced by employing this strategy, five of which were natural products. The catalytic cycle of the whole procedure was realized for the very first time when a visible light-mediated system was added to the biological chain reaction. This approach successfully encouraged the on-site replenishment of coenzyme components. Vitamin B 12 (VB 12 ) showed a superior photocatalytic effect. This procedure broadens the biological cascade's application and offers a fresh approach to the generation of chalcone derivatives ( Scheme 28 ). On the basis of the aforementioned experimental findings and earlier research, decarboxylative coupling was described, for which a proposed reaction mechanism is shown in Scheme 29 . In the reaction system's buffer environment (K 3 PO 4 , pH = 7.0), 44 (p K a ≈ 3.5) first experiences spontaneous deprotonation to create 44 - . There is no need for laccase catalysis because this activity is thermodynamically spontaneous. Next, type I Cu 2+ is reduced to Cu + , while type I Cu 2+ takes one electron from the α-C in 44 - and transforms it to the benzoyl formic acid radical intermediate A˙.

Finally, benzoyl radical B is produced when the unstable A˙ quickly decarboxylates (releasing CO 2 ). Instead of directly catalyzing deprotonation, laccase's primary role in this phase is to oxidize 44 - . In the buffer solution, 44 spontaneously completes a pretreatment called deprotonation, which produces an anion form that is easier to oxidize for the later transfer of electrons. Subsequently, the type I Cu + in the laccase active center completes self-oxidation (Cu + -Cu 2+ ) by transferring electrons to the O 2 bound to the type II/III Cu clusters via the internal electron transport chain (type I-type II-type III Cu clusters). O 2 is simultaneously oxidized to a superoxide anion (O 2 ˙ - ). This results in the reduction of NADP + to NADP(H). This suggests that in addition to being the last electron acceptor, O 2 is also a crucial intermediary that links 44 - oxidation and NADP + reduction. The VB 12 photocatalytic system is capable of recovering the reduction product NADP(H) to NADP + in a closed loop cycle, greatly increasing the reaction effectiveness and accomplishing the coenzyme's reuse and renewal. Following a coupling reaction with the cinnamic acid anion under set A's catalysis, free radical B decarboxylates to generate end product 46 following a single electron transfer. To finish the photocatalytic step, the activated VB 12 * is reduced to ground state VB 12 . Benzil 46′ is also produced during this procedure via a free radical self-coupling phenomenon.

Reaction proticity was shown by Julian G. West et al. 59 to have a significant and consistent impact on VB 12 HAT reactivity with significant synthetic consequences. They were able to create a hydrogenative oxetane ring-opening reaction using this hypothesis-driven reaction design, enabling a novel route for the production of saturated alcohols by oxetanes with protic conditions of reaction. Ring-opening isomerization was made easier by switching to an aprotic solvent containing sub-stoichiometric acid, which produced allylic alcohols from a variety of oxetane substrates ( Scheme 30 ). Combining these concepts, a proposed reaction mechanism for VB 12 -catalyzed opening of a hydrogenative oxetane ring is shown in Scheme 31(a) . To overcome the ring-opening activation obstacle and produce greater electrophilic species A, the oxetane substrate is first protonated. According to earlier findings, the stretched ring can then be cleaved by supernucleophilic reduction of VB 12 to yield Co( iii ) alkyl intermediate B. When exposed to visible light, this species emits carbon-centered radical C via the LiH of the Co-C bond. Donating HAT to the appropriate radical site yields hydrogenated alcohol product 48. According to the advantageous, though not necessarily essential, impact of thiols, the HAT donation step can take place via VB 12 or thiol catalysis. Under these conditions, TRIP disulfide and VB 12 might be reduced to produce a thiolate anion and Co( i ), respectively. The active HAT donors Co( iii )-H and thiol S-H are produced when these species are protonated. Co( ii ) and a thiyl radical should then be expelled by subsequent HAT donation toward the ring-opened radical. Lastly, reduction might complete the suggested reductive HAT cycle by reforming Co( i ) along with thiolate. They concentrated their investigation on the ring-opening isomerization system to take into account the impact of TFA loading on both the reaction efficiency and selectivity for isomerization, proposing that an effective isomerization system with ring opening required a compromise between Co( iii )-H, a probable acidic activator, a possible HAT donor, and Co( ii ), a crucial radical trap, and Scheme 31(b) describes the proposed methodology. Sub-stoichiometric TFA activates it in the beginning, but acidic Co( iii )-H might protonate it in later cycles. Here, Co( ii ) HAA forms allylic alcohol product 49 at the neighboring carbon in the absence of excessive proton supplies or a thiol HAT addition, which is comparable to the olefination procedure suggested in their earlier technique and CarH enzymatic photodetection. The resultant Co( iii )-hydride could undergo proton transfer through a deactivated oxetane to activate the ensuing ring opening and rebuild the Co( i ) nucleophile in order to complete the cycle.

Schmidt and colleagues 60 ( Scheme 32 ) employed potassium peroxydisulfate to serve as a sacrificial oxidizing agent and riboflavin to act as a photocatalyst; they synthesized benzothiazoles from thiobenzanilides under visible light irradiation in a yield of up to 97%. A proposed reaction mechanism is shown in Scheme 33 . When the singlet and triplet excited states of RFTA are photogenerated, thiobenzanilides may function as quenchers through an electron-transfer process, resulting in the radical anion RFTA˙ - and the associated thiobenzanilide radical cation (50˙ + ). Interestingly, the riboflavin moiety is also capable of serving as a base (p K a of [RFTA-H]˙ = 8.3), helping to deprotonate 50˙ + . Thiobenzanilide radical cation 50˙ + is deprotonated to produce the sulfur radical, which is then cyclized and subsequently rearomatized via whatever oxidant species to produce 2-substituted benzothiazoles 51. Gryko and colleagues 61 used the special nucleophilic and photochemical characteristics of vitamin B 12 and related complexes to produce carbon radicals using a number of precursors, such as diazo compounds, strained electrophiles, and alkyl halides. They investigated the use of this catalytic framework, enabling regioselective epoxide ring-openings in a recent noteworthy example. Epoxides are useful building components for the formation of nonsymmetrical alcohols; however, there are still issues with regioselectivity (branched vs. linear), particularly with aryl epoxides, that react mostly at the benzylic position. In this study, scientists created a tandem catalytic system using vitamin B 12 and nickel catalysis to selectively couple epoxides with aryl halides by exposure to visible light, producing linear alcohols ( Scheme 34 ). According to them, the process starts when Co I attacks the less hindered carbon of epoxide 52 nucleophilically, producing alkylcobalamin intermediate A. Following reductive elimination, the primary alkyl radical B produced by subsequent Co III -C bond photolysis joins the nickel cycle via aryl halide 53 to form the C-C bond. The natural thermodynamic propensity for the formation of benzylic radicals is overridden by this sterically regulated pathway, which also overrides the inclination of aryl epoxides to generate branched alcohol product 54. The process worked well with both aliphatic and aryl epoxides, and it could handle either electron-rich or electron-poor aryl halides, particularly difficult heteroaryl substrates. Crucially, vitamin B 12 photocatalysis and nickel catalysis worked together to create reactivity that nickel could not achieve. In a later paper, the scientists also demonstrated the ability to expand this approach for radical creation by opening an oxetane ring ( Scheme 35 ).

This reactivity was further developed in aqueous micellar medium by Gryko et al. 62 In this study, radical Giese-type additions to electron-deficient olefins are followed by the opening of the ring of the aforementioned epoxides and aziridines, which is facilitated via vitamin B 12 catalysis. Additionally, each product was made as a single linear regioisomer. This study marks a significant advancement in reaction sustainability by substituting more sustainable aqueous conditions, including alcohol cosolvents, for organic solvents ( Scheme 36 ).

West and co-workers 63 established an alternative method that directly transforms epoxides into Markovnikov alcohols, working in sequence regarding thiol-derived hydrogen atom transfer (HAT) catalysis ( Scheme 37 ). They demonstrated the regioselective opening of epoxide rings utilizing vitamin B 12 . The reaction starts with Co I 's initial nucleophilic attack on the epoxide's slightly hindered carbon, producing alkylcobalamin intermediate A, which photolyzes to produce primary alkyl radical B. West's methodology uses a TRIP thiol cocatalyst to transmit a hydrogen atom, efficiently resembling hydride nucleophiles and producing associated alcohol product 59 at elevated regioselectivity, while this method supervised this radical to a nickel-driven cross-electrophile coupling cycle and aryl halides. The reaction demonstrated a variety of functional group tolerance parameters, comprising electrophilic and reductively labile components that would ordinarily be susceptible to reduction or cleavage via hydride nucleophiles. The aforementioned ease of application highlights its potential for synthesis. According to the author, turning over the thiol catalyst in the midst of a protic solvent depends on Co II intermediate C produced following the radical production stage. The functional ease of the reaction highlights the synthetic potential of this technique. This work offers a novel method for the development of synergistic vitamin B 12 photocatalysis with thiol HAT catalysis ( Scheme 38 ).

CONCLUSION
Visible light-induced, vitamin-catalyzed chemical transformations have recently emerged as a viable and sustainable approach in modern organic synthesis. Vitamins such as riboflavin (vitamin B 2 ), cobalamin (vitamin B 12 ), and ascorbic acid (vitamin C) have been demonstrated to be viable options as photocatalysts or photoredox mediators since they are naturally abundant, low in toxicity, biocompatible and environmentally friendly. Upon exposure to visible light, these vitamins can generate reactive intermediates using radical and electron-transfer pathways, thus enabling a variety of useful chemical reactions to take place under mild conditions. This review encourages standard and synthetic organic chemists to establish more sustainable procedures utilizing vitamins in multicomponent reactions and oxidation, reduction, rearrangement, cyclization, cross-coupling and photooxidation reactions by illustrating the chemical properties, catalytic efficiency, recycling, and thermal durability of vitamins.
