ABSTRACT
Indigo carmine (IC) persists in aquatic environments due to its high solubility, aromatic stability, and sulfonated structure, raising toxicity concerns and inducing the formation of harmful oxidative intermediates. This review critically summarizes the information on IC and provides a mechanistic evaluation of its removal through adsorption, photocatalysis, electrochemical oxidation, biodegradation, and enzymatic treatment. Adsorption remains the superior performance and simplest decolorization method, whereas photocatalysis and advanced oxidation provide deeper degradation through radical-mediated cleavage of the chromophore and aromatic rings. Electrochemical technologies ensure rapid oxidation through direct/indirect anodic processes, while microbial and enzyme-based treatments (particularly using laccases and peroxidases) offer environmentally benign pathways for detoxification under mild conditions. A comprehensive comparison of material classes, like inorganic adsorbents (activated carbon (AC), biochar (BC), graphene derivatives, zeolites, porous silica, metal oxides, metal sulfides, and metal organic frameworks (MOFs)), organic polymers (synthetic resins, chitosan (CS), cellulose, and alginate), and biological systems (microorganisms and isolated enzymes), highlights their functional roles, advantages, and limitations. The factors enhancing the IC removal efficiency, like the solution pH, functional surface chemistry, porosity, ionic strength, catalyst doping, light utilization, hybrid systems, and process integration, are examined critically to identify optimization strategies. The main challenges remain in scaling laboratory findings to real wastewater treatment processes and understanding by-product toxicity, which, if overcome, would improve catalyst stability/regeneration and reduce energy consumption. Future perspectives emphasize the development of hybrid materials combining adsorption and catalytic properties, visible-light-active and immobilized photocatalysts, engineered enzymes, and integrated treatment trains for complete mineralization with a low environmental footprint. This review provides a holistic and analytical foundation for advancing efficient, scalable, and sustainable technologies for the removal and detoxification of indigo carmine from contaminated water.

INTRODUCTION
Synthetic dyes play a vital role in modern industrial sectors like textiles, 1,2 leather processing, 3,4 paper printing, 5,6 plastics manufacturing, 7 cosmetics, 8 pharmaceuticals, 9 and food technology. 10 Specifically, indigo carmine (IC) (also known as Acid Blue 74) is one of the most widely used indigoid dyes due to its intense blue coloration, high water solubility, and stability under diverse processing conditions. 11,12 Its widespread utilization, particularly in denim dyeing, 13 food coloring, 14 ink formulations, 15 and clinical diagnostics, 16,17 has resulted in the large-scale discharge of IC-containing effluents into aquatic systems. The structural complexity and chemical robustness of IC (which make it industrially desirable) simultaneously contribute to its high environmental persistence, which introduces an urgent need for efficient remediation strategies.

From a toxicological perspective, IC exhibits several hazardous effects on human and aquatic health. The concentration of IC is different in various environments. Its concentration is 20 mg L -1 in regions near textile factories. 18 ElKammah et al. 19 also reported that 32 mg L -1 of IC was present in wastewater. Its permitted level in food is less than 500 mg kg -1 . 20 Exceeding this level, it causes severe health issues. In humans, exposure through ingestion, inhalation, or dermal contact has been associated with hypotension, respiratory irritation, allergic reactions, and oxidative stress. 21-23 The aromatic sulfonated structure of IC can interact with cellular biomolecules and induce DNA damage under certain conditions. 24 In aquatic systems, IC reduces light penetration, disrupts photosynthetic activity, and exerts acute toxicity on microorganisms, algae, and fish. Its stability against natural photolysis and biodegradation leads to long-term accumulation, which makes it a priority pollutant in dye-contaminated wastewater. 25

Numerous methods, including coagulation-flocculation, 26 membrane filtration, 27 photocatalysis, 28-30 adsorption, 31 advanced oxidation processes (AOPs), 32 electrochemical oxidation, 33 and biological treatment, 34 have been studied for dye removal in different fields. However, each method faces critical drawbacks. Coagulation generates large sludge volumes, and their disposal is challenging. The membranes suffer from fouling and high operating costs. AOPs require intensive energy input and reagent consumption. The adsorption process simply transfers the pollutant from one place to another but does not destroy it. Photocatalysis often underperforms in real wastewater due to the presence of light-scattering and competing species, and biodegradation typically exhibits slow kinetics and dye-structure-dependent efficiency. These limitations underline the need for cost-effective, scalable, and robust methods suitable for industrial dye-laden effluents.

Among all reported approaches, adsorptive photocatalysis emerges as the most feasible and widely applicable technique for indigo carmine removal. 35 Its advantages are rapid kinetics, design flexibility, high efficiency over broad pH ranges, reusability of adsorbents, and compatibility with natural, synthetic, and engineered materials, which make it superior for large-scale applications. Furthermore, adsorptive photocatalysis is less affected by the structural complexity of the dye, providing consistent removal even in concentrated or mixed wastewater streams. Emerging materials, such as carbonaceous adsorbents, 36 metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), 37 polymer composites, 38 semiconductors, 39 and bio-based materials, 40 have demonstrated exceptional affinity toward IC, highlighting the potential of technological methods. For example, Adnan et al. 41 reported the degradation of IC with Microbacterium zeae K5. They used 100 mg L -1 to 500 mg L -1 of IC during the study. They established that the decolorization of IC follows a second-order kinetic model more suitably, as compared to zero- and first-order kinetic models. The decolorization efficiency increased with time. With time, an increased number of IC molecules come into contact with the system and get degraded. IC can also be removed selectively based on system and environmental conditions. Ahmed et al. 42 synthesized and used CuFe 2 O 4 nanoparticles for the selective removal of IC from mixtures of (IC + rhodamine-B (RhB)) and (IC + safranin O (SN)), separately. The system removed IC selectively from the mixture at pH 3. At this pH, the surface of the system is positively charged, and therefore, it has a high affinity for negatively charged species, like IC, as compared to other pollutants. The removal efficiency of IC increased with increasing the concentration of IC (10-50 ppm). The removal efficiency of the system increased with time until the equilibrium stage was obtained after 1 h.

Previously reported review articles generalized dye removal, providing limited mechanistic detail or failing to differentiate between structurally diverse dyes. 43-49 Many reviews do not critically evaluate IC-specific adsorption behaviour, photocatalytic degradation pathways, electrochemical oxidation mechanisms, or enzyme-mediated breakdown processes. Moreover, the comparative performance of new-generation materials, like porous silica, metal sulfides, functionalized polymers, and biological substrates, remains underexplored in the context of IC.

This review provides the knowledge for the removal of IC from water using various materials under different conditions critically and comprehensively. It integrates mechanistic understanding with material-specific discussions, highlights the shortcomings of current technologies and identifies opportunities for optimizing removal efficiency. By bridging material science, environmental chemistry, and wastewater engineering, this review provides an updated framework for designing effective IC remediation strategies and outlines future directions for advancing sustainable dye-removal technologies.

CHEMICAL STRUCTURE, PROPERTIES, SYNTHESIS, APPLICATIONS, TOXICITY, AND STRUCTURE-INTERACTION OF INDIGO CARMINE
Indigo carmine (IC) is also known as Acid Blue 74 or disodium 5,5′-indigodisulfonate, which is a synthetic indigoid dye characterized by a highly conjugated aromatic structure containing two sulfonate groups. These sulfonate groups impart exceptional water solubility and strong ionic behavior, while the extended π-conjugation system is responsible for its intense blue coloration and remarkable photochemical stability. This molecular robustness makes IC highly attractive for industrial applications but simultaneously contributes to its environmental persistence and resistance to natural degradation. Its stable chromophore structure (along with sulfonation) allows IC to remain chemically inert under variable pH and temperature conditions, which complicates its removal from contaminated water. 50,51

The extended π-conjugated chromophoric structure contributes to the chemical stability and recalcitrance of IC. The delocalization of electrons across the conjugated indigoid framework stabilizes the molecule and reduces its susceptibility to spontaneous transformation under ordinary environmental conditions. In addition, its intramolecular hydrogen bonding resists interactions with other polar species, as shown in Fig. 1 . Therefore, simple physical treatment methods can efficiently remove IC from water but do not necessarily destroy its molecular structure. At the same time, the electron-rich conjugated system provides chemically accessible sites for attack by highly reactive oxidizing species like hydroxyl radicals (˙OH), superoxide-derived species, photogenerated holes (h + ), and other reactive intermediates. 52 Therefore, the apparent contradiction between the environmental persistence of IC and its susceptibility to advanced oxidation can be understood chemically. Relatively mild conditions cannot effectively disrupt its stabilized conjugated framework, whereas sufficiently strong oxidizing species can initiate chromophore cleavage and subsequent degradation.

The sulfonate groups play an equally important role in determining the environmental behavior of IC. Under typical aqueous conditions, these groups remain predominantly ionized, which gives the molecule a strong anionic character and high water solubility. This characteristic can hinder removal by hydrophobic adsorbents because IC remains solvated in water. Therefore, positively charged or protonated adsorbent surfaces can strongly attract IC through electrostatic interactions. Therefore, pH-dependent adsorption should be discussed primarily in terms of the surface charge of the adsorbent, rather than major protonation-deprotonation changes of the sulfonate groups themselves. At pH values below the point of zero charge ( P PZC ) of an adsorbent, protonated surface sites can promote electrostatic attraction toward anionic IC, whereas negatively charged surfaces can cause electrostatic repulsion. 53 This structural interpretation provides a clearer chemical explanation for the frequently reported strong dependence of IC adsorption on solution pH.

The synthesis of indigo carmine ( Fig. 2 ) involves the sulfonation of indigo using concentrated sulfuric acid to produce indigo disulfonic acid, which is subsequently neutralized with sodium hydroxide to form the final dye salt. 54 Although efficient, this process generates significant amounts of acidic and organic waste, which contribute to secondary environmental pollution. Prior to sulfonation, indigo itself was synthesized through classical routes, such as the Baeyer-Drewson reaction, 55 which also involves aromatic intermediates that are difficult to degrade. The synthesis chain highlights the environmentally intensive nature of IC production, which further highlights the importance of its effective removal from industrial effluents.

Indigo carmine has widespread applications across multiple sectors due to its strong color stability and safe usage at regulated limits. It is most extensively used in the textile industry for dyeing denim 56 and cotton 57 fabrics, where its redox behavior facilitates deep and uniform fiber penetration. In addition, IC serves as a certified food colorant in beverages, 58 confectionery, and processed foods. 59 It is also employed in inks, 60 cosmetics, 61 detergents, and paper coatings 62 due to its resistance to light and chemical agents. In medical fields, IC is used as a biological stain and diagnostic agent, particularly in surgical procedures to trace leaks and monitor kidney filtration. 63 These diverse applications result in a high global demand, which increases the likelihood of IC contamination in water bodies through untreated industrial discharges.

The toxicity of IC should not be attributed simply to the presence of its aromatic rings or sulfonate groups. Toxicity is likely to arise from the combined effects of molecular structure, transformation products, impurities, exposure route, concentration, and biological susceptibility. The conjugated indigoid framework can participate in electron-transfer and oxidative processes, while interactions of IC or its transformation products with cellular components can disturb cellular homeostasis. Experimental evidence has reported cytotoxic effects in cultured human fibroblasts at sufficiently high concentrations. One study observed significant reduction in fibroblast viability at IC concentrations above 100 µg mL -1 . 64 However, these in vitro findings should not be directly extrapolated to normal dietary human exposure because exposure concentration, metabolism, bioavailability, and administration route differ substantially.

Sulfonate groups can participate in ion-mediated interactions. IC has been reported to form stable complexes with metal ions like Cu, Co, Zn, and Ni. Such interactions are important because metal complexation can modify the physicochemical properties and, potentially, the biological behavior of both the dye and the metal species. 20 Therefore, the environmental fate of IC cannot be adequately described by considering the free dye molecule alone. Dye-metal interactions and dye-surface interactions can alter the transport and reactivity of IC in real wastewater.

IC exhibits notable toxicological issues when present at elevated concentrations in the environment, although its safe use in food and medicine is regulated. In humans, excessive exposure can induce oxidative stress, DNA damage, allergic reactions, and respiratory irritation. 65,66 Clinical studies have also reported episodes of hypotension and gastrointestinal discomfort following intravenous administration, which indicate that IC can interfere with physiological oxidation-reduction processes. The dye is particularly harmful in aquatic ecosystems, where it disrupts microbial metabolism, inhibits algal photosynthesis by blocking light penetration, and reduces dissolved oxygen levels. 58 The persistent aromatic sulfonate structure of IC resists photolysis and microbial degradation, which enables its long-term accumulation in sediments and continuous exposure to aquatic organisms. This stability, along with its documented toxicity, emphasizes the importance of developing efficient removal strategies.

The environmental challenges posed by indigo carmine are underestimated due to its approved use in consumable products. While safe at controlled doses, the dye behaves very differently once released into aquatic ecosystems, where its molecular stability transforms it into a long-lasting pollutant. Its synthesis generates environmentally burdensome waste, its structural features impede natural breakdown, and its toxic effects impair both human and ecological health. These combined factors make indigo carmine a compelling target for advanced removal technologies, which demand innovative materials and treatment methods capable of addressing its chemical resilience.

IC REMOVAL MECHANISMS
The removal of IC from aqueous systems relies on a range of physicochemical and biological mechanisms, each exploiting different aspects of the molecular structure and reactivity of the dye. Among these, adsorption, 67 photocatalysis, 68 electrochemical oxidation, 69 and biodegradation 70 represent the most widely investigated approaches. These methods differ in their operational principles, efficiency, environmental impact, and suitability for industrial-scale application, which makes it essential to critically analyze their underlying mechanisms.

Adsorption is one of the most widely adopted techniques for IC removal, owing to its operational simplicity, regenerability, and compatibility with diverse sorbent materials. However, IC adsorption can follow different mechanisms simultaneously. It can occur through physical adsorption (physisorption), 71 chemical adsorption (chemisorption), 40 or pore-filling 72 depending on the sorbent surface chemistry, charge density, and porosity. Understanding the differences among these processes is essential for rational sorbent design and performance optimization.

Physical adsorption or physisorption is one of the most fundamental mechanisms governing the uptake of IC onto porous solids and carbonaceous materials, as shown in Fig. 3(A) . The adsorption energy provides an important thermodynamic basis for distinguishing physisorption from chemisorption. Physisorption is associated with relatively weak non-covalent interactions and comparatively low adsorption enthalpies, whereas chemisorption involves stronger, more specific surface interactions and hence larger energy changes. The adsorption process is considered physisorption if the adsorption energy (or enthalpy of adsorption) value is less than 40 kJ mol -1 , while if the value is greater than 40 kJ mol -1 , the adsorption process is indicative of chemisorption. Physisorption is driven by weak, non-specific intermolecular forces like van der Waals interactions, electrostatic attractions, hydrogen bonding, and π-π interactions, which can produce appreciable adsorption energies without the formation of a true chemical bond. For a large, aromatic, anionic dye like IC, physisorption is especially significant due to its planar conjugated system and negatively charged sulfonate groups, which make it highly responsive to surface charge, hydrophobicity, and aromaticity of adsorbents. 73-75

IC molecules adhere to the surface simply because the adsorbent possesses attractive energy fields that temporarily hold the dye without altering its chemical identity. This results in rapid adsorption, as no activation energy barrier must be overcome. Materials, such as activated carbon (AC), 74 biochar (BC), 76 reduced graphene oxide (RGO), 35 carbon nanotubes (CNTs), 77 and mesoporous carbons, 78 exhibit exceptional physical adsorption due to their high surface area, π-electron-rich structures (promoting π-π stacking), and abundance of weakly polar surface groups. Electrostatic attraction also plays a crucial role as protonated carbon surfaces attract the negatively charged sulfonate groups of IC at low pH and hence significantly enhance adsorption capacity.

The reversibility of physisorption is high due to weak interactions. IC can be desorbed under mild conditions, which enable multiple regeneration cycles with minimal structural degradation of the adsorbent. 79 This makes physisorption-based systems economically advantageous and environmentally sustainable. Still, the weak non-specific forces also mean that competing ions (especially in real wastewater) can disrupt the process. High ionic strength screens the electrostatic interactions and dramatically reduces dye uptake. Additionally, physisorption is sensitive to temperature, with capacities decreasing at high temperatures due to the exothermic nature of the process. Garcia et al. 79 used LiMn 2 O 4 as an adsorbent for IC and examined the thermodynamic parameters. They achieved an enthalpy change of 1.3 ± 0.1 kJ mol -1 , which indicated that the adsorption process is physisorption. The recyclability of LiMn 2 O 4 in this process was very high, and the adsorbent maintained stability even after several recycling processes.

Physisorption is limited by insufficient selectivity and vulnerability to environmental variations. While it enables fast removal and excellent reusability, its interaction strength is weaker than that for chemisorption, which indicates that in complex matrices or under fluctuating pH, dye removal efficiency can drop sharply. Nonetheless, its simplicity, regenerability, and compatibility with low-cost materials ensure that it remains a central pathway for IC removal.

Chemical adsorption or chemisorption represents stronger surface interactions like coordination, surface complexation, or covalent/chemical bonding, and exhibits higher adsorption energies (>40 kJ mol -1 ). Its value depends on the chemical bond which is formed during the chemisorption. During the IC removal, chemical bonds or strong coordination interactions take place between IC molecules and active sites on the adsorbent surface, as shown in Fig. 3(B) . Unlike physisorption (which relies on weak intermolecular forces), chemisorption proceeds through electron sharing, electron transfer, or ion-exchange reactions, which make it both highly selective and significantly more stable. 80,81

IC contains aromatic rings, carbonyl groups, imine-type nitrogen, and sulfonate groups, which provide several reactive sites capable of forming strong interactions. On metal oxides, sulfonate groups can undergo ligand exchange with surface hydroxyls, which creates surface-bound sulfonate complexes. Similarly, metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), 82 amine-functionalized polymers, 83 and chitosan (CS) derivatives 84 facilitate chemisorption through coordination between metal centers or -NH 2 groups and the electron-rich sites of IC, forming stable inner-sphere complexes. These interactions are typically irreversible under mild conditions due to high binding energies.

Chemisorption is usually slower than physisorption because it requires an activation energy barrier to form the new bonds or exchange ions. However, it provides much higher adsorption capacity, particularly when the adsorbent contains functional groups tailored to react with the dye. Functionalized materials like amine-grafted silica, 85 functionalized graphene, 86 or doped polymers 87 display dominant chemisorption behavior due to their strong affinity for the anionic and aromatic characters of IC.

A critical advantage of chemisorption is its resilience in complex water matrices. Competing ions, 88 pH variation, 89 or high salinity 90 conditions affect chemisorption far less severely than physisorption, since reactions are governed by bond formation rather than weak electrostatic forces. This stability allows chemisorbing materials to maintain high performance in real wastewater systems where physisorbing materials would fail. On the other hand, the main drawback of chemisorption is its poor regenerability. Breaking chemical bonds requires harsh chemical reagents (strong acids/bases, chelating agents) or elevated temperatures, which can damage the adsorbent and reduce its long-term performance. As a result, chemisorption-based systems exhibit excellent single-cycle performance but suffer from limited multi-cycle usability. Additionally, functional groups responsible for binding can degrade during repeated regeneration, which causes a decline in capacity.

Chemisorption is highly effective for the selective and permanent removal of IC, especially when handling real or industrial effluents, but its limitations in recyclability and energy-intensive regeneration restrict its large-scale application. Therefore, future materials should aim to balance strong interaction strength with reversible binding, enabling both high performance and long-term operational stability.

Surface adsorption occurs at externally accessible adsorption sites. IC molecules interact with functional groups, metal centers, charged sites, or aromatic domains directly exposed to the aqueous solution. Therefore, the process is strongly influenced by surface charge, functional groups and surface chemistry. The molecule remains associated with the external surface through electrostatic, π-π, hydrogen-bonding, van der Waals or coordination interactions. On the other hand, pore filling describes the progressive occupation of the internal pore volume. Here, the critical factor is not simply the number of surface functional groups but whether the IC molecule can physically enter pores of appropriate dimensions. IC is a relatively large, planar molecule; therefore, pore diameter and molecular dimensions are critical.

The pore-filling mechanism is a distinct adsorption pathway that plays a major role in the uptake of IC by highly porous materials, like activated carbon, 91 biochar, 92 mesoporous silica, zeolite, 82 porous organic polymers, 93,94 and microporous metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), 95 as shown in Fig. 3(C) . Unlike physisorption and chemisorption (both of which primarily involve surface interactions), the pore-filling mechanism depends on the penetration and occupation of dye molecules within internal pores, driven by capillary forces, pore geometry, and molecular-size compatibility.

Indigo carmine is a large anionic dye with an extended aromatic structure. Thus, its removal by pore filling strongly depends on whether the pore diameter matches or exceeds the molecular dimensions of IC. Micropores (<2 nm) facilitate strong retention, which enhances van der Waals interactions through proximity effects, while mesopores (2-50 nm) act as transport channels, allowing efficient diffusion of IC into interior adsorption sites. Materials possessing hierarchical pore structures (combining micro-, meso-, and macropores) often demonstrate superior IC removal because macropores accelerate mass transfer, mesopores stabilize diffusion, and micropores accommodate strong confinement-based adsorption. 96,97

The pore-filling mechanism contributes significantly to the high adsorption capacities observed in activated carbon 98 and MOFs, 99 where interior pore volume (rather than surface functional groups) dictates performance. For these materials, adsorption capacity correlates more strongly with pore volume and surface area than with the nature of surface chemistry. Importantly, pore filling is less sensitive to pH, ionic strength, and competing ions compared to physisorption, because once IC molecules enter the pores, they are stabilized by confinement forces that are not easily disrupted by environmental fluctuations. 100,101 However, a major limitation of pore filling is slow intraparticle diffusion, especially in materials with narrow micropores. Large IC molecules can diffuse slowly through the pore network, which causes a delay in reaching equilibrium and lower removal rates in the initial stages. This makes pore filling the slowest adsorption mechanism, which is identified by intraparticle diffusion-controlled kinetics. Additionally, incomplete pore accessibility (caused by pore blockage, structural collapse, or insufficiently connected mesopores) can hinder dye uptake despite a high measured surface area.

A critical advantage of the pore-filling mechanism is that it provides high adsorption capacity, which surpasses those of materials that rely solely on physisorption or chemisorption. 53 Regeneration is typically easier than that in chemisorption, because no strong chemical bonds are formed. However, desorption can still require prolonged washing due to diffusion resistance. This balance (strong confinement without covalent interaction) makes pore-filling-based materials highly attractive for scalable wastewater treatment, where high capacity, structural stability, and environmental tolerance are required. Pore filling is a dominant and highly effective mechanism in porous adsorbents used for IC removal, but its performance is constrained by diffusion limitations and the need for a tailored pore architecture. Future materials should integrate hierarchical porosity with surface functionalization to synergistically combine fast transport, high capacity, and selective interaction for optimal dye removal. 102

The comparison of these mechanisms reveals that physisorption excels in speed and reusability, chemisorption provides superior selectivity and stability, and pore filling delivers high capacity but slow kinetics. In practice, most high-performance adsorbents integrate these mechanisms synergistically, using surface functionalities to stabilize adsorption while relying on hierarchical porosity for enhanced capacity. Therefore, future material design should aim to balance these characteristics, enabling fast, selective, high-capacity, and regenerable removal of IC under realistic wastewater conditions.

Photocatalysis is a process in which a photocatalyst uses photons to degrade the IC. Unlike adsorption (which merely transfers the dye to another phase), photocatalysis chemically decomposes IC into less harmful species like CO 2 , H 2 O, and SO 4 2- . The process is driven by semiconductor photocatalysts, like g-C 3 N 4 , 109 metal sulfides, 110 metal oxides, 111 and doped 112 or composite 113 photocatalytic materials.

Under light irradiation (UV, visible, or solar), semiconductor photocatalysts absorb photons whose energies are equal to or greater than their band gap. This generates electron-hole pairs (e - /h + ) that migrate to the catalyst surface ( Fig. 4 ), as given in eqn (1) . These reactive oxygen species (ROS) attack the chromophoric and aromatic structures of IC, initiating hydroxylation, ring opening, desulfonation, and stepwise fragmentation of the molecule. A photocatalyst degrades IC by photocatalysis. In this process, the photocatalyst breaks down the stable aromatic rings and strongly electron-withdrawing sulfonate groups of the dye. This makes the IC structure nonbiodegradable in the environment. The photocatalyst can be recycled after the complete degradation of IC and used again as a photocatalyst for further degradation of other IC molecules under light exposure. However, photocatalytic degradation is strongly influenced by parameters like catalyst crystallinity, surface area, band-gap position, light wavelength, pH, oxygen availability, and the presence of scavengers or competing organic pollutants. 38,114-116

The photocatalytic removal faces several limitations. Electron-hole recombination remains a major drawback, which reduces ROS generation and hence decreases the catalytic efficiency of a photocatalyst. Dye self-sensitization can occur, where IC absorbs visible light and transfers electrons to the catalyst, accelerating degradation but also causing catalyst fouling. In real wastewater, high ionic strength, turbidity, and coexisting chemicals can block active sites or hinder light penetration. Excess adsorption of IC on catalyst surfaces can also reduce catalytic performance by shielding active sites from light.

To overcome these challenges, several material-engineering strategies like heterojunction formation, 117 metal 118 and non-metal doping, 119 and composite integration with polymers 38 or carbon-based 109 materials have been developed. Each approach enhances photocatalytic performance through distinct yet complementary mechanisms.

Heterojunctions represent one of the most advanced strategies for enhancing photocatalytic performance. In this approach, two semiconductors are mixed with staggered band structures. Heterojunctions promote efficient charge separation and reduce electron-hole recombination, which is a major issue in conventional photocatalysis. There are four types of mechanisms for heterojunction semiconductors. First is the type-I mechanism, in which one semiconductor has a higher valence band and a lower conduction band than the second semiconductor, as shown in Fig. 5(A) . Such mechanisms are not frequently reported in the literature. The type-II mechanism is another strategy for pollutant degradation, which involves heterojunction materials, as shown in Fig. 5(B) . In this mechanism, the valence band of one semiconductor (X semiconductor) has a higher energy level than that of the second semiconductor (Y semiconductor), while the conduction band of the Y semiconductor has a lower energy level than the conduction band of the X semiconductor. 116 In this case, the electrons of the X semiconductor move from its conduction band to the conduction band of the Y semiconductor. Similarly, holes shift from the valence band of the Y semiconductor to the valence band of the X semiconductor. Now, the oxygen molecules are reduced by the conduction band electrons from the Y semiconductor to form ˙O 2 - radicals, while the holes oxidize the water or OH - ions into ˙OH radicals. These generated ROS degrade pollutants like IC from water. The third mechanism is called Z-scheme, in which the valence band of one conductor (X semiconductor) has a higher energy level than that of the second semiconductor (Y semiconductor), while the conduction band of the Y semiconductor has a lower energy level than the conduction band of the X semiconductor, as shown in Fig. 5 (C). In this case, the conduction band electrons of the Y semiconductor recombine with the valence band holes of the X semiconductor. 120 Now, the conduction band electrons of X react with O 2 to form ˙O 2 - , while the valence band holes of the Y semiconductor react with water or hydroxide to form ˙OH radicals, and these generated ROS decompose the pollutants. The fourth type of mechanism is S-scheme, in which the valence band of one semiconductor (X semiconductor) material has a higher energy level than that of the second semiconductor (Y semiconductor), while the conduction band of the Y semiconductor has a lower energy level than the conduction band of the X semiconductor, as shown in Fig. 5(D) . In this case, the conduction band electrons of the X semiconductor shift to the conduction band of the Y semiconductor, while the holes shift from the valence band of the Y semiconductor to the valence band of X. This shifting of electrons and holes produces an internal electric field, which resists the recombination of electrons and holes. 121 Heterojunctions exhibit superior photocatalytic efficiency compared to single-component semiconductors because they combine improved light absorption with enhanced charge separation. However, their performance depends strongly on band alignment, interface quality, and synthesis method, and mismatched band structures or poor contact can limit efficiency. While they provide robust degradation of IC under visible light and can achieve nearly complete mineralization, heterojunction systems involve complex fabrication processes and can be difficult to scale industrially. Despite these challenges, they remain one of the most effective strategies for high-performance photocatalytic removal of IC in aqueous systems.

The progression of IC degradation via the type-II, Z-scheme, and S-scheme mechanisms reflects a shift from simple charge separation toward a redox-preserving process, which dynamically accelerates architectures. The type-II mechanism provides ease of construction but fails to deliver complete IC mineralization, owing to weakened redox capability. Although the Z-scheme restores redox power, it can suffer from structural complexity and interfacial losses. S-scheme combines the redox strength of Z-scheme with superior charge mobility and internal electric-field enhancement, which results in the most efficient and robust photocatalytic degradation of IC reported to date.

Metal doping involves introducing transition-metal 122-124 or noble-metal 125,126 ions into the semiconductor lattice. These metals act as electron or hole traps, prolonging carrier lifetime and reducing recombination. They can also generate intermediate energy levels that narrow the band gap, which enable visible light activation, as shown in Fig. 6(A) . Noble metals, like Ag 127 and Au, additionally exhibit plasmonic resonance, which enhances light absorption and injects energetic electrons into the conduction band. 128 Metal doping is simpler than heterojunction construction and highly effective for visible light-driven IC degradation. However, excessive metal loading can produce recombination centers, which decrease performance, and leaching of metals can pose environmental hazards.

Non-metal doping 129,130 improves photocatalytic activity by introducing impurity states in the band structure, which narrows the band gap and extends absorption into the visible region, as shown in Fig. 6(B) . Non-metal doping also improves charge mobility and modifies surface chemistry, which increases hydroxyl radical generation and dye adsorption. 131 Unlike metal doping, non-metal doping avoids leaching and enhances environmental stability. However, the effect on charge separation is typically less pronounced than in heterojunctions or metal-doped materials; thus, the overall enhancement is moderate unless combined with other strategies.

Natural polymer 132 and carbon-based composites like graphene, 133 graphene oxide (GO), reduced graphene oxide (RGO), 134 chitosan, 135 and synthetic polymers 136 enhance photocatalysis through multiple synergistic mechanisms. Graphene, GO, and RGO serve as electron highways, which rapidly transfer electrons from the semiconductor and suppress recombination. They also provide strong π-π interactions, which improve the adsorption of IC onto the catalyst surface and increase the local dye concentration for faster degradation. Chitosan adds functional groups (-NH 2 , -OH) for electrostatic adsorption and stabilizes nanoparticles to prevent aggregation, while synthetic polymers improve catalyst dispersion, structural integrity, and resistance to photocorrosion. The primary limitation is that excessive polymer or graphene loading can shield active sites from light, which reduces efficiency. Nonetheless, these composites combine adsorption and photocatalysis, which address the intrinsic limitation of single-component catalysts that rely solely on ROS formation.

Gaur et al. 137 have reported the photocatalytic degradation pathway of IC, as shown in Fig. 7 . The parent IC molecule has a molecular-ion signal around m / z 421 (shown as m / z 420 in the structural scheme), and its degradation proceeds through successive structural modifications, like desulfonation, dehydroxylation, cleavage of the indigoid/ring system, hydroxylation, and formation of lower-molecular-weight aromatic products. According to the authors, the parent IC structure undergoes partial oxidative transformation after approximately 5 min, which produces products at m / z 374 and 358. This indicates early modification of the original conjugated structure. The first major transformation illustrated in Fig. 7 is desulfonation, in which one sulfonated functionality associated with the IC aromatic ring is removed or transformed. This step is chemically important because the sulfonate groups contribute strongly to IC's water solubility and ionic character. Their removal progressively converts the highly sulfonated dye into less ionic intermediates. The subsequent dehydroxylation/oxidative modification produces the m / z 358 intermediate, which indicates further alteration of the oxygenated and conjugated structure. Continued photocatalytic oxidation then destabilizes the central indigoid framework and promotes ring cleavage, which generates smaller molecular fragments. The study detected products at m / z 248 and m / z 218 after 10 min, identified as 2-(2-amino-5-sulfophenyl)-2-oxoacetic acid and 2-amino-5-sulfobenzoic acid, respectively. These products demonstrate that the original large IC molecule has been divided into smaller aromatic/oxygenated fragments. The formation of 2,3-dioxoindoline-5-sulfonic acid ( m / z 229) indicates further oxidative transformation of the indole-derived portion of IC after 15 min. Therefore, the transformation proceeds from the highly conjugated parent dye toward smaller, less structurally complex aromatic intermediates. Therefore, hydroxylation and additional oxidative ring transformation generate 6-amino-2,3,4-trihydroxybenzoic acid ( m / z 184), demonstrating that the aromatic ring has undergone extensive hydroxylation and oxidative modification. The pathway leads toward 2-aminobenzoic acid (anthranilic acid, m / z 138), which the authors identify as a relatively stable low-molecular-weight intermediate. Hence, Fig. 7 demonstrates that IC degradation is not a single-step disappearance of the dye but a sequential molecular transformation, which begins with modification/desulfonation of the parent molecule, followed by dehydroxylation and cleavage of the indigoid/ring structure, formation of smaller sulfonated aromatic compounds, progressive hydroxylation and ultimately formation of anthranilic-acid-type products. The authors describe the process as involving oxidative demethylation, deamination, desulfonation and aromatic-ring cleavage, which produce simpler and less toxic intermediates.

Heterojunctions show the best performance compared to other strategies in terms of separation and redox potential, which make them highly effective for complete IC mineralization. Metal doping excels in visible light absorption and electron trapping, while non-metal doping provides environmentally stable, moderate band-gap tuning. Polymer and carbon-based composites are superior for adsorption enhancement and catalyst stability, although they cannot significantly alter intrinsic redox properties. The best-performing photocatalysts integrate multiple strategies (like a metal- or non-metal-doped heterojunction embedded in a graphene or chitosan matrix) to synergistically achieve enhanced light absorption, prolonged carrier lifetime, improved IC adsorption, and higher ROS generation. In this way, photocatalytic removal becomes fast, efficient, and robust even in complex wastewater systems to overcome the limitations of individual approaches.

Fenton-like degradation of IC is a heterogeneous advanced oxidation process in which surface redox-active sites activate H 2 O 2 to generate highly reactive oxygen species (particularly ˙OH), which attack the electron-rich conjugated indigoid structure of IC. Continued oxidation can ultimately produce H 2 O, CO 2 , and inorganic ions. 138,139 During the Fenton-like oxidation process, reactions are taking place as given in eqn (8)-(12) . 8 Fe 2+ + H 2 O 2 → Fe 3+ + HO˙ + OH - 9 Fe 3+ + H 2 O 2 → Fe 2+ + HOO˙ + H + 10 Fe 2+ + HO˙ → Fe 3+ + OH - 11 HO˙ + H 2 O 2 → HOO˙ + H 2 O 12 HO˙ + HOO˙ → H 2 O 2 + O 2 In the first step, ferrous ion gains an electron from H 2 O 2 to form ferric ion, OH - , and ˙OH radicals, as given in eqn (8) . The produced ferric ions further react with H 2 O 2 to form ferrous ions and HOO˙ radicals, as given in eqn (9) . In eqn (10) , Fe 2+ reacts with HO˙ to produce ferric and OH - ions. The HO˙ radicals react with H 2 O 2 to form HOO˙ and water. According to eqn (12) , the HO˙ radicals react with HOO˙ radicals to again form H 2 O 2 and O 2 molecules.

The degradation mechanism of IC, shown in Fig. 8 , proceeds through a sequence of hydroxyl-radical-mediated oxidation, structural fragmentation, desulfonation, aromatic-ring transformation, and eventual mineralization during the Fenton-like process, which has been reported by Bopda et al. 139 Under UV-C irradiation in the presence of H 2 O 2 , reactive species (particularly ˙OH) initiate degradation by attacking the highly conjugated central C C bond of IC, which is responsible for its characteristic chromophore. The cleavage of this bond is accompanied by the transformation of the adjacent carbonyl groups into hydroxyl functionalities, which produce the first intermediate ByP-1. Further reactions of ByP-1 with ˙OH and H˙ lead successively to ByP-2 and ByP-3 through hydroxyl-group abstraction, hydrogen addition, and C-OH bond cleavage. Thereafter, the pathway proceeds through desulfonation, in which the sulfonate/bisulfite groups are removed through ˙OH attack at the sulfur-containing moieties, which involves S-C bond cleavage and the formation of sulfur-containing products, like HSO 3 - /H 2 SO 4 , ultimately producing ByP-4. Later, ˙OH attack on the central C-C bond of ByP-4 causes the cleavage of the remaining bis-indole framework and generates IntP-1 (2,3-dihydro-1 H -indole-2,3-diol), together with a carbon-centered radical fragment. Further oxidation of IntP-1 produces the carbonyl-containing ByP-5 (3-hydroxy-1,3-dihydro-2 H -indol-2-one; dioxindole-type intermediate). Hydroxyl-radical attack on the carbonyl carbon of ByP-5 then promotes N-C bond cleavage and the formation of ByP-6 [2-(2-aminophenyl)-2-hydroxyacetic acid], which undergoes further ˙OH oxidation and decarboxylation with the release of CO 2 , yielding IntP-2 (2-aminobenzyl alcohol). IntP-2 can follow more than one pathway, as the ˙OH-mediated removal of its hydroxyl group produces IntP-3 (2-methylaniline), while an alternative branch generates IntP-5 (benzyl alcohol). Further hydroxyl-radical attack on the aromatic nucleus of IntP-3 causes electron-transfer and hydrogen-addition reactions, producing IntP-4 (methylcyclohexa-1,5-diene-1-amine), which subsequently transforms into ByP-7 (2-methylcyclohex-1-en-1-amine). In the parallel route, IntP-5 is transformed toward ByP-7* (2-cyclohexen-1-ylmethanol). Finally, continued oxidation of these smaller intermediates by ˙OH and O 2 leads to further ring degradation and the formation of H 2 O, CO 2 , and inorganic nitrogen products like NH 3 , which indicate progression from chromophore destruction toward deeper degradation and mineralization. Hence, Fig. 8 demonstrates that IC degradation is not simply a decolorization process but a sequential chemical transformation involving initial C C cleavage, hydroxylation, desulfonation, C-C/N-C bond cleavage, indole/isatin-type intermediate formation, decarboxylation, aromatic-ring oxidation and transformation, generation of low-molecular-weight products, and mineralization.

Notably, complete mineralization occurs more slowly than initial decolorization. Unlike homogeneous Fenton treatment, heterogeneous Fenton-like catalysts immobilize redox-active sites on supporting materials like biochar, ferrites, metal oxides, MOFs or other porous materials, which improve catalyst recovery and reduce iron sludge. Recent evidence demonstrates the potential of such systems. Ferromagnetic biochar achieved 99.66% IC degradation at pH 3, while a sustainable γ-Fe 2 O 3 /calcite catalyst derived from copper metallurgical slag achieved 96.1% degradation within 60 min under photo-Fenton conditions. 139,140 However, high decolorization should not be equated with detoxification. Identification of intermediates, metal leaching, TOC removal, H 2 O 2 utilization efficiency and residual toxicity are essential for demonstrating genuine mineralization and environmental applicability.

Electrochemical oxidation (EO) is a highly effective advanced oxidation process that removes IC through electron-transfer reactions at the electrode surface and the in situ generation of strong oxidizing species. The mechanism operates through two complementary pathways, such as (1) direct oxidation 141 and (2) indirect oxidation. In direct oxidation, IC molecules adsorb onto the anode surface and undergo electron transfer, which results in oxidative cleavage of the azo group, desulfonation, and breakdown of the aromatic rings, eventually leading to the mineralization of IC to CO 2 , H 2 O, and inorganic ions. This process is highly efficient for those molecules that strongly interact with the electrode surface. In indirect oxidation, the anode generates reactive oxygen species (ROS) like hydroxyl radicals (˙OH), chlorine radicals (Cl˙), ozone, or persulfates, which diffuse into the bulk solution and oxidize IC molecules without requiring direct contact with the electrode. This allows EO to be effective for treating high-concentration dye solutions or molecules with low surface affinity. 142-144

In direct anodic oxidation, IC is oxidized through electron transfer at or extremely close to the anode surface. The IC molecule must first approach and adsorb sufficiently close to the electroactive surface, after which electrons are directly withdrawn from its conjugated molecular structure under the applied anodic potential. This initial direct electron transfer can destabilize the electron-rich π-conjugated indigoid chromophore and generate oxidized IC radical intermediates. The central C C bond is susceptible to oxidative transformation. Therefore, direct electron transfer can initiate the modification of the conjugated system, which depends on the electrode material and potential, followed by bond cleavage and further oxidation of the resulting intermediates. Recent electrochemical studies emphasize that the contribution of direct electron transfer depends strongly on electrode composition, applied potential, solution pH, and mass transport because IC must reach the anode surface for oxidation to occur. 145 Therefore, direct oxidation is most important for highly active anodes and conditions favoring pollutant adsorption and rapid interfacial electron transfer. Its main limitation is its surface-dependent nature. Electrode fouling, competitive adsorption, formation of polymeric surface films, and mass-transfer limitations can progressively reduce access of IC to active anodic sites.

In indirect electrochemical oxidation, IC does not need to remain in direct contact with the anode. Here, the electric current first generates soluble or diffusible oxidizing species, which subsequently migrate into the bulk solution and chemically oxidize IC. The most important example in chloride-containing wastewater is active chlorine-mediated oxidation. Chloride ions are initially oxidized at the anode according to 2Cl - → Cl 2 + 2e - . The electrogenerated Cl 2 subsequently hydrolyzes in water to form HOCl, which is in acid-base equilibrium with OCl - as Cl 2 + H 2 O ⇄ HOCl + H + + Cl - and HOCl ⇄ H + + OCl - . Therefore, the relative concentrations of Cl 2 , HOCl and OCl - depend strongly on the solution pH. These species collectively constitute active chlorine and act as secondary oxidants that attack IC predominantly in the bulk solution rather than requiring continuous dye adsorption at the electrode surface. IC-specific studies have demonstrated that chloride enhances electrochemical degradation because electrogenerated active chlorine provides an additional homogeneous oxidation pathway. 146,147

The nature of the supporting electrolyte can influence the electrochemical degradation of IC because the electrolyte determines not only the solution conductivity and mass transfer but also the identity of the oxidizing species generated at the anode. NaCl delivers a higher IC degradation efficiency than Na 2 SO 4 when chloride-mediated indirect oxidation is operative because anodic oxidation of Cl - generates active chlorine species through reactions, as discussed in the above paragraph. These electro-generated chlorine species can diffuse into the bulk solution and rapidly attack the electron-rich conjugated central C C chromophore of IC, which produces intermediates like isatin-5-sulfonic acid and subsequently smaller aliphatic oxidation products. Palma-Goyes et al. 146 conducted an IC-specific electrooxidation study using a Ti-IrO 2 -SnO 2 -Sb 2 O 3 dimensionally stable anode and found that chloride enhanced IC oxidation due to the electrogenerated active chlorine under optimized conditions of 0.25 M NaCl, pH 2 and 9.375 mA cm -2 , achieving complete color removal. They obtained a 24% TOC removal rate, which demonstrates that rapid decolorization does not indicate complete mineralization. On the other hand, Na 2 SO 4 is regarded as a comparatively inert supporting electrolyte because sulfate does not readily generate the same powerful, long-lived bulk oxidants as chloride under conventional anodic conditions. Therefore, IC degradation relies more strongly on direct electron transfer and/or surface-generated oxidizing species like ˙OH when Na 2 SO 4 is used, which depend on the anode material. Palma-Goyes et al. 148 also reported that chloride increased the dye-removal rate by 20-fold relative to Na 2 SO 4 and the oxidation rate by approximately six-fold, which demonstrates the strong catalytic contribution of active chlorine in chloride-containing media. Nevertheless, the apparent advantage of NaCl must be considered critically because chloride-mediated electrooxidation can generate undesirable chlorinated secondary products. Active chlorine can react not only with IC but also with its aromatic and aliphatic degradation intermediates, which produce chlorinated organic transformation products, while further electrochemical oxidation of active chlorine can form oxychlorine species like chlorate and perchlorate under appropriate conditions. Therefore, a higher IC decolorization efficiency in an NaCl-containing electrolyte does not correspond to superior environmental performance. Na 2 SO 4 (although less effective for rapid color removal) can be advantageous when the objective is to minimize chlorine-derived by-products and evaluate the intrinsic contribution of direct anodic oxidation or non-chlorine ROS. Therefore, NaCl favors rapid indirect oxidation through active chlorine, whereas Na 2 SO 4 provides a more chemically neutral background in which direct anodic oxidation and oxygen-derived reactive species become more important. Therefore, electrolyte selection should be based not only on the IC removal percentage but also on TOC/mineralization, transformation-product toxicity, chlorate/perchlorate formation, energy consumption and the composition of the real wastewater matrix.

The choice of the electrode material is critical for EO efficiency. Electrodes such as boron-doped diamond (BDD) provide high oxygen overpotential, minimal adsorption of intermediates, and excellent chemical stability, achieving almost complete mineralization of IC. 149-151 Other electrodes, such as PbO 2 , SnO 2 , 152 or Ti-based 153 materials, also generate ROS, but they suffer from surface fouling or lower oxidative potential, which can retard IC degradation or produce partial breakdown products. The electrolyte composition, pH, applied voltage, and current density also significantly influence the generation of ROS and the overall reaction kinetics.

The electrochemical degradation of IC is highly dependent on the applied current density ( j ) and/or cell voltage ( E cell ) because these parameters determine the rate of electron transfer, the generation of surface-bound and solution-phase oxidants, and ultimately the extent of mineralization. Therefore, the current density and applied voltage should not be treated as interchangeable parameters. Current density is calculated from the applied current divided by the geometrical electrode area ( j = I / A ) and directly determines the electron flux reaching the electrode. Increasing the current density increases the rate of water oxidation, chloride oxidation to active chlorine, and production of surface ˙OH and other electro-generated oxidants, hence accelerating IC disappearance. 154

The applied cell voltage should be interpreted differently. Increasing voltage increases the electrical potential driving force across the electrochemical cell and can increase current, but the resulting current density depends on the electrode spacing, electrode area, electrolyte conductivity, reactor geometry and solution resistance. Therefore, a voltage reported for one reactor cannot be directly compared with a current density reported for another reactor. This is important for IC studies employing low-voltage pulse electrolysis. 155

Tien et al. 145 reported a Ti-SnO 2 -Sb-Co-βPbO 2 electrode study, which found that IC reacts rapidly with several electro-generated oxidants, with reported second-order rate constants of 1.51 × 10 10 M -1 s -1 for ˙OH and 7.4 × 10 9 M -1 s -1 for SO 4 ˙ - , while direct electron transfer and multiple radical pathways contributed to degradation. Mosleh et al. 156 also reported a study on N-α-Fe 2 O 3 -G. The authors demonstrated that pH, supporting electrolyte, and water-matrix composition altered the relative contributions of these pathways. The N-α-Fe 2 O 3 -G system reported 98% IC degradation under electrocatalytic/photoelectrocatalytic conditions, which illustrates how improved electronic conductivity and interfacial charge transfer can enhance degradation without simply relying on increasing the applied electrical input.

EO has distinct advantages and limitations as compared with photocatalysis. Unlike photocatalysis, EO is independent of light, which makes it suitable for dark or turbid wastewater treatment. It achieves faster degradation rates because reactive species are generated directly at the electrode surface. EO is also highly controllable, which allows precise tuning of reaction rates by adjusting the applied potential or current. 147 However, its main limitations are high energy consumption and potential formation of undesirable byproducts, especially in chloride-containing waters (chlorinated intermediates). In comparison, photocatalysis provides energy savings under sunlight but is limited by light penetration, charge recombination, and dependence on catalyst properties.

EO is a robust, versatile, and scalable method for IC removal, particularly in industrial applications where high dye concentrations and complex matrices are present. Its efficiency can be further enhanced by combining it with catalytic anodes, flow reactors, or hybrid systems, which improve ROS generation, reduce energy consumption, and minimize by-product formation. Therefore, EO represents a complementary approach to photocatalysis, which shows rapid, light-independent, and complete dye mineralization, albeit at higher operational costs and with careful process control.

Biological degradation of IC relies on the metabolic activity of microorganisms and their enzymatic systems capable of reductive or oxidative breakdown of the highly stable aromatic and sulfonated structure of the dye. Microorganisms such as bacteria ( e.g. , Pseudomonas , 34 Bacillus , 157 Klebsiella 158 ), fungi ( e.g. , Trametes versicolor , 159 Phanerochaete chrysosporium 160 ), and yeasts 161 actively convert IC into smaller, less toxic compounds through enzymatic catalysis.

The efficiency of microbial degradation strongly depends on environmental conditions, like pH, temperature, oxygen availability, carbon/nutrient supplementation, and the presence of co-substrates (electron donors). For example, carbon-rich substrates, such as glucose, acetate, or molasses, enhance microbial electron transfer, which accelerates reductive cleavage of IC. 162 Similarly, fungi with robust ligninolytic enzyme systems, like laccase, 163 manganese peroxidase, 164 and lignin peroxidase, 165 display superior degradation capability due to their ability to generate non-specific oxidizing radicals capable of attacking even the sulfonated aromatic rings of IC. Fungal systems outperform bacteria in the mineralization step because their extracellular enzymes can break down complex aromatic intermediates that bacterial systems can leave behind.

The biodegradation of IC is fundamentally an enzyme-mediated redox process in which microorganisms use extracellular or intracellular oxidoreductases to transfer electrons between the dye and molecular oxygen or other electron acceptors. IC contains a highly conjugated indigoid π-system with a central C C bond and two sulfonated indole units, as discussed in the previous section. Enzymatic oxidation disrupts the electron-rich conjugated chromophore rather than simply removing the dye from solution. Among the enzymes involved, laccases (multicopper oxidases) are important because they catalyze the one-electron oxidation of suitable organic substrates while reducing O 2 to H 2 O. The laccase active site contains type-1, type-2 and type-3 copper centers. IC or a mediator transfers electrons to the type-1 Cu center, followed by intramolecular electron transfer to the trinuclear Cu cluster, where molecular oxygen is reduced to water. The resulting IC radical or mediator-derived radical undergoes oxidation and cleavage reactions, which disrupt the conjugated C C chromophore and produce smaller oxygenated and desulfonated molecules. Direct oxidation of IC by laccase can be kinetically limited because IC is not an ideal laccase substrate. Therefore, redox mediators like syringaldazine, syringaldehyde, acetosyringone or 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine-1-oxyl (TEMPO) can function as electron shuttles, which receive electrons from the enzyme and transfer oxidative equivalents to IC. Petrila et al. 166 reported a similar study. They used immobilized Trametes versicolor laccase and demonstrated that a mediator (particularly syringaldehyde) improved IC degradation, while immobilization improved enzyme stability and allowed complete discoloration over four reuse cycles. Sanachai et al. 167 explored Streptomyces salinarius CS29 laccase and showed that molecular interactions between the enzyme and IC contribute to decolorization, with van der Waals interactions and specific binding residues contributing to the stabilization of the dye-enzyme complex.

Peroxidases provide an oxidative pathway. Lignin peroxidase and manganese peroxidase utilize H 2 O 2 as an oxidizing co-substrate and generate highly oxidizing enzyme intermediates capable of abstracting electrons from aromatic pollutants. In the catalytic cycle, the resting ferric enzyme reacts with H 2 O 2 to form compound I (a high-valent oxoferryl species containing a porphyrin radical), and subsequent one-electron oxidation of the substrate converts compound I into compound II (ferryl-porphyrin complex). Then, compound II follows a second electron-transfer step that regenerates the resting enzyme. These successive one-electron oxidation reactions generate dye-centered radicals that can undergo cleavage of the conjugated indigo structure, hydroxylation and desulfonation. Therefore, peroxidases differ mechanistically from laccases because H 2 O 2 is directly involved in the catalytic cycle, whereas laccases use O 2 as the terminal electron acceptor. Evidence from biological IC degradation supports the involvement of several oxidative enzymes simultaneously. Adnan et al. 41 used Microbacterium zeae K5 during their study. IC degradation was accompanied by measurable activities of laccase (12.02 U g -1 ), manganese peroxidase (4.23 U g -1 ), and quinone dehydrogenase (0.09 U g -1 ), and the degradation products included isatin sulfonic acid and isatin. This finding is important because it demonstrates that IC biodegradation should not be attributed to one enzyme alone. Microorganisms can employ a concerted oxidative enzyme network in which different enzymes attack different stages or intermediates of IC transformation.

Biological degradation remains slower compared to advanced oxidation processes and can be inhibited by high dye concentration, ionic strength, or the presence of toxic heavy metals. IC itself is toxic to many microorganisms; it impairs cell membrane integrity and reduces enzymatic efficiency. Therefore, microbial acclimatization or the use of consortia (mixed cultures) is necessary to achieve stable biodegradation. 168,169 Immobilizing microorganisms on carriers such as alginate beads, biochar, or chitosan hydrogels improves stability, enhances enzyme expression, and tolerates higher pollutant loads. Genetic engineering of microorganisms or overexpression of key enzymes has also emerged as a strategy to overcome biological limitations, although economic and regulatory barriers still exist.

Biological degradation provides a green, low-cost, and sustainable approach for IC removal, but its full potential is realized only when combined with optimized operational conditions, immobilization techniques, or integrated anaerobic-aerobic bioreactor systems. While this technique is environmentally appealing and capable of complete mineralization, its inherent slow kinetics, sensitivity to operational parameters, and IC microbial toxicity remain the major constraints compared to advanced photocatalytic or electrochemical oxidation processes.

The individual treatment technologies used for IC removal have complementary strengths but also significant limitations. Adsorption provides rapid separation but transfers IC from water to a solid phase rather than destroying the molecule. Although photocatalysis and advanced oxidation can chemically degrade the indigoid chromophore, they are limited by electron-hole recombination, light attenuation and incomplete mineralization. Electrochemical oxidation provides controllable and rapid oxidation but requires electrical energy and can generate secondary oxidants or by-products. Biological treatment operates under relatively mild conditions with low energy requirements but is slower and sensitive to salinity, pH, temperature and toxic wastewater constituents. Therefore, integrated treatment systems are increasingly attractive because the weakness of one process can become the functional advantage of another. For IC, the most suitable combinations include adsorption-photocatalysis, sonophotocatalysis, photoelectrocatalysis, electrochemical-biological systems, and bio-electrochemical treatment.

The combination of adsorption and photocatalysis is very attractive because adsorption can rapidly concentrate IC near the catalytic surface, while photocatalytic reactions chemically destroy the adsorbed molecules. This eliminates one of the major disadvantages of conventional adsorption (the production of an IC-loaded spent adsorbent). Al-Wasidi et al. 170 reported a similar study using a chitosan-modified NiO-g-C 3 N 4 (CS-NiO-g-C 3 N 4 ) nanocomposite. The material exhibited a maximum IC adsorption capacity of 227 mg g -1 , which is 3 and 10 times higher than those for pure CS and g-C 3 N 4 , respectively, while the combined adsorption-photodegradation system achieved a synergistic elimination ratio of 91.1%. The authors attributed IC uptake to ionic attraction, van der Waals forces, π-π interactions, and hydrogen bonding, whereas visible light irradiation promoted a Z-scheme photocatalytic process and enhanced reactive oxygen species formation.

Rosaiah et al. 171 also provided another relevant example using a CuCo 2 O 4 -CuO-g-C 3 N 4 heterostructure. The material simultaneously functioned as an adsorbent and visible light photocatalyst, which achieved a Langmuir adsorption capacity of 53.54 mg g -1 and 97.60% IC discoloration within 90 min under visible light. They demonstrated that the heterostructure improved visible light harvesting and charge-carrier separation. These results demonstrate a main principle for integrated IC treatment: adsorption improves mass transfer and local pollutant concentration, while photocatalysis converts the captured pollutant into degradation products.

Sono-photocatalysis is one of the most convincing coupled systems because it combines acoustic cavitation with photocatalytic oxidation. Ultrasound generates cavitation bubbles whose collapse produces localized high temperature and pressure, promotes turbulence and improves mass transfer. It can also assist catalyst dispersion and reduce boundary-layer limitations. Simultaneously, irradiation activates the semiconductor and produces electron-hole pairs and ROS. Thus, ultrasound can address some of the mass-transfer and catalyst-surface limitations of photocatalysis.

Foroughi et al. 172 investigated IC removal using a zeolite 4A-WO 3 -CuO catalyst under photocatalytic, sonocatalytic and sonophotocatalytic conditions. Under optimized sonophotocatalytic conditions, IC removal reached 97.24% within 25 min using 0.15 g L -1 of catalyst, 10 mg L -1 of dye, pH 3 and H 2 O 2 . The reported IC rate constant was 0.1433 min -1 , and the calculated synergy value was 2.303, which demonstrates that the combined process performed better than the individual processes. The catalyst retained more than 90% of its performance after five cycles. Scavenger experiments indicated the involvement of ˙OH, e - , h + and ˙O 2 - .

This study is valuable because it provides quantitative evidence of synergy rather than merely assuming that combining two processes will improve degradation. However, the authors also found that chloride and phosphate ions reduced degradation and that performance declined in river and well water, which demonstrate that laboratory synergy does not necessarily translate directly into real wastewater performance.

Photoelectrochemical treatment combines the advantages of photocatalysis and electrochemical oxidation. Light generates electron-hole pairs in the photoelectrode, while an external electrical bias promotes charge separation and suppresses recombination. The improved electron transfer can increase ROS generation and hence accelerate IC degradation. This approach is suitable for IC because the dye's highly conjugated structure requires sustained oxidative attack after the initial chromophore cleavage.

Mirzajani et al. 155 studied sonochemically synthesized BiVO 4 -CeVO 4 -g-C 3 N 4 and developed a photoelectrode for IC treatment. The optimized system achieved 96.7% IC degradation, with the authors attributing its performance to favorable band alignment and enhanced charge mobility. Similarly, Anwar and his coworkers 154 studied biosynthesized ZnO/gum-arabic materials. They investigated both electrocatalytic and photoelectrocatalytic IC degradation, which illustrates the potential of coupling light activation with electrochemical charge transfer.

Therefore, the major advantage of photoelectrochemical systems is better utilization of photogenerated charge carriers, whereas their limitations include electrode fabrication costs, electrical energy consumption and possible electrode fouling. Thus, future research should compare these systems using not only percentage degradation but also TOC removal, mineralization current efficiency, energy consumption and catalyst/electrode stability.

Bio-electrochemical systems are very interesting because they combine microbial or enzymatic oxidation with electrochemical electron transfer. In principle, microorganisms or enzymes can provide selective biochemical oxidation, while the electrode acts as an electron acceptor/donor or regenerates redox-active species. This can overcome the relatively slow electron-transfer kinetics of some biological processes while avoiding the high energy demand of intensive chemical oxidation.

There is direct precedent for IC bio-electrochemical treatment. Garcia et al. 173 reported a similar study. A bio-electrochemical system employed microporous activated carbon fiber felt (ACFF) as both an electrode and an immobilization support for laccase. IC discoloration increased from 62.7% with the ACFF electrode to 83.6% with laccase-immobilized ACFF after 60 min. The importance of this study lies in conceptual integration. The conductive carbon support provides adsorption/electrochemical functionality, while immobilized laccase supplies enzymatic oxidation.

Recent IC biodegradation research strengthens the biological component. Microbacterium zeae K5 was shown to exhibit laccase and manganese peroxidase activities during IC degradation, which provide a biochemical basis for future bio-electrochemical designs.

A more realistic industrial treatment train can involve sequential rather than simultaneous coupling. For example, adsorption could initially remove the majority of IC and reduce the pollutant load, and photocatalytic/electrochemical oxidation could then destroy the recalcitrant indigoid structure and convert it into smaller oxygenated compounds. Finally, biological treatment could mineralize relatively biodegradable intermediates. Such a configuration can reduce the burden on each process and lower the overall energy consumption.

Biological degradation of IC is the most environmentally friendly method, which uses microorganisms to metabolize the dye into simpler compounds, but it suffers from slow kinetics, microbial inhibition at high dye concentrations, and dependency on optimal conditions (like pH, nutrients, and oxygen levels). Enzymatic treatment shows faster and more selective degradation than whole-cell biodegradation because isolated enzymes, such as laccase, peroxidase, and azoreductase, directly attack the chromophore of the dye, yet its high cost, instability, and need for cofactors limit its large-scale application. Electrochemical oxidation provides highly efficient mineralization of IC through in situ generation of strong oxidants (˙OH, O 3 , H 2 O 2 ), which allow complete degradation even in complex wastewater. However, it is energy-intensive, expensive, and susceptible to electrode fouling. Photocatalytic degradation stands out for its ability to generate reactive oxygen species under light irradiation, exhibiting strong oxidation power and complete mineralization, especially when advanced catalysts (Z-scheme, S-scheme, doped semiconductors) are used, though its performance is restricted by light availability, catalyst recombination losses, and the need for a precise reactor design. In contrast, adsorption is the simplest, fastest, and most cost-effective method, which relies on physical or chemical interactions with adsorbent surfaces ( e.g. , carbon materials, polymers, clays), but it only transfers IC from water to a solid material without destroying it and requires regeneration or secondary treatment. Adsorption is ideal for rapid removal, while photocatalysis and electrochemical oxidation ensure deeper degradation, and biological or enzymatic treatments are greener but slower alternatives. These highlight that the best method depends on whether the priority is speed, cost, sustainability, or complete mineralization.

INSTRUMENTS USED FOR THE IDENTIFICATION AND REMOVAL OF INDIGO CARMINE
The evaluation of IC removal from aqueous solutions relies on a suite of analytical, spectroscopic, microscopic, and electrochemical techniques that together provide quantitative, mechanistic, and structural information. UV-Visible spectrophotometry (UV-Vis) remains the primary method for evaluating IC concentration in solutions, which measures absorbance at the maximum wavelength of dye (610 nm) to determine the removal efficiency, adsorption capacity, and kinetic parameters. 68 Complementary to this, high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) separates IC from its degradation intermediates, providing detailed insights into photocatalytic, enzymatic, or microbial degradation pathways and confirming whether complete mineralization has been achieved. 180 Total organic carbon (TOC) analyses further differentiate between simple adsorption and actual breakdown of the dye molecules, providing a measure of the overall organic load removal. 169

To elucidate molecular interactions and functional group involvement, Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy identifies changes in hydroxyl, amino, carboxyl, and sulfonate groups, distinguishing physical adsorption, chemical bonding, or complexation mechanisms. 181 X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) provides elemental composition and chemical state information, confirming the presence of IC sulfur or nitrogen on adsorbent surfaces and detecting the formation of chemical bonds during chemisorption. 182 Raman spectroscopy is particularly useful for carbon-based materials, as it tracks π-π interactions and structural changes during dye adsorption or photocatalysis. Zeta potential analysis monitors the surface charge of adsorbents, biological matrices, or catalysts, and it is critical for predicting electrostatic interactions under different pH conditions and correlating with adsorption efficiency.

The morphology and structure of adsorbents, photocatalysts, and hybrid materials are characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM), which reveal surface roughness, porosity, particle size, and distribution. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) coupled with SEM confirms the elemental composition and the adsorption of IC by detecting sulfur and other dye-derived elements. For crystalline materials, X-ray diffraction (XRD) determines crystallinity and phase stability, while Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface area analysis quantifies specific surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution, directly linking structural properties to adsorption capacity. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) is employed to examine the thermal stability of polymeric or hybrid adsorbents before and after IC removal, which ensures material integrity for multiple cycles. 39

In photocatalytic systems, photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy is used to evaluate the electron-hole recombination rate, which directly correlates with the photocatalytic efficiency. 112 Fluorescence spectroscopy detects ROS like ˙OH and O 2 ˙ - , which confirm oxidative degradation pathways. Electrochemical techniques, like cyclic voltammetry (CV), 183 linear sweep voltammetry (LSV), 184 and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), 185 assess the kinetics and mechanism of IC oxidation on electrodes, which provide insights into electron transfer, active site efficiency, and electrode stability.

For biological systems, enzymatic activity assays ( e.g. , laccase or peroxidase) quantify IC degradation rates, while microbial performance is monitored through optical density (OD 600 ), plate counts, confocal microscopy, and scanning electron imaging to evaluate biomass growth, biofilm formation, and dye uptake. 186 Advanced techniques, such as nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, can characterize IC degradation products, which confirm the cleavage of azo or aromatic bonds in enzymatic or photocatalytic treatments. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) is sometimes used to measure particle-size distribution in nanoparticle-based adsorbents, which influences diffusion rates and adsorption efficiency. 94

The integration of these instruments provides a multidimensional understanding of IC removal, which enables researchers to differentiate between physical adsorption, chemical adsorption, pore-filling, photocatalytic oxidation, electrochemical degradation, and biological or enzymatic mineralization. By correlating structural properties, surface chemistry, reaction kinetics, and degradation pathways, these techniques guide the rational design of high-performance adsorbents, photocatalysts, and biocatalysts, ensuring optimized IC removal under variable operational and environmental conditions. Moreover, such comprehensive characterization provides mechanistic insights and enables reproducibility, which are essential for translating laboratory-scale studies into industrial-scale wastewater treatment applications.

MATERIALS USED FOR IC REMOVAL
A variety of materials have been employed for the efficient removal of IC, which are categorized broadly as inorganic substances, biological polymers, synthetic polymers, and semiconductors, each with distinct mechanisms and performance characteristics.

Inorganic materials are widely employed for the removal of IC due to their high stability, tunable surface properties, and versatile mechanisms, like adsorption, ion-exchange, and photocatalysis. Carbon-based materials (activated carbon, 97 biochar, 96 and graphene derivatives 36,187 ) show exceptionally high surface area and porosity, enabling strong π-π interactions with the aromatic rings of IC and electrostatic interactions with sulfonate groups, which result in high adsorption capacities and rapid removal kinetics. Zeolites 82 and mesoporous silica 188 act primarily through ion-exchange and pore-filling mechanisms, which effectively trap IC molecules within their structured frameworks. Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) (combined metal nodes with organic linkers) 189 provide both high surface area and catalytic activity, which make them effective for adsorption-coupled photocatalytic degradation.

Carbonaceous materials are among the most effective and widely studied adsorbents for the removal of IC, owing to their high surface area, tunable porosity, and rich surface chemistry. This class includes graphene ( Fig. 9(A) ), 36 graphene oxide (GO) ( Fig. 9(B) ), 134 reduced graphene oxide (RGO) ( Fig. 9(C) ), 190 activated carbon (AC) ( Fig. 9(D) ), 98 biochar ( Fig. 9(E) ), 191 carbon nanotubes (CNTs) ( Fig. 9(F) ), 192 and graphite. 109 Each has distinct advantages and mechanisms of IC removal.

Graphene and its derivatives (GO, RGO) exhibit exceptional adsorption performance due to their two-dimensional π-conjugated structures, high specific surface area, and functional groups, like -OH, -COOH, and -C O, as shown in Fig. 9(A-C) , respectively. 36,187,193 GO (with abundant oxygen-containing groups) provides strong hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions with the sulfonate groups of IC, while RGO (being more hydrophobic and conductive) enhances π-π stacking interactions with IC aromatic rings. Graphene-based materials also facilitate electron transfer in hybrid photocatalytic systems, which combine adsorption with oxidative degradation for improved efficiency.

Activated carbon (AC) (produced from coal, wood, or biomass) is widely used due to its large microporous and mesoporous structure, as shown in Fig. 9(D) , which allows high-capacity physical adsorption through van der Waals forces, pore-filling, and electrostatic interactions. The adsorption capacity of AC can vary depending on the pore-size distribution and surface functionalization, making it highly versatile. 97,98,194

Biochar and hydrochar are carbon-rich materials produced from biomass, but they are different in terms of preparation conditions and resulting physicochemical properties. Biochar is produced through pyrolysis of biomass under oxygen-limited conditions and at high temperatures (300-800 °C), 195 whereas hydrochar is produced through hydrothermal carbonization (HTC) of wet biomass in water under autogenous pressure, commonly at lower temperatures (180-280 °C) compared to the biochar synthesis temperature. 196 Therefore, biochar formation involves substantial dehydration, devolatilization, decarboxylation, and aromatization, whereas hydrothermal carbonization proceeds through hydrolysis, decarboxylation, dehydration, polymerization, and condensation reactions. This difference is important for wastewater treatment because the resulting materials exhibit markedly different surface characteristics. Biochar possesses a more aromatic and carbonized structure, greater structural stability, and higher specific surface area under proper activation and pore volume conditions (as shown in Fig. 9(E) ) compared to untreated hydrochar. Increasing the pyrolysis temperature increases aromaticity and surface area while decreasing oxygen-containing functional groups. Therefore, biochar can provide abundant micropores and mesopores that favor pore filling and physical adsorption of organic pollutants. Highly pyrolyzed biochar can lose polar functional groups, like hydroxyl, carboxyl, and carbonyl groups, which can reduce its ability to interact with certain pollutants through hydrogen bonding or specific surface complexation. On the other hand, hydrochar contains more oxygen-containing surface functional groups and retains a greater fraction of the original biomass structure because HTC occurs at lower temperatures. Therefore, it is more hydrophilic and polar than conventional pyrolytic biochar. Hydrochar can provide carboxyl, carbonyl, hydroxyl, and other oxygenated groups that participate in hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interactions, and other specific interactions with contaminants. Pristine hydrochar has a lower surface area and less developed pores than activated or high-temperature biochar. Thus, hydrochar can provide stronger chemical/specific interactions per unit accessible surface, whereas highly porous biochar can provide greater physical adsorption capacity. 89,197,198 The difference becomes important for IC because IC is a highly water-soluble, sulfonated, predominantly anionic dye. Therefore, the adsorption mechanism depends strongly on surface charge, porosity, functional groups, pH, and ionic strength. At acidic pH values, the protonation of oxygen-containing groups on biochar or hydrochar can increase the positive surface charge and promote electrostatic attraction toward anionic IC. At higher pH values, deprotonation makes the carbon surface more negatively charged, which increases electrostatic repulsion and decreases IC adsorption. 199 Hydrochar's greater abundance of oxygenated groups can provide stronger hydrogen-bonding and specific interactions, whereas the well-developed biochar pores can promote pore filling and π-π interactions with the aromatic chromophore of IC. However, neither material should be assumed to outperform the other universally. Their relative performance depends on the pyrolysis/HTC conditions, biomass precursor, activation, pore accessibility, surface charge, and solution chemistry.

Carbon nanotubes (CNTs) (both single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNTs) and multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs)) show high surface area, tubular porosity (shown in Fig. 9(F) ), and strong π-π interactions with IC molecules. 200-202 CNTs are particularly effective in rapid adsorption kinetics due to their accessible tubular channels but are more expensive than AC or biochar. Although graphite (being crystalline and less porous) exhibits lower adsorption capacity compared to other carbonaceous materials, it still exhibits activity through π-π stacking and surface interactions.

Graphene derivatives generally exhibit the highest adsorption capacity due to a combination of π-π interactions, hydrogen bonding, and a large surface area, followed by CNTs and activated carbon. Biochar and hydrochar are moderately effective but highly sustainable, while graphite shows the lowest capacity due to its limited surface area and porosity. The choice of material depends on cost, reusability, sustainability, and specific adsorption requirements.

The adsorption of IC on carbonaceous materials is influenced by pH, temperature, initial dye concentration, ionic strength, and surface functionalization. 31,203,204 At low pH values, the surface of carbon materials becomes protonated, which enhances electrostatic attraction to the anionic sulfonate groups of IC, while at high pH values, repulsion can reduce adsorption due to the identical charges on the dye and adsorbent network. Temperature can affect both the adsorption capacity and kinetics. Physical adsorption dominates at lower temperatures through van der Waals forces, whereas higher temperatures can enhance chemical interactions or diffusion into mesopores. An increase in the initial IC concentration enhances the driving force for adsorption initially up to a specific level (saturated active sites), and after this level, there is no increase in the adsorption capacity due to the poor availability of active sites for adsorption. Co-existing ions or salts can compete with IC for adsorption sites or screen electrostatic interactions, modifying the adsorption mechanism from electrostatic to primarily π-π stacking or pore-filling.

Carbonaceous materials are highly versatile, allowing the tuning of the adsorption capacity and mechanism through functionalization, hybridization with metal oxides or polymers, and structural engineering, which place them among the most effective and widely applied materials for IC removal. Graphene derivatives outperform other carbonaceous materials in adsorption capacity and kinetic efficiency, while sustainable alternatives, like biochar and hydrochar, provide eco-friendly, cost-effective solutions, particularly when hybridized with photocatalysts or polymers for synergistic removal.

Beyond carbonaceous materials and metal oxides, other inorganic materials, such as metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) ( Fig. 10(A) ), 205 zeolites (ZL) ( Fig. 10(B) ), 32 mesoporous silica (MPS) ( Fig. 10(C) ), 188 layered double hydroxides (LDHs) ( Fig. 10(D) ), 206 and clay minerals 204 have been extensively investigated for IC removal due to their high surface area, tunable pore structure, chemical stability, and versatile adsorption mechanisms.

MOFs are crystalline hybrid materials composed of metal nodes coordinated with organic linkers (as shown in Fig. 10(A) ), which produce highly porous frameworks with tunable surface chemistry. IC molecules are adsorbed through π-π stacking with aromatic linkers, hydrogen bonding with functional groups, and electrostatic interactions between the sulfonate groups of IC and positively charged metal nodes. 37,95,99 MOFs in composite form exhibit photocatalytic activity, generating ROS under light to degrade IC. MOFs show higher adsorption capacity and selectivity compared to conventional adsorbents due to precise pore size control and functionalization. However, their chemical stability under acidic/basic conditions (due to a decrease in the complexation of ligands with metals in the protonated or deprotonated medium) and synthesis cost can limit their large-scale applications. The functionalization of MOFs with amino or hydroxyl groups enhances their adsorption performance and pH tolerance.

Zeolites are crystalline, microporous aluminosilicate materials, whose framework is primarily constructed from aluminum (Al), silicon (Si), and oxygen (O). The basic structural units are tetrahedrally coordinated SiO 4 and AlO 4 units, which are linked through shared oxygen atoms to produce a three-dimensional framework containing interconnected channels and cavities. Zeolites (crystalline aluminosilicates with uniform micropores) remove IC predominantly through ion-exchange and electrostatic attraction between anionic dye molecules and positively charged cation sites in the framework. Zeolites exhibit excellent chemical stability and reusability, which make them suitable for repeated cycles. However, the limited pore size of microporous zeolites can restrict the diffusion of large IC molecules, which reduces adsorption efficiency compared to mesoporous materials or carbonaceous adsorbents. Performance can be improved by modifying the surface charge or creating hierarchical structures with mesopores to enhance mass transfer. 82,185,207,208

Mesoporous silica materials (SBA-15, MCM-41, and KIT-6) provide ordered mesopores and high surface areas, which facilitate IC adsorption through pore-filling, hydrogen bonding, and electrostatic interactions. Functionalization with amino, hydroxyl, or thiol groups increases their electrostatic attraction to the anionic sulfonate groups of IC and can enhance the adsorption capacity. 188,201,209,210 Mesoporous silica is particularly effective for larger dye molecules compared to microporous zeolites. Its weak intrinsic photocatalytic activity can be overcome by hybridizing with metal oxides or semiconductors, combining adsorption and catalytic degradation.

Layered double hydroxides (LDHs) have a fundamentally different structure. Their positively charged hydroxide layers generally consist of a mixture of divalent metal cations (M 2+ ) and trivalent metal cations (M 3+ ) coordinated octahedrally by hydroxyl groups. M 2+ can include Mg 2+ , Zn 2+ , Ni 2+ , Co 2+ , Cu 2+ , or Ca 2+ , while M 3+ commonly includes Al 3+ , Fe 3+ , Cr 3+ , or Mn 3+ . The substitution of M 2+ by M 3+ generates a net positive charge in the hydroxide layers. This positive charge is compensated by exchangeable interlayer anions (A n - ) like CO 3 2- , Cl - , NO 3 - , SO 4 2- , and organic anions, together with interlayer water molecules. Layered double hydroxides are lamellar materials consisting of positively charged metal hydroxide layers and interlayer anions, which remove IC through anion exchange and electrostatic interactions. Adsorption efficiency depends on layer composition, interlayer spacing, and pH. 211-213 LDHs are reusable and environmentally safe, but their adsorption capacity is moderate compared to that of MOFs or mesoporous silica. The intercalation of IC into LDH layers can also retard kinetics if diffusion is limited.

Clay minerals (bentonite, kaolinite, montmorillonite) are naturally abundant, low-cost adsorbents. IC removal occurs via electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and surface complexation. While clays are environmentally safe and easily available, their adsorption capacity is lower than that of engineered materials, and their performance can vary with mineral type, particle size, and surface functionalization. 125,183,204 Chemical or thermal modification of clays can significantly enhance IC adsorption by increasing surface area or introducing additional functional groups.

MOFs exhibit the highest tunable adsorption capacity and selectivity, followed by mesoporous silica and LDHs. Zeolites provide stability and ion-exchange selectivity, whereas clays are cost-effective but less efficient. Mechanistically, adsorption on these materials is highly pH-dependent. At low pH, protonation of surface groups enhances electrostatic attraction to the anionic IC, while at high pH, repulsion reduces adsorption. Temperature can influence the kinetics and adsorption capacity, with physical adsorption dominating at lower temperatures and chemisorption or ion exchange dominating at higher temperatures. Co-existing ions at varying ionic strengths can compete with IC for adsorption sites, which modifies the mechanism from electrostatic-dominated to hydrogen bonding or pore-filling.

These inorganic materials provide a versatile platform for IC removal, and each has its strengths and limitations. MOFs and mesoporous silica are highly effective for high-capacity adsorption and hybrid catalytic systems, while LDHs, zeolites, and clays are economical and stable, suitable for large-scale or environmentally sensitive applications. Optimal IC removal involves the hybridization of these materials with carbonaceous adsorbents or semiconductors to combine adsorption, selectivity, and photocatalytic degradation, achieving fast, efficient, and sustainable dye removal.

Organic polymers represent a versatile class of adsorbents for IC due to their functional group diversity, tunable structure, and ability to form hybrid systems with inorganic or carbonaceous materials. They can be categorized broadly into synthetic polymers and biological polymers, and each exhibit distinct advantages and limitations.

Synthetic polymers are man-made macromolecules designed with specific functional groups and structural properties to enhance the adsorption of dyes, such as IC. They are widely used due to their tunable surface chemistry, ease of functionalization, and ability to form hybrid systems with carbonaceous or inorganic materials. Polyacrylonitrile (PAN) and polyacrylic acid (PAA) are common examples, containing nitrile (-C Created by potrace 1.16, written by Peter Selinger 2001-2019 N) and carboxyl (-COOH) groups that interact electrostatically with the anionic sulfonate groups of IC. PAN can be fabricated into fibers or beads with a high surface area, which provide rapid adsorption kinetics, while PAA hydrogels swell in water, which enhances accessibility to active sites and facilitates hydrogen-bonding interactions.

Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) is another widely used synthetic polymer that forms hydrogels that allow IC molecules to diffuse into the polymer network. Adsorption occurs primarily through hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions. 38,214,215 PVA also serves as an excellent support for immobilizing nanoparticles or enzymes, which enable hybrid adsorption-catalysis systems. Conductive polymers, such as polyaniline (PANI) 67 and polypyrrole (PPy), 216 provide an additional mechanism for IC removal through π-π stacking interactions with the aromatic rings of IC, while their conductivity facilitates electron transfer in photocatalytic or electrochemical hybrid systems. These polymers are coated on carbonaceous or inorganic supports to enhance both adsorption capacity and catalytic degradation efficiency.

PAN 217 and PAA 67 exhibit high adsorption capacity due to electrostatic interactions, while PVA offers moderate capacity but benefits from hydrogel swelling and functional versatility. Conductive polymers like PANI and PPy outperform others in hybrid systems due to their combined π-π stacking and catalytic capabilities. However, synthetic polymers generally have moderate adsorption capacity compared to carbonaceous materials, MOFs, or functionalized biological polymers. Excessive polymer thickness or dense crosslinking can hinder mass transfer, which reduces efficiency in high-concentration dye solutions.

The adsorption mechanism of IC on synthetic polymers is strongly influenced by environmental conditions. Low pH promotes protonation of polymer functional groups, which enhances electrostatic attraction to IC, whereas high pH reduces adsorption due to electrostatic repulsion. Temperature affects both diffusion and hydrogen bonding, with moderate increases improving dye diffusion, while excessive heat can weaken hydrogen bonding. Co-existing ions or salts compete with IC for adsorption sites, which potentially shift the dominant mechanism from electrostatic attraction to hydrogen bonding or π-π stacking. The synthetic polymers provide versatile, tunable, and processable materials for IC removal, and their integration into hybrid systems with carbonaceous or inorganic materials can significantly enhance removal efficiency, kinetics, and reusability for practical wastewater treatment.

Biological polymers are naturally derived macromolecules (like chitosan, alginate, and cellulose derivatives) widely used for IC removal due to their eco-friendliness, renewability, biodegradability, and functional versatility. Chitosan (obtained from chitin) contains amine (-NH 2 ) and hydroxyl (-OH) groups, which interact strongly with IC through electrostatic attraction, hydrogen bonding, and chelation. 80,218 Its adsorption capacity is particularly high under acidic conditions, where the amine groups are protonated, which creates strong attraction to the anionic sulfonate groups of IC. Chitosan can also be crosslinked or modified with nanoparticles, carbonaceous materials, or metal oxides to enhance mechanical strength, stability, and adsorption efficiency, which makes it suitable for both batch and continuous wastewater treatment systems. 203,219,220

Alginate differs structurally from both chitosan and cellulose because it is a natural anionic polysaccharide composed mainly of two uronic-acid monomers, namely β- d -mannuronic acid (M units) and α- l -guluronic acid (G units), linked through (1-4) glycosidic bonds. The carboxylate groups (-COO - ) of these uronic-acid residues give alginate an intrinsically negative charge under neutral and alkaline pH conditions. Therefore, pristine alginate does not provide the same electrostatic attraction for anionic IC that protonated chitosan provides. IC adsorption onto alginate can involve hydrogen bonding and other non-electrostatic interactions, while chemical modification, protonation, incorporation of cationic species, or formation of metal-alginate networks can introduce positively charged or coordination-active sites that improve IC uptake. The high density of -COO - groups also allows alginate to coordinate multivalent cations like Ca 2+ , which produce cross-linked “egg-box” structures. These metal-cross-linked structures can modify porosity, swelling, surface charge and accessibility of adsorption sites. Hence, alginate's IC-removal behavior is strongly dependent on the M/G ratio, degree of cross-linking, pH, ionic environment, and incorporation of metal or cationic components. 221-223 Cellulose and its derivatives, like carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) and hydroxypropyl cellulose (HPC), exhibit hydroxyl-rich surfaces for hydrogen bonding and van der Waals interactions with IC. 224,225 Surface modification of cellulose can further enhance the adsorption capacity and stability while maintaining biodegradability and biocompatibility.

Chitosan is a linear polysaccharide consisting predominantly of β-(1-4)-linked d -glucosamine (GlcN) units, together with a variable fraction of N -acetyl- d -glucosamine (GlcNAc) units resulting from the incomplete deacetylation of chitin. Its most important functional group for IC removal is the primary amino group (-NH 2 ). In acidic solutions, these groups become protonated (-NH 3 + ), providing strong electrostatic attraction toward the negatively charged sulfonate groups (-SO 3 - ) of IC. Therefore, chitosan can exhibit strong IC uptake under acidic conditions through ion-pair formation, while hydrogen bonding can provide additional stabilization. As the pH increases, the -NH 3 + groups are progressively converted into neutral -NH 2 , which reduces electrostatic attraction and decreases IC adsorption. 226 Hence, the degree of deacetylation, molecular weight, amino-group density, protonation state, and porosity of chitosan are critical parameters. Therefore, chitosan is chemically distinct from cellulose because it contains nitrogen-containing amino groups capable of pH-dependent protonation, whereas cellulose does not. This explains why chitosan-based materials can show stronger affinity toward anionic IC under acidic conditions.

Cellulose is a linear homopolysaccharide composed exclusively of β- d -glucose units connected through β-(1-4)-glycosidic bonds. Each glucose unit contains three hydroxyl groups (-OH), which give cellulose a highly hydrogen-bonded structure and strong hydrophilicity. Unlike chitosan, cellulose has no amino groups and, therefore, does not become strongly positively charged through protonation under normal aqueous conditions. Its interaction with IC consequently relies primarily on hydrogen bonding, dipole interactions, van der Waals interactions, and, when cellulose is chemically modified or incorporated with aromatic/carbonaceous phases, π-related interactions. Therefore, native cellulose can exhibit weaker electrostatic attraction toward anionic IC than protonated chitosan. Cellulose nanofibers, oxidized cellulose, nanocrystals, cellulose aerogels, and cellulose-based composites can provide a much greater accessible surface area and additional functional groups, which improve IC uptake. 227-229 Moderate temperature increases improve the diffusion of IC into polymer matrices, whereas high temperatures can weaken hydrogen bonds. Although a high initial dye concentration increases the driving force for adsorption, it leads to the saturation of active sites, which reduces efficiency. Co-existing ions or salts can compete for adsorption sites, which potentially shifts the dominant mechanism from electrostatic attraction to hydrogen bonding or van der Waals interactions. 203,220,230,231

Biological polymers also excel in hybrid systems, where their functional groups facilitate anchoring of carbonaceous materials, metal oxides, or MOFs, combining high adsorption capacity with catalytic degradation. For example, chitosan-coated graphene or alginate-MOF composites integrate electrostatic adsorption, π-π interactions, and photocatalytic activity, enabling rapid, efficient, and sustainable IC removal. These hybrids overcome the limitations of pure biological polymers (such as moderate adsorption capacity and lower mechanical stability) while retaining their eco-friendly and renewable characteristics, which make them highly suitable for industrial wastewater applications.

Semiconductors (metal oxides, metal sulfides, doped semiconductors, and their composites) are among the most effective materials for the photocatalytic degradation of IC due to their ability to generate electron-hole pairs upon light irradiation, producing reactive oxygen species like ˙OH, ˙O 2 - , and singlet oxygen. These ROS are highly reactive and can oxidize IC molecules into smaller, less toxic products, which ultimately leads to mineralization.

Metal oxides are extensively studied because of their chemical stability, low cost, and high surface area. TiO 2 (ref. 232 ) is a benchmark photocatalyst effective under UV light due to its wide band gap (3.2 eV), whereas ZnO 68 and Fe 3 O 4 (ref. 233 ) have narrower band gaps and can be modified for visible light activation. The adsorption of IC on metal-oxide surfaces occurs through electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and π-π interactions, which increase the proximity of dye molecules to ROS generation sites and hence enhance degradation. Fe 3 O 4 also provides magnetic recoverability, which enables easy separation after treatment.

Metal sulfides have narrower band gaps (1.2-2.4 eV), which make them active under visible light and more efficient for IC degradation in sunlight. MoS 2 (with its layered structure) facilitates large surface area adsorption and strong π-π interactions with the aromatic rings of IC. However, metal sulfides suffer from photocorrosion and leaching, which can release toxic ions and hence limit their long-term use. Stabilization through hybridization with polymers, carbon materials, or other oxides improves both stability and reusability. 113,234-236

Composites with polymers 38,237 or carbonaceous materials like graphene, 36 GO, 238 RGO, 239 or chitosan 240 further enhance photocatalytic performance. These materials increase the adsorption of IC through π-π stacking, hydrogen bonding, and electrostatic interactions, which enable the dye molecules to approach the active semiconductor surface. They also serve as electron sinks that promote charge transfer and reduce the recombination of electron-hole pairs. For instance, chitosan-functionalized ZnO/graphene 241 composites combine strong IC adsorption with enhanced photocatalytic degradation, which provides synergistic performance in aqueous solutions. 238,242

Doped semiconductors 243 and heterojunctions 244 exhibit the highest photocatalytic efficiency due to their reduced electron-hole recombination and extended light-absorption range, whereas pristine metal oxides are more stable but limited under visible light. Metal sulfides excel under sunlight but require stabilization to prevent leaching. 245 Hybrid composites with polymers 38 or carbonaceous 246 materials provide dual advantages of adsorption and catalytic activity, which enhance both the degradation rate and overall efficiency.

Environmental conditions significantly affect the photocatalytic IC removal efficiency. pH influences surface charge. Low pH favors protonation and electrostatic attraction, which enhance adsorption and ROS-mediated degradation, whereas high pH can reduce adsorption due to repulsion. 68,109 Temperature affects dye diffusion and ROS generation. Moderate increases in temperature improve mass transfer, but excessively high temperatures can reduce hydrogen bonding. The initial dye concentration impacts the driving force. At higher IC concentrations, the degradation rate increases initially but plateaus as the active sites of the adsorbent reach saturation. After saturation, there is no further increase in the degradation rate of IC. 36 Co-existing ions or salts can compete with IC for adsorption sites, which alters the dominant adsorption mechanisms from electrostatic interaction to π-π stacking or hydrogen bonding. 247

Hybrid semiconductor systems integrating metal oxides, metal sulfides, doped materials, heterojunctions, and polymer/carbonaceous composites represent the most efficient and sustainable approach for IC removal. They deliver rapid adsorption, which enhances light absorption, reduces recombination, and improves catalytic degradation, enabling effective treatment of industrial wastewater under both UV and visible light. These systems provide tunability, reusability, and eco-friendly operation, which position them as superior alternatives to standalone adsorbents or photocatalysts.

Biological materials provide sustainable and eco-friendly approaches for the removal of IC through biosorption and biodegradation mechanisms. They are increasingly studied because of their renewability, selectivity, and potential for complete dye mineralization, which chemical adsorbents alone cannot achieve.

Microorganisms like bacteria, 161 fungi, 34 enzymes, 248 and algae 249 remove IC through biosorption onto cell walls or intracellular degradation.

Bacterial species like Pseudomonas aeruginosa , 161 Bacillus pacificus , 157 and Enterobacter interact with IC through electrostatic attraction, hydrogen bonding, and van der Waals forces between functional groups on their cell surfaces ( e.g. , amino, carboxyl, phosphate) and the anionic sulfonate groups of IC. 159 Some bacteria can enzymatically degrade IC by converting it into less toxic intermediates. Bacteria are advantageous due to their rapid growth, adaptability, and potential for use in continuous bioreactors, but their efficiency is affected by environmental stresses, such as pH, temperature, and high dye concentration.

Fungal species such as Aspergillus niger 250 and Trametes versicolor 251 can remove IC through biosorption on hyphal surfaces and enzymatic biodegradation. Fungi produce extracellular enzymes like laccases and peroxidases that degrade IC. Their filamentous structures provide a high surface area, which facilitates adsorption, and the ability to tolerate a wide range of dye concentrations. 252 However, fungal systems can have slower kinetics compared to bacteria and require controlled conditions for optimal growth and enzyme activity.

Algae such as Spirulina platensis and Chlorella vulgaris remove IC primarily through biosorption on cell walls rich in polysaccharides, proteins, and lipids. The anionic groups of IC interact electrostatically with cationic sites on algal biomass. Algae are inexpensive, renewable, and exhibit high adsorption efficiency, but their performance can be limited in large-scale wastewater systems due to biomass settling and mass-transfer limitations. 253

Enzymatic systems generally provide faster and more selective IC degradation under controlled conditions, with minimal secondary pollution. 254,255 Microbial systems (especially bacteria) show dual functionality, combining adsorption with enzymatic or intracellular degradation, and they are suitable for continuous bioprocesses. Fungi excel in high-surface-area adsorption and extracellular enzyme production but have slow kinetics. Algae are cost-effective and eco-friendly, but their large-scale adoption is limited by biomass handling.

Microorganisms and enzymatic systems provide complementary approaches for IC removal, where microorganisms provide sustainable biosorption and biodegradation, and enzymes provide selective, rapid catalytic degradation. Hybrid systems (where enzymes are immobilized on microbial biomass or biopolymers) combine the advantages of both, achieving higher efficiency, reusability, and complete IC mineralization. These advantages make them highly suitable for industrial wastewater treatment applications.

The efficiency of each material (inorganic materials, organic polymers, biological polymers, semiconductors, and biological materials) for the removal of IC from different media is different, and their performance conditions are different.

FACTORS ENHANCING THE REMOVAL EFFICIENCY OF MATERIALS
The removal efficiency of IC is strongly influenced by a combination of solution conditions, operational parameters, and material properties. Understanding these factors is crucial for designing effective adsorption, photocatalytic, and biological systems that achieve rapid, high-capacity, and sustainable dye removal.

pH is arguably the most critical parameter because it dictates the surface charge of adsorbents or biological materials and the ionization state of IC molecules. 38,183,256,257

IC contains two sulfonate groups whose acidic character causes them to remain predominantly deprotonated in aqueous solutions. Braz et al. 258 reported a structural/DFT analysis, which confirmed that IC 2- is the dominant species over the relevant aqueous pH range, while the amine groups have p K a values above 11. Therefore, IC should be treated as a dianionic dye rather than assuming that its charge changes dramatically from neutral to anionic across the normal experimental pH range.

The critical parameter on the adsorbent side is the point of zero charge (pH pzc ). When pH < pH pzc , the protonation of surface hydroxyl, carboxylate, amino, or other ionizable groups gives the surface a net positive charge. This generates favorable electrostatic attraction between the positively charged adsorbent and IC 2- , particularly its sulfonate groups. Therefore, when pH > pH pzc , surface deprotonation increases the negative charge of the adsorbent, which promotes electrostatic repulsion with IC 2- and decreases adsorption. This relationship has been experimentally demonstrated for IC adsorbents by ElKammah. 19 They reported that NAMOSBC exhibited a pH pzc of 6.8 and a maximum IC removal rate of 97.5% at pH 4, of which adsorption decreased markedly above pH 7.

Similarly, pH affects the functional groups of adsorbents. So, at acidic pH, the protonation of functional groups, such as amines in chitosan, hydroxyls in cellulose, or surface hydroxyls on metal oxides, enhances electrostatic attraction, which results in a higher adsorption capacity. At alkaline pH, deprotonation reduces electrostatic interactions, which leads to repulsion and decreased removal efficiency. In enzymatic and microbial systems, pH also affects enzyme ionization, microbial metabolism, and ROS generation, influencing both the adsorption and biodegradation rates. A carefully optimized pH range is thus essential for maximizing IC removal in diverse systems. Azadikhah et al. 203 used a crosslinked chitosan-Fe 3 O 4 composite for adsorptive removal of IC at different pH values (4, 7, 9). They obtained 98.9% adsorptive removal of IC at pH 4, but this removal percentage decreased continuously with increasing the pH of the medium. At pH 4, the structures of the composites have a positive charge due to the protonation of amine groups as -NH 3 + . In this form, the composites have the opposite charge relative to IC (negatively charged structure).

Therefore, the maximum interaction is present between composites and IC molecules. On increasing the pH, this protonated form (-NH 3 + ) of the composite shifts to the deprotonated form (-NH 2 ), which results in decreased interaction; consequently, the removal percentage decreases with increasing pH of the medium. If the semiconductor or composite does not contain amine or carboxylic groups in its structure, then no such effect of interactions is present between them. In this case, the photocatalytic degradation rate increases with the pH of the medium because of the greater production of hydroxyl radicals from hydroxide ions. Banu et al. 109 studied the photocatalytic degradation of IC with NiO-graphitic carbon nitrite (NiO-g-CN) under different pH conditions, as shown in Fig. 11 .

Initially, the photocatalytic degradation efficiency of IC decreases on shifting the pH from 3 to 5, due to the decrease in the content of H + ions. Basically, the protons react with ˙O 2 - radicals to form HOO˙. In this way, the proton increases the production of ˙O 2 - radicals, which also degrade the IC dye molecules. After increasing this pH value, the degradation efficiency increased again due to the greater formation of ˙OH radicals. The OH - ions (which are produced by the addition of a base) react with the holes of the VB to produce ˙OH radicals. These radicals rapidly degrade the IC dye from the medium. Therefore, the maximum degradation efficiency was obtained at pH 12.

Temperature affects both the kinetics and thermodynamics of IC removal. Moderate increases in temperature enhance the diffusion of IC molecules into polymer matrices, pores of inorganic adsorbents, or microbial biofilms, which accelerates adsorption or biodegradation. 31,40,67 In photocatalysis, higher temperatures can slightly improve ROS formation and electron-hole mobility, hence increasing performance efficiency. However, excessive temperatures can denature enzymes, weaken hydrogen bonds in polymers, or destabilize microbial cells, leading to decreased efficiency. Therefore, maintaining an optimal temperature range is essential, and thermally stable materials or immobilized enzymes are preferred for high-temperature applications. Ghamni et al. 96 used biomass and biochar for the adsorptive removal of IC at different temperatures. The adsorptive removal efficiency of IC increased as the temperature increased from 298 K to 328 K. The kinetic energy of IC molecules increases with increasing the temperature of the medium. Therefore, IC molecules rapidly reach the surface of biochar. Therefore, adsorption increases with increasing temperature. The obtained values of ΔG° (J mol -1 K -1 ), Δ H ° (J mol -1 K -1 ), and Δ S ° (J mol -1 K -1 ) are given in Table 3 . A positive value of Δ H ° indicates that the adsorption of IC on biochar is an endothermic process. Therefore, the adsorption efficiency increases by increasing the temperature of the medium. A positive value of Δ S ° indicates that it is chemisorption because Δ S ° indicates the disorder and randomness of the surface.

Initial dye concentration determines the driving force for mass transfer. 40,68,259,260 Higher IC concentrations increase the concentration gradient, which facilitates faster adsorption or catalytic degradation initially. Yet, excessively high concentrations can saturate adsorption sites, block pores, or reduce light penetration in photocatalytic systems and ultimately decrease overall efficiency. This highlights the importance of balancing dye load with material capacity for effective treatment. For example, Awad et al. 39 degraded the IC with AgO-kaolinite (AgO-KN) composites under various concentrations of IC, as shown in Fig. 12 . They increased the IC dye concentration from 5 to 30 mg L -1 for the degradation study at pH 7 for 60 min, using 1500 mg L -1 of AgO-KN. The percentage degradation increased initially with increasing the concentration from 5 to 20 mg L -1 but decreased after further increasing the concentration of IC. Initially, the degradation increased with the concentration due to the rapid approach of IC molecules to the surface of AgO-KN, while the approach speed of IC molecules decreased due to increased collisions.

Contact time influences the extent of dye removal. Adsorption typically follows a rapid initial phase, due to the availability of abundant active sites, followed by a slower approach to equilibrium as sites become saturated. 261-263 In biological systems, sufficient contact time is necessary for microbial uptake and enzymatic degradation, as IC molecules diffuse into cells or interact with enzymes. Hybrid systems (combining adsorption and catalysis) reduce the required contact time by bringing IC molecules into proximity with reactive sites. Isik et al. 67 investigated the adsorption capacity of polyaniline@polyacrylic acid (PA@PAA) against IC, as shown in Fig. 13 . They noted that the adsorption capacity of PA@PAA depends on the contact time. The removal percentage of IC increases as the contact time increases. The PA@PAA system shows these increasing trends occurring until 30 h, after which an equilibrium state is reached. After 30 h, the rate of adsorption of IC becomes equal to the rate of desorption. Therefore, no increase is obtained in the removal percentage after this contact time.

The ionic strength and co-existing ions can significantly impact the IC removal rate. Cations and anions in wastewater can compete with IC for active binding sites, reducing the adsorption or biosorption efficiency. 27,203,264 High ionic strength can also inhibit microbial metabolism or enzyme activity and hence affect biodegradation. In photocatalytic systems, salts can alter surface charge or ROS dynamics, which highlights the need for careful evaluation of real wastewater matrices.

Light intensity and wavelength are particularly critical in photocatalytic systems. Increased light intensity enhances electron-hole pair generation, ROS formation, and consequently IC degradation. 116,151 Doped semiconductors and heterojunction materials optimize light absorption, but insufficient or excessive illumination can either reduce ROS formation or increase electron-hole recombination, which affects efficiency. For example, Ahmed et al. 68 studied the photocatalytic degradation of IC by ZnO NPs under various intensities of light. They increased the intensity of light from 6.46 W m -2 to 32.3 W m -2 , which enhanced the photocatalytic removal percentage of IC from 30% to 70% in 180 min, as shown in Fig. 14 .

The reason behind this is that as the intensity of light increases, the shifting of electrons from the VB to the CB of ZnO also increases, which results in an increase in the generated ROS. These ROS rapidly degrade the IC, which results in enhanced performance of the photocatalyst.

Material properties play a fundamental role in IC removal. Surface area, 31 porosity, 169 functional groups, 94 hydrophilicity, 265 and hybridization 133 determine the adsorption capacity and catalytic performance. For example, chitosan functionalized with graphene oxide 187 provides a high surface area, abundant binding sites, and π-π interactions, while TiO 2 /g-C 3 N 4 heterojunctions 112 combine adsorption with enhanced photocatalysis. Crosslinking, doping, or structural modifications can further improve mechanical stability, reusability, and active-site accessibility, which are crucial for practical wastewater treatment.

These factors interact synergistically. For instance, optimal pH enhances electrostatic adsorption, which in turn improves IC availability for photocatalytic degradation or enzymatic action. Similarly, hybrid systems integrate adsorption, diffusion, and catalytic mechanisms, which make them less sensitive to individual parameter fluctuations. Conversely, neglecting any factor (ionic competition, extreme pH, or high dye concentration) can significantly compromise the removal efficiency, which highlights the need for comprehensive process optimization.

To achieve a high IC removal efficiency, an integrated understanding of pH, temperature, dye concentration, contact time, ionic strength, light conditions, and material properties is required. Proper optimization enables faster kinetics, higher adsorption or degradation capacity, and sustainable operation, and it informs the design of hybrid systems combining adsorption, photocatalysis, and biodegradation, representing the most promising approach for industrial wastewater treatment.

STABILITY, REUSABILITY, AND ELECTRON-TRANSFER EFFICIENCY
Catalytic systems can be used again after recycling to control the cost of materials. The reusability of any catalytic system depends on its stability. The systems that can be recycled after IC elimination and that maintain their performance after recycling are more suitable than others.

In the recycling process, the material is first used to remove/degrade IC from a medium, after which it is recovered by centrifugation or using a magnetic field. Thereafter, the system is purified and used again to remove/degrade IC. If the performance of the system does not decrease during recycling, then the system is stable, and if its performance decreases rapidly during recycling, then the system is not stable for long periods. The equation used to find the efficiency of the system is given in eqn (13) . 13 In eqn (13) , n indicates the number of cycles, and k app stands for the apparent rate constant value during the catalytic degradation of IC.

The catalytic performance of materials used for IC degradation is strongly governed by the balance between chemical stability, accessible surface area, electron-hole separation, interfacial charge transfer, and reusability. Metal oxides and mixed-metal oxides show high chemical and photochemical stability, well-established semiconductor properties, and relatively robust crystalline structures, which make them attractive for repeated photocatalytic operations. Their major limitation is that many pristine metal oxides possess relatively wide band gaps, which result in inefficient visible light utilization, while rapid electron-hole recombination can decrease the fraction of photogenerated carriers available for IC oxidation. de Graaf and coworkers 266 worked on Co 3 O 4 -TiO 2 to illustrate this trade-off. The incorporation of only 1.4 wt% of Co 3 O 4 enabled visible light-driven IC decolorization while enabling sufficient surface area for ROS generation, but the study also demonstrated that the disappearance of the IC UV-Vis band does not necessarily correspond to complete molecular degradation, which emphasizes the need for product analysis rather than relying solely on decolorization. Similarly, Alqarni et al. 267 used the Cr-TiO 2 -g-C 3 N 4 heterojunction to reduce the optical band gap from 3.12 to 2.87 eV and improve charge separation, affording a 96.3% IC degradation rate within 60 min. The enhanced performance was attributed to the highly efficient charge transport and Z-scheme charge-transfer pathway. Therefore, metal oxides provide excellent structural stability, but heterojunction formation, doping, defect engineering or coupling with conductive supports is required to overcome the charge-recombination and visible light limitations.

MOFs provide a different advantage because their highly tunable porous structures combine large surface areas with chemically adjustable metal nodes and organic linkers. Their interconnected pores can concentrate IC near catalytic sites, while metal clusters can participate in electron transfer and ROS generation. Additionally, linker functionalization can modify adsorption and interfacial charge-transfer properties. Özen et al. 268 used the Ag/Ni-MOF system and achieved a 98.6% IC degradation rate within 45 min, with its performance associated with an increased surface area, a reduced band gap, improved visible-light absorption, and enhanced electron transfer. However, MOFs should not be regarded as more stable than metal oxides. Their metal-ligand coordination bonds can be vulnerable to hydrolysis, extreme pH, competing ions and prolonged irradiation, and structural collapse or metal/ligand leaching can compromise their recyclability. Interestingly, Mbega et al. 269 reported an Al-fumarate MOF study on IC adsorption and their feasible reuse for more than five cycles, which demonstrated that appropriately selected MOFs can possess useful operational stability. However, the adsorption stability should not be equated to photocatalytic stability because catalytic operations introduce light while inducing ROS generation and the oxidative degradation of the framework. Therefore, MOFs are attractive when a high surface area, selective adsorption and catalytic functionality need to be integrated, but their long-term hydrolytic and oxidative stability must be demonstrated under realistic wastewater conditions.

Carbon-based catalysts and carbon-supported photocatalysts show exceptional advantages in electron transport, surface adsorption and interfacial charge separation. Graphene, RGO, graphene quantum dots, carbon quantum dots, activated carbon and related conductive carbon materials can act as electron acceptors or transport channels and hence reduce electron-hole recombination and increase the lifetime of photogenerated charge carriers. This role is evident in ternary systems like SnS 2 -g-C 3 N 4 -RGO and CuS-g-C 3 N 4 -RGO, where RGO facilitates charge separation and electron transfer, while the semiconductor component supplies the photogenerated redox potential required for ROS formation. 270 Jamil et al. 271 worked on graphene nanodots and demonstrated that carbon nanostructures can themselves participate in photocatalytic IC degradation, although their performance depends strongly on the surface functional groups, electronic structure and defect density. The major limitation of carbon-based materials is that high conductivity alone does not guarantee strong photocatalytic oxidation ability. Pristine graphitic carbon lacks the appropriate semiconductor band structure and sufficiently strong redox potentials to generate all relevant ROS efficiently. Therefore, carbon is more effective as a photosensitizer, adsorption-photocatalysis interface, electron mediator, or support for a semiconductor/metal active phase rather than as an isolated photocatalyst. Excessive carbon loading can also shield the photocatalyst from light or generate recombination/transport pathways that reduce activity.

Therefore, a suitable strategy is the development of hybrid catalysts that combine the strengths of these three material classes. For example, carbon can provide adsorption and rapid electron transport, a semiconductor can provide strong redox potentials, and a MOF or porous scaffold can provide molecular confinement and high densities of accessible active sites. Chuan et al. 272 synthesized an Ag-Bi 2 O 3 -biomass-carbon catalyst and illustrated the synergistic concept. Ag-Bi 2 O 3 supported on biomass-derived carbon achieved 98.6% IC degradation within 18 min and maintained its activity over five cycles. The enhancement was associated with an increased surface area, a narrower band gap and reduced electron-hole recombination. Likewise, CQD/g-C 3 N 4 achieved 96% IC degradation within 60 min and retained good performance over three successive cycles, with the authors associating the improvement with enhanced electron-hole separation, increased surface area and suitable redox potential. 273 These results demonstrate that hybridization is more scientifically meaningful than simply increasing the catalyst surface area, because the best materials simultaneously improve IC adsorption, interfacial electron transfer and ROS generation.

CHALLENGES AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS
The removal of IC through adsorption, photocatalytic/catalytic oxidation, electrochemical oxidation, and biological processes, as well as their transition from laboratory-scale experiments to practical wastewater treatment, remains challenging. A major limitation is that many studies evaluate IC in synthetic aqueous solutions containing a single dye, whereas actual textile effluents contain salts, dissolved organic matter, auxiliary chemicals, surfactants, suspended solids, and other dyes that can compete for active sites, alter surface charge, scavenge reactive species, and interfere with electron transfer. Therefore, the removal efficiencies reported under controlled conditions can misrepresent the performance in real wastewater. Therefore, future research should prioritize experiments involving authentic textile wastewater, fluctuating pH and ionic strengths, realistic IC concentrations, continuous-flow reactors, and long-term operation rather than relying primarily on short batch experiments.

Important challenges to overcome are catalyst deactivation and structural instability. Photocatalysts can lose their activity because of the adsorption of IC and its oxidation intermediates, photocorrosion, nanoparticle aggregation, pore blockage, surface reconstruction, or leaching of active metal species. These processes can decrease the number of accessible active sites and interfere with charge separation. Although many studies report excellent degradation after several cycles, a small number of reuse cycles is insufficient to establish long-term stability. Future investigations should evaluate catalysts over longer operating periods and characterize the fresh and spent materials using XRD, FTIR spectroscopy, XPS, BET, electron microscopy, photoluminescence and electrochemical techniques. Particular attention should be given to metal leaching because a catalyst that removes IC efficiently but releases potentially toxic metals simply transfers one environmental problem to another.

Adsorbent regeneration represents another major challenge. Adsorption can rapidly remove IC, but the pollutant is transferred from the aqueous phase to the solid adsorbent. Strong interactions between IC and adsorbent functional groups (electrostatic attraction, π-π interactions, hydrogen bonding, and pore filling) can make regeneration difficult. Acidic, salt-assisted, alkaline, solvent or thermal regeneration can restore the adsorption capacity, but they consume chemicals or energy and generate concentrated IC-containing regeneration wastewater. Therefore, future adsorbent development should focus on regenerable and mechanically stable materials with high capacity but controllable adsorption strength. Particularly suitable are self-regenerating adsorption-photocatalysis materials; in processes using these materials, IC is initially captured and subsequently degraded on the same surface, ensuring the continuous restoration of adsorption sites.

Electrode fouling and passivation remain important limitations for electrochemical IC treatment. IC, aromatic intermediates, suspended solids and natural organic matter can accumulate on electrode surfaces and form insulating films, which reduce the electrochemically active area and increase charge-transfer resistance. Inorganic precipitation and electrode corrosion can further modify the electrode surface. Increasing the applied current density can accelerate IC oxidation initially, but excessive current can favor oxygen evolution, increase electricity consumption and reduce current efficiency. Chloride-containing wastewater presents an additional concern because electro-generated active chlorine can enhance IC oxidation while simultaneously increasing the possibility of chlorinated transformation products and chlorate/perchlorate formation. Therefore, future research should optimize electrode composition, applied potential, current density, electrolyte composition and hydrodynamics simultaneously while monitoring both the IC degradation and mineralization/by-product formation.

Another major challenge is the distinction between decolorization and complete detoxification. The disappearance of the characteristic visible absorption band of IC demonstrates the destruction of its chromophore but does not necessarily indicate complete degradation. The cleavage of the central C C bond can rapidly eliminate color while leaving aromatic and oxygenated intermediates in solution. Therefore, future studies should combine UV-Vis analysis with GC-MS, HPLC/UPLC-MS, TOC/DOC analysis and toxicity assays. The identification of transformation products is important for photocatalytic, Fenton-like and electrochemical processes because highly reactive oxidation can produce intermediate compounds that are more toxic than the parent dye. Therefore, future research priority should be the development of complete degradation pathways linking specific chemical-bond cleavage to intermediate formation, mineralization and toxicity reduction.

The energy requirement of advanced oxidation processes also needs greater attention. Although photocatalysis, sonophotocatalysis and electrochemical oxidation can provide rapid IC removal, they become energetically expensive when irradiation, ultrasound or high current densities are required. Therefore, reporting only the removal percentage provides an incomplete assessment of process sustainability. Future studies should report electrical energy per order, quantum efficiency where applicable, mineralization efficiency, energy consumption per gram of IC removed, and catalyst productivity. Solar-driven visible light photocatalysis, efficient photoelectrodes, low-energy LEDs, and optimized reactor geometries could reduce energy demand. In sonophotocatalytic systems, the additional degradation achieved through ultrasound should be weighed against the energy required to generate acoustic cavitation.

Integrated and coupled systems represent one of the most suitable future directions because no individual process simultaneously provides rapid removal, complete mineralization, low energy consumption and high environmental compatibility. Adsorption-photocatalysis can combine rapid IC capture with subsequent degradation. Sono-photocatalysis can improve mass transfer and reactive-species generation. Photoelectrochemical systems can enhance photogenerated charge separation, while bio-electrochemical systems can combine enzymatic selectivity with electrochemical electron transfer. Sequential treatment training, e.g. , adsorption then catalytic oxidation then biological polishing, can be particularly attractive because each process performs the function for which it is most efficient. Nonetheless, coupling should not automatically be considered beneficial. Genuine synergy must be demonstrated quantitatively while accounting for additional energy, chemical consumption and reactor complexity.

The design of next-generation materials should move beyond simply increasing the surface area or achieving higher initial removal percentages. Materials should be engineered to provide multiple functions, like IC adsorption, efficient electron transfer, visible light harvesting, resistance to fouling, ROS generation, structural stability and self-regeneration. Hierarchical porous carbons, semiconductor-carbon heterostructures, MOF/COF-derived materials, S-scheme/Z-scheme photocatalysts, single-atom catalysts and enzyme-functionalized supports show potential opportunities. Nonetheless, increased structural complexity can also increase synthesis cost, environmental burden and uncertainty regarding long-term stability. Therefore, future material development should balance activity with synthesis simplicity, regeneration, durability, toxicity, resource availability and life-cycle impacts.

The chemical nature of IC should be incorporated more systematically into future material design. IC contains an extended indigoid π-conjugated system and sulfonate groups, which influence its optical properties, surface interactions, aqueous solubility, and oxidative reactivity. Future studies should establish quantitative relationships between the IC molecular structure and adsorption/catalytic behavior, like the effects of pH-dependent charge, aromatic π-π interactions, sulfonate interactions, and susceptibility of the central C C linkage to oxidative attack. Molecular simulation, spectroscopy, density functional theory (DFT), and in situ characterization could help identify the preferred adsorption configurations and reaction sites rather than assigning removal mechanisms solely based on indirect evidence.

Scale-up, techno-economic analysis and life-cycle assessment (LCA) remain insufficiently addressed. A material that is highly effective at the milligram scale cannot be economically or environmentally viable when synthesized in kilogram or tonne quantities. Future investigations should consider precursor availability, regeneration frequency, solvent and energy requirements, synthesis yield, catalyst recovery, sludge production, metal leaching, material lifetime, and final disposal. Pilot-scale continuous-flow studies should be performed before claiming industrial applicability. Techno-economic and LCA assessments should compare the whole treatment process, like catalyst/adsorbent synthesis and regeneration, rather than considering only the treatment reactor.

Future research should shift from the conventional objective of achieving “maximum IC removal” toward achieving rapid, mineralizing, selective, regenerable, low-energy, non-toxic, and scalable treatment. The most suitable strategy is likely to involve multifunctional materials and integrated treatment trains in which adsorption, electrochemical, and catalytic oxidation processes, as well as biological polishing, are strategically combined. Such systems should be validated using real textile wastewater and evaluated through a common framework incorporating IC removal, toxicity reduction, transformation-product identification, TOC mineralization, material stability, energy consumption, regeneration efficiency, and life-cycle impacts. This integrated approach would provide a more realistic pathway for translating laboratory advances regarding IC removal into sustainable industrial wastewater treatment strategies.

CONCLUSION
Indigo carmine is a persistent and chemically recalcitrant sulfonated indigoid dye whose extended π-conjugated structure, aromatic rings, central C C linkage, and sulfonate groups govern its aqueous stability, surface interactions, optical properties, and susceptibility to chemical and biological transformation. Its high water solubility and structural stability make conventional treatment insufficient, while the formation of persistent or toxic transformation products emphasizes that decolorization alone cannot be considered equivalent to complete detoxification. Therefore, effective IC remediation requires simultaneous consideration of molecular transformation, material stability, toxicity reduction, mineralization, and process sustainability.

Adsorption remains attractive for rapid and simple IC separation among the available technologies, particularly when porous carbons, MOFs, polymeric materials, biochar, and other functionalized adsorbents provide abundant active sites. Nevertheless, adsorption primarily transfers IC from water to a solid phase and consequently raises concerns associated with adsorbent saturation, regeneration, and secondary waste. The adsorption process is strongly controlled by pH, surface functionality, porosity, ionic strength, surface charge, and IC speciation. Therefore, a high adsorption capacity obtained under optimized laboratory conditions should not automatically be interpreted as superior practical performance.

Photocatalytic and catalytic oxidation show a more fundamental advantage because they can chemically transform the IC molecule rather than simply separate it from water. Photogenerated holes and ROS, like ˙OH and ˙O 2 - , can attack the highly conjugated indigoid structure, including the central C C chromophore and aromatic framework, ultimately producing smaller oxygenated intermediates under sufficiently strong conditions, which are transformed into inorganic products like CO 2 and H 2 O. However, the disappearance of the IC absorption band does not necessarily demonstrate mineralization. Catalyst deactivation, metal leaching, light penetration, electron-hole recombination, incomplete oxidation and transformation-product toxicity remain important limitations.

Electrochemical oxidation provides an efficient and controllable alternative, with IC degradation governed by direct anodic electron transfer and/or indirect oxidation through electro-generated reactive species. Its performance can be manipulated by varying the electrode composition, applied potential, current density, electrolyte concentration and reactor configuration. However, excessive electrical input can decrease energy efficiency, while electrode fouling, passivation and corrosion can progressively reduce performance. Chloride-containing electrolytes require particular caution because although active chlorine can accelerate IC degradation, it can also generate chlorinated organic intermediates and oxychlorine species. Thus, future electrochemical studies should evaluate mineralization, current efficiency, energy consumption and by-product toxicity rather than reporting IC discoloration alone.

Biological and enzymatic processes provide an environmentally attractive pathway, particularly through enzymes like laccases and peroxidases and through microorganisms capable of producing oxidative enzymes. These systems can operate under comparatively mild conditions and can reduce toxicity through selective biochemical transformation. However, their slower kinetics and sensitivity to pH, salinity, temperature, nutrient availability and toxic co-contaminants limit their direct application to highly concentrated and chemically complex textile wastewater. Greater understanding of enzyme-mediated electron transfer, active-site interactions and intermediate metabolism is required to improve biological IC detoxification.

A major conclusion emerging from this review is that no single treatment technology simultaneously provides maximum removal, minimal secondary pollution, low energy consumption, complete mineralization, high stability and straightforward scale-up. Therefore, integrated systems represent a suitable future direction. Adsorption-photocatalysis can combine rapid IC capture with subsequent destruction of adsorbed molecules. Sono-photocatalysis can enhance mass transfer and ROS generation. Photoelectrochemical systems can improve charge separation, and bio-electrochemical systems can integrate enzymatic oxidation with electrode-mediated electron transfer. Sequential systems involving adsorption, catalytic/electrochemical oxidation and biological polishing can be advantageous because they distribute treatment functions among complementary processes.

The future development of IC-removal technologies should move beyond maximizing percentage removal toward achieving complete detoxification, material regeneration, mineralization, low energy consumption and long-term operational stability. Therefore, future research should employ real textile wastewater, continuous-flow reactors and extended cycling experiments while simultaneously monitoring the IC concentration, toxicity, transformation products, TOC, catalyst/adsorbent stability, regeneration efficiency, electrode fouling, energy consumption and potential secondary pollution. Incorporating molecular-level understanding, computational modelling, advanced characterization, multifunctional materials and life-cycle/techno-economic assessment will be essential for translating laboratory-scale advances into practical treatment technologies. The most sustainable pathway is likely not a single “best” technology but an intelligently integrated treatment strategy that exploits complementary chemical, physical, electrochemical and biological mechanisms for IC removal.
