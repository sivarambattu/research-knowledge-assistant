ABSTRACT
Abstract Grubbs catalysts, a class of Ru-based complexes, have revolutionized modern organic synthesis by enabling efficient and selective carbon-carbon bond-forming reactions, particularly olefin metathesis. Since their introduction, these catalysts have evolved significantly across multiple generations, each improving stability, activity, and functional-group tolerance. This review encompasses a broad class of ruthenium-based Grubbs-type catalysts and their derivatives. Further, an extensive survey of the wide range of organic transformations mediated by these catalysts, olefin metathesis (OM) and its key variants, including cross metathesis (CM), ring-closing metathesis (RCM), macrocyclization, ring-rearrangement metathesis (RRM), and ring-opening metathesis polymerization (ROMP), along with other transformations, has been conducted. These reactions are compiled into a unified framework that underscores the versatility, efficiency, and expanding applications of Grubbs catalysis in contemporary synthetic chemistry. This review offers a valuable and timely resource for researchers, providing both foundational knowledge and practical perspectives that will support future developments and applications in the field of catalysis.

INTRODUCTION
Grubbs catalyst (GC) has become a cornerstone in organic synthesis, particularly in the area of olefin metathesis, a reaction that allows for the exchange of carbon-carbon double bonds. 1 Developed by Robert H. Grubbs, this catalyst transformed the way that chemists approach the formation of complex molecular architectures. 2 Its high efficiency, selectivity, and tolerance to a number of functional groups have made it indispensable in both academic and industrial settings. 3 The GC emerged from the development of olefin metathesis, a reaction involving the exchange of alkylidene fragments between alkenes, first observed in the 1950s by Ronald N. Sheppard and Herbert S. Eleuterio at DuPont. 4 A key breakthrough came in the 1960s when Yves Chauvin proposed a mechanism involving metal alkylidene intermediates, leading to better catalyst design. 5 In the 1970s, Robert H. Grubbs developed stable ruthenium-based catalysts. 6 In 1992, he introduced the first-generation Grubbs catalyst (G-I), a robust and functional-group tolerant system. Grubbs, Schrock, and Chauvin shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2005 for their contributions to metathesis. 3 In the early 1990s, Robert H. Grubbs and his team at the California Institute of Technology developed the first ruthenium-based metathesis catalysts. 7 These catalysts are air-stable, highly efficient, and adaptable to olefins, opening up new possibilities for complex organic synthesis. 8,9 The G-I, reported in 1992, featured a Ru metal coordinated with 2 phosphine ligands, 2 chloride ligands, and an alkylidene group. 10 This structure provided a balance between stability and reactivity, making the catalyst easy to handle and broadly applicable. 6 However, further improvements led to the second-generation Grubbs catalyst (G-II), introduced in 1999, which replaced one of the phosphine ligands with a more robust N-heterocyclic carbene (NHC) ligand. 6,11 This modification greatly enhanced the catalyst's activity and stability, allowing for faster reactions and greater efficiency at lower catalyst loadings. 6,12 Structurally, the G-I consists of a Ru centre bound to two tricyclohexylphosphine (PCy 3 ) ligands, a benzylidene (CHPh) alkylidene group, and two chloride ligands. 13 The introduction of the NHC ligand in the G-II increased electron density at the Ru centre, improving the catalyst's ability to mediate a wider range of olefin metathesis reactions. 12 The unique structure made these catalysts highly versatile, capable of facilitating reactions that were previously challenging or impractical. 14,15

The GC has become a pivotal tool in organic synthesis, with wide-ranging applications in key reactions such as ring-closing metathesis (RCM), cross metathesis (CM), and ring-opening metathesis polymerization (ROMP). 16 In RCM, the GC forms cyclic alkenes which are essential intermediate in pharmaceutical synthesis, the CM mechanism is for the exchange of alkylidene groups between olefins, while ROMP allows the polymerization of cyclic olefins to produce advanced materials for medical devices, coatings, etc. 17-19 Its extensive utility across organic and polymer chemistry helps in major innovation. 19,20 Besides metathesis, the GC has demonstrated versatility in other reactions as well like cyclopropanation, transfer hydrogenation, ATRP (atom transfer radical polymerization) and carbonyl olefination, spreading its impact into hydrogen transfer, polymer synthesis, and functional group transformations. 21-24

In this review, a complete overview of GCs and their evolution, beginning with a discussion of their fundamental structural features across different generations, has been discussed. Further, the broad spectrum of organic transformations mediated by these catalysts, including OM, ROMP, RRM, CM, macrocyclization, hydrogenation, dehydrogenation, RCM, and other related processes, has been surveyed and compiled into a unified framework that highlights the versatility, efficiency, and expanding applications of GC in modern synthetic chemistry. To the best of our knowledge, there has been no prior review that combines a complete introduction to all generations of GC with such an extensive survey of their mechanistic features and diverse reaction scope, making this work a distinctive and timely contribution to the field.

TYPES OF GRUBBS CATALYSTS
GCs are Ru-based complexes and play a crucial role in OM reactions because of the unique properties, for instance tolerance to many functional groups and ease of use. 35 Since the G-I introduction, the family of these catalysts has advanced significantly, with improvements in stability, activity, and selectivity as depicted in Fig. 2 . 2,6,27,31,35,36 Each generation has addressed challenges in improving performance across a wide range of metathesis reactions. 37 Further, a detailed exploration of the types of GCs has been discussed that cover overall structural properties and unique characteristics.

• NHC ligand (H 2 IMes): The NHC ligand (1,3-bis(2,4,6-trimethylphenyl)imidazole-2-ylidene) is a strong σ-donor and a weak π-acceptor, making it more electron-donating than phosphine ligands. 12,45 This electron donation increases the reactivity of Ru.

By swapping one phosphine ligand with an NHC ligand, the G-II shows a significant increase in activity and stability, including better tolerance for air and moisture. 48 It is also more effective in catalyzing sterically hindered olefins. This improved activity, combined with the ability to work under milder conditions, makes G-II catalysts versatile and broadly applicable. 10,12

• Isopropoxy-styrene ligand: The key feature of the HG catalyst is the tethered isopropoxy group, which stabilizes the active catalyst species by forming a chelated complex with Ru center. 50 This design allows for a slow, controlled release of the carbene ligand, providing better control over the initiation of the metathesis reaction. 51

The slow initiation of the catalyst due to the isopropoxy-styrene ligand allows for more controlled and selective reactions, making HG catalysts highly desirable for processes where a slower initiation rate is beneficial. 53 The tethering also improves the overall robustness and recyclability of the catalyst, making it a valuable tool for industrial processes where catalyst longevity is critical. 54

Fast-initiating Grubbs catalysts are modified ruthenium carbene complexes developed to achieve rapid catalyst activation in olefin metathesis reactions. These catalysts typically contain a ruthenium centre coordinated with an N-heterocyclic carbene (NHC) ligand and modified benzylidene ligands, while some systems also contain labile ligands such as pyridine or phosphine derivatives that dissociate easily during initiation. Z -Selective GCs represent a more specialized class of catalysts designed to promote the selective formation of Z -olefins ( cis -olefins) over the more common E -olefins ( trans -olefins). 56,57 These catalysts contain specially designed ligands that favor the Z -geometry during metathesis. 58 For example, some Z -selective GCs feature bidentate ligands such as pyridyl-alcohol or pyridyl-amine, which coordinate to the Ru centre in a way that stabilizes Z -olefin formation: [Ru(NHC)(X-chelate)( Created by potrace 1.16, written by Peter Selinger 2001-2019 CHR)]

These catalysts are particularly valuable in organic synthesis where the geometric configuration of the product is critical, such as in the synthesis of natural products or pharmaceutical intermediates. 17,59 Their ability to selectively produce Z -olefins gives chemists precise control over the stereochemistry of metathesis products. 60

G-III are designed for even greater activity and stability than their second-generation counterparts. 61 These catalysts are modified with more advanced NHC ligands or electron-donating groups, allowing for higher turnover rates and better functional group compatibility. 12 The structure remains similar to G-II, but with enhanced ligand design: [RuCl 2 (NHC)(Py) 2 ( Created by potrace 1.16, written by Peter Selinger 2001-2019 CHR)]

• Advanced NHC ligands: The NHC ligands in G-III are often more sterically bulky or electronically tuned to increase the reactivity of the Ru center. 62 These ligands enhance both the initiation and propagation steps of the metathesis cycle.

G-III are optimized for complex metathesis reactions, such as those involving sterically hindered or highly functionalized substrates. 63,64 Their faster initiation and broader substrate scope make them ideal for advanced organic synthesis. 61

The Schrodi-Grubbs B (SG-B) catalyst is a tailored G-II derivative, developed to enhance initiation rates and broaden functional group tolerance. 65 Its design allows more efficient olefin metathesis under relatively mild conditions, making it a versatile tool for complex transformations. 65 The Stewart-Grubbs (S-G) catalyst is another G-II derivative, optimized by reducing steric hindrance around the Ru center. 33 This modification improves its performance in RCM, especially for sterically demanding and hindered olefins where traditional GCs show limited activity. 66

The evolution of Grubbs catalysts reflects systematic ligand modifications aimed at improving catalytic performance. 7 Compared with the first-generation catalyst, the incorporation of NHC ligands in the second-generation catalyst enhances catalyst initiation and stability. 67 Hoveyda-Grubbs catalysts exhibit improved stability because of the chelating benzylidene ether ligand, whereas third-generation catalysts initiate more rapidly owing to the presence of labile pyridine ligands. 68 Stewart-Grubbs catalysts further improve the metathesis of sterically hindered olefins through optimized ligand design. 69 These structural modifications collectively demonstrate how ligand engineering governs catalyst activity, stability, selectivity, and substrate scope. 6,70

The progressive evolution of Grubbs catalysts demonstrates that subtle ligand modifications have been the primary driving force behind improvements in activity, stability, and substrate scope. Future catalyst development is expected to focus on balancing rapid initiation with enhanced catalyst longevity and stereoselectivity.

GENERAL INTRODUCTION OF OLEFIN METATHESIS
Olefin metathesis is an important catalytic reaction in organic chemistry that involves the breaking and rearrangement of carbon-carbon double bonds to form new olefins. It is widely used for the synthesis of complex organic molecules, polymers, pharmaceuticals, and fine chemicals due to its high efficiency and functional group tolerance.

Olefin metathesis reactions are mainly classified into five types: cross metathesis (CM), ring-closing metathesis (RCM), acyclic diene metathesis polymerization (ADMET), ring-opening metathesis polymerization (ROMP), and ring-opening cross metathesis (ROCM). 5

According to the mechanism proposed by Yves Chauvin and Jean-Louis Hérisson in 1971, olefin metathesis proceeds through metal-carbene and metallacyclobutane intermediates. In the first step, the active catalyst is formed when the ruthenium catalyst loses a phosphine ligand to generate a 14-electron metal-carbene active species. This is followed by the coordination of the alkene substrate to the metal centre of the catalyst. Subsequently, a [2 + 2] cycloaddition takes place between the carbon-carbon double bond of the alkene and the metal-carbene bond, resulting in the formation of a four-membered metallacyclobutane intermediate. The metallacyclobutane intermediate then undergoes cyclo-reversion in the opposite direction to produce a new olefin along with a new metal-carbene species. The newly formed metal carbene again reacts with another alkene molecule, thereby propagating the catalytic cycle and continuously forming metathesis products. Later, Robert H. Grubbs developed well-defined ruthenium carbene catalysts that experimentally validated and efficiently carried out the Chauvin mechanism Fig. 3 . 5,6

Further mechanistic insight was provided by Deryn E. Fogg and co-workers, who investigated catalyst decomposition pathways during olefin metathesis. Their studies demonstrated that donor molecules such as amines and pyridine can deactivate Grubbs catalysts by interacting with the active methylidene or metallacyclobutane intermediates. Fogg proposed that donor-induced decomposition occurs through nucleophilic attack or deprotonation of the metallacyclobutane intermediate, leading to formation of unstable σ-alkyl ruthenium species and eventual catalyst decomposition. These studies highlighted that the metallacyclobutane intermediate is highly vulnerable to Brønsted bases and donor ligands during the metathesis process. 78,79

Beyond the classical Chauvin mechanism, the performance of Grubbs catalysts is influenced by several mechanistic factors, including catalyst initiation, ligand effects, solvent effects, catalyst decomposition, and stereochemical control. These factors have significantly contributed to the understanding and development of modern ruthenium-based metathesis catalysts.

Catalyst initiation involves the formation of the active 14-electron ruthenium carbene species through ligand dissociation. The introduction of N-heterocyclic carbene (NHC) ligands enhances catalyst activity by promoting phosphine dissociation while improving catalyst stability. In addition, the electronic and steric properties of ligands, together with the reaction solvent, influence catalyst activity, selectivity, and lifetime. 80,81

Catalyst decomposition remains a major challenge in olefin metathesis. Previous studies have shown that methylidene instability, bimolecular decomposition, C-H activation, and poisoning by strongly coordinating donor molecules contribute to catalyst deactivation. Understanding these pathways has facilitated the development of more stable and efficient ruthenium catalysts. 82-85

Several computational and experimental studies have also provided valuable insights into catalyst initiation, decomposition pathways, and stereochemical ( E / Z ) control, enabling the rational design of improved Grubbs catalysts. Compared with other transition-metal catalysts, ruthenium catalysts are widely preferred because of their excellent functional-group tolerance, air and moisture stability, and broad substrate compatibility. 86-90

Among the transition metals employed in olefin metathesis, ruthenium catalysts are the most widely used because of their excellent functional-group tolerance, high stability toward air and moisture, and compatibility with a broad range of reaction conditions. Compared with molybdenum- and tungsten-based catalysts, ruthenium complexes exhibit greater operational simplicity, improved substrate tolerance, and enhanced stability, making them highly suitable for both laboratory synthesis and industrial applications. These advantages have established Grubbs-type ruthenium catalysts as one of the most versatile and extensively studied catalyst systems in olefin metathesis. 7,30,91,92

APPLICATIONS OF GRUBBS CATALYST IN ORGANIC SYNTHESIS
Table 3 summarizes selected representative studies highlighting key advances across the major application areas of Grubbs catalysts.

Pump et al. 100 investigated OM of a library of 2nd generation GC by examining a variety of reactants (carbene ligands, NHC-ligands and substrates), leading to diverse products, and utilized computational analysis to elucidate reaction mechanisms and energy profiles, achieving notable yields. The mechanism involved the formation of key intermediates, including the 14-electron species and metallacycles, which dictated the reaction pathways. Applications spanned industrial transformations and pharmaceutical synthesis, with the pros including high efficiency and selectivity, while the cons involved potential catalyst decomposition and non-productive metathesis events. Overall, the research provided valuable insights for optimizing catalyst design and improving reaction outcomes in olefin metathesis ( Scheme 1 ).

Özer and coworkers 101 developed one-pot procedure for dehydrogenative alcohol coupling and cross-metathesis using the HG-II under a N 2 atmosphere with KOH as the base. The procedure utilized various saturated and unsaturated alcohols, particularly 1-octanol, to produce β-alkylated alcohols and functional esters like γ-butyrolactone in high, often quantitative yields. The mechanism involved the in situ transformation of the GC into Ru hydride species, which facilitated the reactions at elevated temperatures (110 °C) until the conversion stabilized. This method found applications in the synthesis of functional materials and offered significant advantages, including high efficiency and versatility ( Scheme 2 ).

Bell and coworkers 102 utilized a CAAC-substituted GC (C668) for bulk acyclic diene metathesis (ADMET) polymerization, demonstrating its effectiveness in producing high molecular weight aliphatic polysulfones with minimal olefin isomerization. The synthesis involved degassing the monomer, adding the catalyst at a 0.5 mol% concentration, and conducting the reaction at elevated temperatures (100 or 150 °C) for approximately 40 h, resulting in significant product yields. This methodology highlighted the catalyst's enhanced selectivity for metathesis over isomerization, allowing for the formation of desired functional derivatives, while the study acknowledged the advantages of high TON and stability at elevated temperatures ( Scheme 3 ).

Li et al. 103 investigated the synthesis of self-healing elastomers using methyl vinyl silicone rubber (MVQ) and Fe 3+ -catechol coordination, facilitated by G-II. They reported a catalyst loading of 0.23 g DOPA, with the reaction conducted under a nitrogen atmosphere for 3 h, followed by the addition of FeCl 3 ·6H 2 O to form the product Fe(DOPA) 3 -ICA-MVQ. For synthesis, MVQ was dissolved in THF, stirred with the catalyst, and pH was adjusted with triethylamine, which yielded a reddish-black final elastomer. This procedure included the elastomer's ability to self-heal and uphold functionality after damage, making it suitable for flexible sensors. This methodology highlighted the advantages of recyclability ( Scheme 4 ).

The catalytic activity of an ammonium-containing HG catalyst supported on magnetically separable core silica was investigated for carboxylic acid addition reactions to terminal alkynes by Oztürk and coauthors. 104 Ru-1 catalyst, demonstrated high E -selectivity and proved effective with various carboxylic acid derivatives, achieving conversion rates of up to 99% with just 1% Ru catalyst loading. The synthetic process involved stirring the reaction mixture at 85 °C under an air atmosphere. The study revealed that the catalyst maintained its activity up to 11 runs with minimal Ru leaching (1%), demonstrating its robustness and reusability. The methodology showed high efficiency and compatibility with non-degassed solvents. There was limited application found with the internal alkynes and also the catalyst leaching was noted as drawbacks ( Scheme 5 ).

In 2020, Samkian et al. 99 synthesized and evaluated a series of six-membered cyclic alkyl amino carbene (CAAC-6) Ru olefin metathesis catalysts. Catalyst loading of 1-5 mol% was employed with various olefin reactants, to yield products such as macrocycles and ethenolysis derivatives. The synthetic procedure involved four steps to prepare CAAC-6 salts, with yields of up to 78% under optimized conditions, within 1.3 to 4.6 h at 40-60 °C. The CAAC-6 catalysts demonstrated rapid initiation rates, but lower activity in RCM and ethenolysis compared to their CAAC-5 counterparts, certain derivatives exhibited enhanced selectivity for ethenolysis. The proposed mechanism suggested that substrate binding was influenced by the steric effects of the ligands. Despite their advantages, the catalysts encountered stability issues and decomposition under certain conditions, highlighting both their potential and limitations for applications in OM ( Scheme 6 ).

Jung and researchers 105 utilized dithiolate-chelated Ru-based GC for the synthesis of 1,6-heptadiyne derivatives via β-selective cyclopolymerization (CP). The catalyst featured bulky N -2,6-diisopropylphenol (DIPP) ligands, enabled high β-selectivity and ensured the stability of propagating species. Catalyst loadings were carefully optimized to maximize efficiency. The reaction conditions included the addition of pyridine, which enhanced initiation rates and led to product yields ranging from 67% to 95%, with reaction times varying from one minute to one hour depending on the reactants and conditions. The mechanism involved the coordination of pyridine to the catalyst, which stabilized the propagating species and facilitated the synthesis of conjugated polyenes with intricate architectures. Although the approach is simple, it required meticulous optimization of reaction parameters to avoid uncontrolled polymerization, and the scope of suitable monomers with smaller substituents remained limited ( Scheme 7 ). In addition, stereoselective ruthenium-based catalysts play a crucial role in enabling the synthesis of internal olefins with well-defined E / Z geometry, thereby expanding their applicability in the preparation of structurally precise molecules.

Kumandin et al. 106 synthesized novel HG catalysts featuring N to Ru coordinate bonds within six-membered rings, employing 2-vinylbenzylamines and 1,3-bis(2,4,6-trimethylphenyl)imidazolidine (SIMes). The G-III catalyst was utilized with catalyst loadings of 0.1 mol% in reactions involving styrene and N , N -diallyltosylamide, resulting in high conversion rates. Moderate to good yield was achieved under mild conditions, demonstrating the catalysts effectiveness in olefin CM and RCM. Key findings showed that longer N to Ru bonds improved catalytic performance, with morpholine and isoquinoline derivatives exhibiting superior activity. While the study emphasized the advantages of using fewer toxic reagents and simpler equipment, it also noted the stability of certain catalysts as a limitation. The research provided valuable insights into the structure-activity relationship of Ru complexes in metathesis reactions ( Scheme 8 ).

The synthesis of conjugated polyenynes through cascade metathesis and metallotropic polymerization using advanced GC, specifically the stereo-retentive Ru3 catalysts, was explored by Kang and coworkers. 107 A catalyst loading of 5-10 mol% with reactants was employed, including 1,6-heptadiynes and various alkyne derivatives, leading to products characterized by alternating six- and five-membered rings. The synthetic procedure involved sequential ring-closing and metallotropic shifts, achieving product yields of 65-92% within reaction times of 1-3 h under mild conditions (RT). The mechanism revealed a switch from α-to β-addition, enhancing regioselectivity. The study highlighted the advantages of high selectivity and control over molecular weight, although it noted the limitations of catalyst stability and the need for precise reaction conditions ( Scheme 9 ).

Liu and teammates 108 synthesized ester end-functionalized hydrogenated nitrile-butadiene rubber (EF-HNBR) by employing a one-step functional metathesis and hydrogenation process using G-II. The catalyst loading was optimized at 0.016 g in a 4% NBR solution, with functionalised olefin as a CTA (chain transfer agent), achieving a high hydrogenation degree of up to 95% under optimal conditions of 125 °C in 24 h. The synthetic procedure involved dissolving NBR in chlorobenzene, followed by the addition of the catalyst and subsequent hydrogenation, resulting in products with improved thermal stability and adhesion properties. The mechanism involved the formation of a new ruthenium carbene species that facilitated the metathesis hydrogenation of NBR. While the process demonstrated significant advantages such as high selectivity and lower viscosity, potential drawbacks included the risk of catalyst decomposition at elevated temperatures and the need for careful control of reaction conditions to prevent cross-linking ( Scheme 10 ).

Le and co-authors 109 investigated the synthesis of polyesters via acyclic diene metathesis (ADMET) polymerization using G-II, specifically RuCl 2 (PCy 3 )(H 2 IMes)(CHPh). They utilized bio-based reactants, including 4-allyl-2-methoxyphenyl 10-undecenoate, achieving product yields of up to 72-91% with unimodal molecular weight distributions ( M n = 12 700, M w / M n = 1.85). The synthetic procedure involved a reaction time of 30 min under N 2 atmosphere, with catalyst loading of 0.5-1.5 mol% and cross-linker addition after 30 minutes. The mechanism highlighted the efficient removal of ethylene, which positively influenced molecular weight, while the use of renewable resources presented a significant advantage. However, the study noted potential limitations in reaction scalability and the need for careful control of reaction conditions to optimize yields ( Scheme 11 ).

In 2020, Zak and co-workers 93 successfully synthesized chromophore-functionalized silsesquioxanes using a straightforward synthetic protocol. The process employed the G-I (1 mol%) for CM and Suzuki-Miyaura coupling reactions. Monovinyl-substituted silsesquioxanes (SQ1) and various unsaturated compounds served as reactants, yielding nearly quantitative amounts of mixed styrylarenes and chromophore-anchored silsesquioxanes after 24 h at 40 °C. The methodology exhibited high thermal stability and favourable photophysical properties, with the incorporation of silsesquioxane cores significantly enhancing the thermal performance of the products. However, the study identified some drawbacks, including the risk of contamination from boronic acids and incomplete substrate conversion caused by intermediate precipitation. Despite these challenges, the research showcased the simplicity and effectiveness of the synthetic approach, offering insights into optimizing reaction conditions and catalyst efficiency for improved outcomes ( Scheme 12 ).

Mandava and colleagues 110 investigated the cross-metathesis of methallyl halides using four ruthenium-based catalysts: the Grubbs catalyst (GC) and G-II exhibited poor reactivity, whereas the HG-II and Stewart-Grubbs catalyst (S-G) afforded moderate to good yields with moderate E / Z selectivity in the cross-metathesis of methallyl chloride or bromide with a benzyl ether-containing substrate. The S-G catalyst demonstrated particular effectiveness, enabling the formal total synthesis of (-)-presphaerene with fewer steps and a higher overall yield compared to conventional approaches. However, the reaction required an excess of methallyl halide and specific solvents to minimize side reactions. The study addressed the advantages of CM by introducing methallyl halides, as more efficient alternative to traditional synthetic routes ( Scheme 13 ).

In 2020, Woźniak and coworkers 111 developed an effective methodology to synthesize functionalized α,β-unsaturated sulfonamides through olefin CM using HG-type catalysts. The authors explored various reactants with simple terminal olefins and styrene derivatives, achieving good product yields, with notable yields of 22 to 95% for sulfonamide under optimized conditions involving 2.5-5 mol% catalyst loading and a reaction time of 3-28 h. The synthetic procedure demonstrated compatibility with a number of functionalities, enabling the successful formation of biologically active derivatives such as moxifloxacin and naratriptan. The research highlighted advantages such as mild reaction conditions and the potential to synthesize polyfunctional compounds, while also noting the limitations of certain functional groups that hindered reactivity ( Scheme 14 ).

Xu and researchers 112 investigated novel Z -selective cyclometalated Ru metathesis catalysts featuring bulky N-heterocyclic carbene ligands, specifically utilizing GC (Ru-6) at a loading of 2.5 mol%. The reaction involved terminal olefins and acrylamides, yielding cis -Michael acceptors with high Z -selectivity under optimized conditions in THF. The synthetic procedure demonstrated isolated yields of the Z -products, with reaction times varying based on substrate complexity. The mechanism revealed a significant “pivalate effect” that enhanced selectivity, showcasing the advantages of these catalysts in terms of reactivity and substrate compatibility. However, challenges included the need for relatively small NHC ligands for high-yielding anion exchange, which could limit the scope of bulky ligand applications ( Scheme 15 ).

The ethenolysis of maleate esters with ethylene was investigated by Engl and coworkers 113 using a series of Grubbs catalysts, particularly those with ligands like PCy 3 (tricyclohexylphosphine). The catalysts started the reaction by olefin exchange between ethylene and maleate esters involving Ru-methylidene species, which cleaved C-C bonds to form acrylate esters through metathesis. An in-depth optimization was done with catalyst loadings (up to 2000 ppm), reaction times (up to 90 min), and ethylene pressures (up to 10 bar) to achieve high acrylate conversion (up to 71%) and TON up to 5200. The method demonstrated high productivity and selectivity, that make it a feasible alternative to conventional processes. However, the challenges included potential catalyst deactivation and the need to reduce Ru loading, though the addition of a polymeric phenol resin enhanced catalyst stability and proficiency. The findings for this method established a benchmark for performance of Ru catalyst and highlighted its potential for industrial applications ( Scheme 16 ).

Roenko and colleagues 114 studied the CM between polynorbornene (PNB) and poly(5,6-epoxy-1-octenamer) (PECO) using the G-I. PECO was synthesized by epoxidation of 1,5-cyclooctadiene with m -CPBA followed by ROMP using G-II. The cross-metathesis reaction produced NB-ECO multiblock copolymers whose composition and block length were controlled by reaction conditions. Structural analyses confirmed copolymer formation, and the materials exhibited higher thermal stability and glass transition temperatures due to epoxy incorporation. The study highlighted this methodology as an efficient route to functional and thermally stable multiblock copolymers ( Scheme 17 ).

For the synthesis of macrocyclic β-peptidomimetics, an approach was developed by Tran and colleagues 94 using RCM with G-II catalyst. Moderate yields of 45% to 60% was achieved depending on solvent choice and catalyst concentration. The methodology involved the use of N -alkylated β-amino acids as reactants, at reflux for 16 h to produce 9-membered cyclic products, which were characterized by NMR and MS. The mechanism was based on the metathesis of double bonds facilitated by GC. The study highlighted challenges such as by-product formation at higher concentrations, moderate yields that required further optimization, and the limited investigation of the biological activity of the synthesized compounds ( Scheme 18 ).

The use of ruthenium N-heterocyclic carbene Hoveyda II-type complexes as catalysts for ring-closing metathesis (RCM) reactions was studied by Al-Enezi et al. 115 The synthesis of novel Ru N-heterocyclic carbene Hoveyda II-type complexes involved a controlled process with specific precursors and reagents under an argon atmosphere, followed by purification through column chromatography. These catalysts demonstrated high efficiency in catalyzing the RCM reactions of N , N -diallylanilines to produce 1 H -pyrroles, with reactions conducted at 40 °C yielding products with notable isolated yields. While the catalysts exhibited enhanced activity and selectivity, the study identified potential scalability issues and the need for further optimization to improve yields and reduce reaction times, marking a significant advancement in metal-catalyzed reactions ( Scheme 19 ).

Jones and coauthors 116 explored a novel method for the selective depolymerization of polyurethanes (PUs) using HG-II at a low catalyst loading of 2 mol%. They synthesized unsaturated polyols (DEDO5 and DEDO6), which were co-polymerized with toluene diisocyanate to produce linear and crosslinked PUs, which were then depolymerized into low molecular weight products with vinyl and cycloalkene functionalities. The reaction was conducted under mild conditions, yielding a diverse array of products in a relatively short time, facilitated by a mechanism involving RCM. While the approach demonstrated significant potential for recycling and re-polymerization of the depolymerized materials, it also faced challenges related to the economic feasibility of catalyst loadings and the need for further optimization in the synthetic procedure ( Scheme 20 ).

Although olefin metathesis remains the primary application of Grubbs catalysts, their unique ruthenium carbene chemistry has enabled several non-classical transformations beyond conventional metathesis. These reactions are mechanistically related through the involvement of reactive ruthenium carbene intermediates or closely associated catalytic pathways, highlighting the versatility of Grubbs-type catalysts and broadening their synthetic utility.

In recent years, this broader catalytic versatility has also been extended to stereoselective transformations, enabling the construction of structurally and stereo-chemically complex molecules. Chen et al. 147 reported a diastereoselective cyclopropene-allene metathesis for the synthesis of axially chiral skipped ene-allenes containing an all-carbon quaternary stereocenter. The reaction demonstrated the potential of Grubbs-type catalysts for accessing stereochemically complex molecular architectures.

Sole et al. 148 explored the use of GCs to promote intramolecular carbene C(sp 3 )-H insertion reactions from α-diazoesters, demonstrating that these catalyst, particularly the G-I, effectively facilitated the formation of pyrrolidines with high yields (up to 98%) under reflux conditions in CH 2 Cl 2 for 24 h, using a catalyst loading of 3 mol%. The research highlighted that no competition from metathesis reactions occurred when substrates contained alkene or alkyne moieties, and it was found that the presence of amine moieties was crucial for the reaction's success, as supported by DFT calculations that provided insight into the reaction mechanism. While the GCs exhibited versatility and high atom economy, the study noted that bulky ortho -substituents could affect catalyst efficiency, indicating both advantages and limitations in their application for synthetic transformations ( Scheme 54 ).

Kawauchi et al. 149 introduced a method for the aerobic dehydrogenation of N -containing heterocycles using the GC, leveraging oxygen-mediated structural changes to enable tandem catalysis that combine RCM with aerobic dehydrogenation. Using reactants such as indolines and their derivatives, the method efficiently synthesized indole derivatives and N -containing fused heteroarenes, including the antibiotic pyocyanine, with reaction times of 1 to 144 h and yields 34-99%. The approach offered several advantages, including high efficiency, low catalyst loading (0.1-10 mol%), and excellent functional group tolerance, making it versatile for complex organic synthesis. However, it also had some limitations, such as the requirement for elevated temperatures (70 °C) for optimal performance. By transforming simple heterocycles into valuable aromatic compounds, the study highlighted the GC's versatility and significant utility in organic synthesis ( Scheme 55 ; Method 1). Amines and aldehydes were used as the primary reactant, to synthesize nitrile product with 6-97% yield in 30 min to 12 h using toluene under an O 2 atmosphere. Amine reacted with the catalyst to form an imine intermediate, which was oxidized to yield the desired nitrile. The methodology has plus points including a range of functional groups, its mild reaction conditions, and environmental sustainability although it is observed that other GCs resulted in low yields with extended reaction times ( Scheme 55 ; Method 2).

Biberger and co-authors 151 introduced a light-driven gem-hydrogenation process using GCs to facilitate the hydrogenative metathesis of enynes, by converting internal alkynes into cyclic alkenes. The Ru complex had NHC ligand and a π-acceptor cymene ligand that functioned through a light-driven mechanism where UV irradiation started the de-coordination of the cymene ligand, producing a reactive 12-electron fragment to activate the alkyne substrate. This procedure permitted the geminal transfer of hydrogen atoms from H 2 to a single carbon atom of the alkyne, resulting in good to excellent yields of the desired products under mild conditions (approx. 50 °C). This approach offered significant advantages, such as the ability to modify the ligand sphere for enhanced reactivity however, challenges included a limited substrate scope ( Scheme 56 ).

Öztürk and colleague 152 investigated the transfer hydrogenation of ketones and aldehydes using a Ru indenylidene complex (M1) as the catalyst, with a specific focus on its performance in aqueous media. The GC was utilized at a loading of 1 mol%, facilitating the conversion of acetophenone to 1-phenylethanol with yields of 70-99% within 12 h. The synthetic approach involved a one-pot CM/transfer hydrogenation reaction using 10-undecenal and methyl acrylate, which produced the product with 99% conversion yield. The study highlighted the catalyst's high tolerance for functional groups and its overall efficiency, though it acknowledged potential limitations in reaction rates due to diffusion constraints. These findings emphasized the benefits of employing GCs under environmentally friendly conditions, while also identifying the need for further research into their mechanisms and broader applications ( Scheme 57 ).

Overall, the evolution of ruthenium-based Grubbs catalysts reflects a continuous effort to balance catalytic activity, initiation rate, stability, and functional-group tolerance. 153,154 First-generation catalysts exhibit excellent stability and functional-group compatibility but generally display slower initiation and lower activity than later generations. 12 The introduction of N-heterocyclic carbene ligands in second-generation catalysts significantly enhanced catalytic activity and broadened substrate scope, while third-generation catalysts further accelerated initiation and improved performance in demanding metathesis reactions. 155-157 Hoveyda-Grubbs catalysts provide additional advantages in catalyst stability, ease of handling, and controlled initiation, making them particularly useful for many practical applications. 70 Nevertheless, no single catalyst is universally optimal, and catalyst selection ultimately depends on the substrate, reaction conditions, desired selectivity, and operational considerations.

In our opinion, future developments in Grubbs-type catalysts should focus on achieving an optimal balance between catalytic efficiency, sustainability, and operational simplicity, rather than solely improving catalyst activity. Such advances are expected to further broaden the practical applications of olefin metathesis in both academia and industry.

CONCLUSION
In conclusion, we have provided a comprehensive and systematic account of Grubbs-type catalysts, tracing their development from the first generation to the most advanced variants. We discussed the fundamental mechanisms and structural features that govern their reactivity, stability, and selectivity, followed by an extensive survey of their applications across a wide range of organic transformations, including OM, ROMP, RRM, CM, macrocyclization, hydrogenation, dehydrogenation, RCM, etc. By organizing these diverse reactions into a unified framework, we aimed to highlight the versatility, efficiency, and growing importance of GCs in contemporary synthetic chemistry. Despite being metal-based catalysts, particularly employing Ru, their design and operational characteristics align with several principles of green chemistry. Their ability to catalyze reactions under milder conditions, with high selectivity and reduced waste, allows for more sustainable processes. We also emphasized how their recyclability, lower reagent requirements, and compatibility with greener solvents contribute to minimizing environmental impact.

Looking toward the future, ongoing research is expected to focus on addressing current limitations such as cost, metal recovery, and long-term catalyst stability, while expanding substrate scope and functional group tolerance. Advances in computational methods, catalyst immobilization, and process optimization promise to further enhance their environmental and economic viability. Integrating Grubbs catalysis into greener, more energy-efficient, and circular chemical processes offers exciting opportunities for sustainable innovation.
